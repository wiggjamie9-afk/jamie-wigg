**Status:** READY FOR LAUNCH (Day 1)

**Frequency:** Weekly (every Tuesday) + occasional bonus deep dives

**Target Audience:** Aspiring and current entrepreneurs, ages 22–50 (founders, side-hustlers, indie hackers, small-business owners)
**Revenue Target:** $25k/mo by Month 6

---

## Podcast Positioning

**Founders' Stories** is the entrepreneurship podcast that tells the *real* journey of how businesses actually got built — the near-deaths, the wrong turns, the unglamorous grind, and the moment things finally clicked. Twelve focused minutes, one company per episode, framed as a story you can't stop listening to. Then we pull out the concrete lesson you can use Monday morning.

This is the anti-hustle-porn show. No "rise and grind at 4am" theatre. No overnight-success myths. Just honest accounts of how ordinary people built extraordinary companies, including all the parts the highlight reels leave out.

**Why it works:**
- Entrepreneurship is **evergreen and aspirational** — millions are starting or dreaming of starting something.
- Story format is **bingeable** — people remember narratives, not bullet points.
- The audience is **high-value to advertisers** — founders buy SaaS, banking, hosting, courses, and tools.
- The **anti-hustle-porn positioning is differentiated** — the market is saturated with motivational noise; honesty stands out.
- Solo TTS narration keeps **production cost near zero** and cadence reliable.

**A note on sourcing (read this):**
The five pilot episodes below are **narrative case studies built entirely from publicly reported accounts** — founder interviews, press coverage, books, and company statements that are already in the public record. They are framed as storytelling, not investigative journalism or insider reporting. Where accounts differ or details are popularly retold, we present the widely cited version and avoid inventing private dialogue or unverifiable specifics. Every episode opens with a short disclosure that the story is reconstructed from public accounts. Once real founder interviews are possible, the show transitions to first-person episodes; the case-study format remains the fallback and the on-ramp.

### **EPISODE 1: "Three Air Mattresses and a Cereal Box — How Airbnb Survived Near-Death"**
**Air Date:** Today (Day 1)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: Warm, hopeful 4-second sting, then fade under]

HOST: You're listening to Founders' Stories — the show about how businesses actually get built. Not the highlight reel. The whole thing. I'm your host, and before we start, one quick note: this episode is a story reconstructed from public accounts — interviews, press, and the founders' own retellings. It's storytelling, not insider reporting. Okay. Let's go.

[BEAT]

In late 2007, two broke design-school grads in San Francisco couldn't make rent. Their names were Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia. The apartment was too expensive, the bank account was nearly empty, and a big design conference was coming to town. Every hotel in the city was booked out.

So they had an idea that sounds almost too small to matter. What if they bought a few air mattresses, threw them on the living room floor, and rented out space to conference-goers who couldn't find a room? They built a quick website. They called it "Air Bed and Breakfast." They charged eighty dollars a night, made a bit of breakfast, and three strangers showed up to sleep on their floor.

It worked. Barely. But it worked. And here's the first thing worth noticing: the original idea was tiny, specific, and born out of a personal problem. They weren't trying to disrupt the global hospitality industry. They were trying to make rent.

[BEAT — soft transition tone]

Now here's the part most people forget. After that first weekend, almost nothing happened. They brought in a third co-founder, an engineer named Nathan Blecharczyk, rebuilt the site, and launched it again. And again. They relaunched the company multiple times. Each time they expected lift-off. Each time it landed with a thud.

They were carrying real debt. To stay alive, they did something that has since become almost legendary. During the 2008 US presidential election, they designed novelty breakfast cereal boxes — "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's" — and sold them as collector's items. It sounds ridiculous because it was. But it reportedly brought in around thirty thousand dollars, and that money kept the lights on while the actual product was going nowhere.

Think about that. The thing that funded the future of Airbnb was not Airbnb. It was cereal. Founders love to talk about focus, and focus matters — but survival sometimes looks like doing something undignified to buy yourself another month.

[BEAT]

Eventually they got into a startup accelerator called Y Combinator. And the advice they got there became the turning point of the whole story. Their mentor, Paul Graham, reportedly told them something simple and strange: it's better to have a hundred people who love you than a million people who sort of like you. And then he asked them where their users actually were. The answer was New York.

So they did something that does not scale and is not glamorous. They got on a plane. They went door to door, meeting hosts in person. And they noticed the listings had terrible photos — dark, blurry phone pictures that made nice apartments look like crime scenes. So the founders borrowed a good camera and photographed the apartments themselves, one by one, by hand.

Bookings went up. Not because of some clever algorithm. Because the founders did manual, un-scalable, deeply unsexy work — talking to real users and fixing the obvious thing right in front of them.

[BEAT — lift in tone]

From there, the curve finally bent upward. The company that had relaunched and flatlined over and over became one of the most valuable hospitality companies on earth — without owning a single hotel room. By the time Airbnb went public in 2020, it was worth tens of billions of dollars.

[CLOSING — slow down delivery]

So what's the lesson? Not "never give up," because that's a poster, not advice. Here's the real one. Airbnb almost died — repeatedly — and what saved it wasn't a brilliant master plan. It was two things working together. One: they bought themselves time by any honest means necessary, even selling cereal. And two: when they finally had time, they spent it talking to actual customers and doing manual work that didn't scale.

If you're building something right now and it isn't taking off, the question usually isn't "should I quit." The question is "have I actually talked to the hundred people who would love this — in person, one at a time?" Most founders haven't. The ones who do are the ones who survive long enough to get lucky.

That's Airbnb. A company that started with three air mattresses, was kept alive by breakfast cereal, and grew because its founders refused to sit behind a laptop and guess.

I'm your host. This has been Founders' Stories. Next time: how a woman with five thousand dollars and a pair of scissors built a billion-dollar company without a single outside investor. See you then.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Lesson(s):** Buy yourself time by any honest means; growth often comes from un-scalable, manual customer work, not clever tactics.
**Hook line:** "The thing that funded the future of Airbnb was not Airbnb. It was breakfast cereal."

### **EPISODE 2: "Five Thousand Dollars and a Pair of Scissors — The Spanx Bootstrapping Story"**
**Air Date:** Day 8 (Week 2)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: Warm 4-second sting, fade under]

HOST: This is Founders' Stories — how businesses actually get built. Quick note up front: today's episode is a story put together from public accounts and the founder's own widely shared retellings. It's storytelling, not insider reporting. Let's get into it.

[BEAT]

In the late 1990s, a young woman named Sara Blakely was selling fax machines door to door in Florida. Not the dream. She'd wanted to be a lawyer, failed the entrance exam twice, and somewhere along the way ended up doing one of the hardest jobs there is: cold-calling strangers and getting doors shut in her face, over and over, all day.

Here's the thing about that job, though. It taught her two skills that would matter enormously later. The first was hearing "no" without falling apart. The second was selling something to a person who wasn't asking for it. Hold on to those, because they're the whole story.

[BEAT]

One evening, getting ready to go out, Sara had a problem familiar to a lot of people. She had a pair of cream-colored trousers and nothing that looked right underneath them. So she took a pair of scissors and cut the feet off a pair of control-top pantyhose. Instantly the look was smoother, but the cut edges kept rolling up her legs all night.

And that annoyance — that small, specific, personal annoyance — was the idea. A footless, smoothing undergarment that didn't roll. She figured someone must already make this. Nobody did.

She had about five thousand dollars in savings. That was the entire startup budget. No investors. No co-founder. No fashion background. No manufacturing contacts. What she had was a problem she'd personally felt and a stubborn belief that other women felt it too.

[BEAT — transition tone]

Now watch how she got it built, because this is where most people would have quit. She drove around to hosiery mills in North Carolina and pitched her idea. Almost every one of them turned her down. Reportedly, the running theme was: this is run by men who don't wear the product, who couldn't see why any woman would want it.

Then one mill owner called her back. Not because he was convinced — but, as the story goes, because he ran the idea past his daughters and they got it instantly. The customer understood the product before the industry did. That's a pattern you'll hear again and again on this show: the gatekeepers are often the last to see it.

She wrote her own patent to save on legal fees, working from a book she bought at a bookstore. She designed the packaging herself — choosing a bright red that would stand out on a shelf full of beige. She did it all on that tiny budget, because there was no other budget.

[BEAT]

Then came the part where those door-to-door skills paid off. She got the product into a major department store, Neiman Marcus, by — in the often-told version — taking the buyer into a bathroom and showing her the before-and-after on her own body. The buyer agreed to a trial in a handful of stores.

But getting on the shelf isn't the same as selling off the shelf. So Sara did something quietly brilliant. She'd go into the stores herself, find her own product tucked away in some forgotten corner, and move it to a better spot near the register. She bought her own product to keep the early numbers strong. She basically refused to let it fail through neglect.

And then it caught a break that no money can buy. The product reportedly landed in Oprah Winfrey's hands, and Oprah named it one of her favorite things. The phone, as you'd imagine, did not stop ringing.

[BEAT — lift]

From there, Spanx grew into a brand worth, by various reports, over a billion dollars — and for a long stretch, Sara Blakely owned essentially all of it, because she never took outside funding and never gave equity away. When she eventually sold a majority stake years later, she reportedly handed every employee plane tickets and a chunk of money to celebrate.

[CLOSING — slow down]

So what's the real lesson here? It's tempting to say "follow your passion," but Sara wasn't passionate about shapewear. She was passionate about solving a problem she'd actually had, cheaply, and refusing to let other people's lack of imagination be her ceiling.

Three things made it work, and none of them cost money. One: the idea came from a real, personal annoyance — not a market-research deck. Two: she expected rejection and treated it as data, not defeat, because selling fax machines had already taught her that "no" is just the start of the conversation. And three: she did the unglamorous follow-through — writing her own patent, fixing her own shelf placement, buying her own stock — because she cared more about the outcome than about looking like a real company.

You don't need five investors. Sometimes you need five thousand dollars, a problem you genuinely have, and the willingness to keep going after the door slams. That's Spanx.

I'm your host. This has been Founders' Stories. Next time: a software tool that was almost shut down by its own founders — and the rewrite that saved it. See you then.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Lesson(s):** Bootstrapping forces discipline; ideas from personal problems beat market-research ideas; rejection is data; do the unglamorous follow-through.
**Hook line:** "Five thousand dollars, a pair of scissors, and a problem nobody in the industry could see."

### **EPISODE 3: "The Rewrite That Saved It — How Notion Almost Died, Then Rebuilt"**
**Air Date:** Day 15 (Week 3)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: Warm 4-second sting, fade under]

HOST: Welcome to Founders' Stories — how businesses actually get built. As always, a quick note: this is a story reconstructed from public accounts and the founders' own retellings. Storytelling, not insider reporting. Here we go.

[BEAT]

Around 2013, a small team led by Ivan Zhao set out to build a tool that would let anyone create their own software — documents, databases, wikis, the works — without writing code. The dream was huge. Honestly, maybe too huge. They wanted to reshape how people use computers. And for the first couple of years, that ambition nearly killed the company.

Here's what went wrong, and it's a trap a lot of founders fall into. They built something incredibly ambitious and incredibly complicated. The product was buggy. It tried to do too much. It was technically fragile — built on a foundation that kept breaking under its own weight. And it wasn't catching on. The team had grown, money was running low, and they were staring at the very real possibility that this thing was over.

[BEAT — transition tone]

So in 2015, they made a decision that sounds insane on paper. They didn't just cut features. They tore the whole thing down. They laid off the team — reportedly the founders had to let people go — and a tiny core group, basically Ivan and his co-founder Simon Last, decided to rebuild the product almost from scratch.

And then they did something even more dramatic. To cut their burn rate to almost nothing, they reportedly left San Francisco — one of the most expensive cities on earth — and decamped to Kyoto, Japan, where they could live cheaply and just code. Two people. A radically simpler vision. Months of heads-down rebuilding.

Stop and feel how risky that is. They'd already nearly failed. They'd let go of their team. And their response wasn't to do more or hire more — it was to do *less*, with fewer people, in a cheaper place, betting everything on a cleaner foundation. Most companies in that position try to raise more money and push harder on the broken thing. Notion did the opposite.

[BEAT]

When they came back, the new version was built on a much more solid technical foundation, and crucially, it was simpler and more focused. Same big vision underneath — software anyone can shape — but expressed through a product that actually worked and that people could understand. They relaunched it.

And this time, something different happened. People didn't just use it. They loved it. They wrote about it. They made videos showing off the elaborate systems they'd built inside it — habit trackers, company wikis, entire personal operating systems. Notion grew not through a giant marketing budget but through devoted users who treated the product like a creative medium and evangelized it for free.

[BEAT — lift]

The growth that followed was the kind founders fantasize about. A product that had nearly died as a bloated, buggy mess came back lean, and rode word-of-mouth to millions of users and, eventually, a valuation reported in the billions of dollars. The same core idea. A completely different execution.

[CLOSING — slow down]

So what do we take from this? The headline isn't "pivot." Notion didn't change what it believed in. The vision stayed almost identical. What changed was the willingness to admit that the *execution* was wrong, and to be brutal about it.

Two lessons stand out. First: ambition without a solid foundation is a slow-motion failure. They tried to build the cathedral before they could lay a single straight brick, and it nearly buried them. Sometimes the bravest move is to tear down two years of work and start clean. Second: cutting back can be a strategy, not just a surrender. By going smaller — fewer people, lower costs, a simpler product — they bought themselves the focus and the runway to actually get it right.

If you're drowning in a product that's too complex, too buggy, and not catching on, the instinct is to add: more features, more people, more money. Notion's story says the opposite might save you. Subtract. Simplify. Rebuild the foundation. Then let the people who genuinely love it do your marketing for you.

That's Notion — a company that had to almost die before it could actually live.

I'm your host. This has been Founders' Stories. Next time: a man who built five thousand one hundred and twenty-six failed prototypes before one worked. Five thousand failures. One success. See you then.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Lesson(s):** Vision can be right while execution is wrong; tearing down and rebuilding on a solid foundation beats piling features on a broken one; subtraction and lower burn can buy the focus to win; loyal users are the cheapest growth engine.
**Hook line:** "Their response to nearly failing wasn't to do more. It was to do less, with fewer people, in a cheaper city."

### **EPISODE 4: "Five Thousand One Hundred and Twenty-Six Failures — The Dyson Prototype Story"**
**Air Date:** Day 22 (Week 4)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: Warm 4-second sting, fade under]

HOST: This is Founders' Stories — how businesses actually get built. Quick note before we start: today's episode is a story assembled from public accounts and the inventor's own widely repeated retellings. Storytelling, not insider reporting. Let's begin.

[BEAT]

In the late 1970s, a British inventor named James Dyson got annoyed at his vacuum cleaner. A small thing. It kept losing suction as the bag filled up. The dust clogged the bag, the airflow choked, and the machine became useless long before the bag was actually full. Most people would shrug and buy a new bag. Dyson decided to fix the entire concept of the vacuum cleaner.

He'd seen industrial sawmills use something called a cyclone — a spinning column of air that flings dust outward and separates it from the airflow. He wondered: could you shrink that idea down and put it inside a vacuum cleaner, so it would never need a bag and never lose suction?

Good idea. Now here comes the part that made him famous — and nearly broke him.

[BEAT — transition tone]

To get from idea to working product, Dyson built prototypes. One after another. He'd build a version, test it, find the flaw, change one small thing, and build the next version. And he did this not a dozen times, not a hundred times, but — by his own account — five thousand one hundred and twenty-six times.

Five thousand, one hundred and twenty-six prototypes. The five thousand one hundred and twenty-seventh was the one that worked.

Let that sink in, because it's easy to hear it as a fun trivia number and miss the human reality. This took years. Roughly five years. During that time he reportedly went deep into debt to fund the work, supported by his family. He wasn't a wealthy industrialist tinkering for fun. He was a man with a mortgage and children, building thousands of failed machines in a shed, watching the money drain away, with no guarantee any of it would ever pay off.

And here's the mindset that's worth stealing. Dyson has said he didn't see those as five thousand failures. He saw each one as learning exactly one more thing that didn't work — and every "didn't work" narrowed the path to the thing that would. A failure wasn't a verdict on him. It was information.

[BEAT]

When he finally had the working cyclone vacuum, you'd think the hard part was over. It wasn't. He took it to the big manufacturers — and they didn't want it. Why would they? The existing vacuum companies made a fortune selling replacement bags. A bagless vacuum that never lost suction would destroy a profitable part of their own business. So they turned him down. The incumbents had every reason to reject the better product, because the better product threatened their cash cow.

That rejection is its own lesson. Sometimes the people best positioned to make your idea aren't your customers — they're your competitors, and they'll quietly hope you fail.

So Dyson did what bootstrappers and stubborn founders do. He went around them. He licensed the technology elsewhere to raise funds, fought legal battles over his patents, and eventually launched the vacuum under his own name. And when consumers finally got their hands on it, they bought it. In a few years, his bagless vacuum became one of the best-selling vacuums in the country, and Dyson became a household name and, eventually, a billionaire.

[BEAT — lift]

The company he built kept that same DNA — engineers obsessively iterating, failing on purpose, refusing to accept "good enough." The five-thousand-prototype habit wasn't a one-time ordeal. It became the entire culture.

[CLOSING — slow down]

So what's the real takeaway, beyond "persistence is good"? Persistence on its own is just stubbornness, and stubbornness can sink you. The thing that made Dyson's persistence *work* was that every single attempt produced information. He wasn't doing the same thing five thousand times and hoping. He was changing one variable, learning, and moving forward. That's not banging your head against a wall. That's a methodical search.

Two lessons. First: reframe failure as data. If each attempt teaches you something specific that the last one didn't, you're not failing — you're narrowing in. Five thousand experiments is just five thousand answers to the question "is it this?" Second: expect the incumbents to reject your best ideas, especially when those ideas threaten how they make money. Their "no" is often a signal you're onto something real.

If you're on attempt number twelve and feeling like a fraud, remember the number. Five thousand one hundred and twenty-seven. The difference between Dyson and the people who quit at attempt thirty wasn't talent. It was that he treated each failure as a lesson, not a sentence.

That's James Dyson. The man who failed five thousand one hundred and twenty-six times on purpose.

I'm your host. This has been Founders' Stories. Next time: how a greeting-card side project became an email empire — with no outside funding, for nearly two decades. See you then.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Lesson(s):** Persistence only works when each attempt produces information — reframe failure as data; methodical iteration beats stubbborn repetition; incumbents reject ideas that threaten their cash cow, and that rejection can be a signal.
**Hook line:** "Five thousand, one hundred and twenty-six prototypes. The next one worked."

### **EPISODE 5: "Twenty Years, No Investors — How Mailchimp Grew on Its Own Money"**
**Air Date:** Day 29 (Week 5)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: Warm 4-second sting, fade under]

HOST: Welcome back to Founders' Stories — how businesses actually get built. One quick note as always: this is a story put together from public accounts and the founders' own retellings. Storytelling, not insider reporting. Let's get into it.

[BEAT]

In the early 2000s, two people in Atlanta — Ben Chestnut and Dan Kurzius — were running a small web design agency. Not a startup with a grand mission. A service business, building websites for clients to pay the bills. And like a lot of agencies, they kept getting asked by clients for the same thing: help sending email newsletters.

So they built a little tool on the side to do it. They called it Mailchimp. It was a side project. It was not the plan. The plan was the agency. Mailchimp was the thing they tinkered with when they had a spare moment.

And here's the first lesson, hiding right at the start: you don't always have to know you're sitting on the big idea. Sometimes the big idea is the boring side tool that solves a problem your customers keep repeating to you. The signal was there — clients kept asking — they just had to notice it.

[BEAT — transition tone]

Now here's what makes this story unusual, and why founders should pay attention. For most of its life, Mailchimp took no venture capital. None. No outside investors, no big funding rounds, for nearly two decades. They grew it on revenue — the money customers actually paid them. That's called bootstrapping, and in the startup world, where raising millions is treated as the only real way to win, doing it on your own money for that long is almost unheard of.

That single decision shaped everything. Because they weren't spending investor money, they couldn't afford to chase growth at any cost. They had to build something people would actually pay for, quickly, and then keep them happy enough to keep paying. There was no safety net of someone else's cash. The customer *was* the funding.

For years it grew slowly and steadily. They reinvested profits. They kept the team lean. They focused on small businesses — the customers the big enterprise email companies mostly ignored — and they made the product friendly, approachable, even playful, with that famous chimp mascot. While competitors talked to corporations, Mailchimp talked to the little guy.

[BEAT]

Then they made one move that's now studied as a masterstroke. In 2009, they shifted to a freemium model — they let small businesses use the product for free up to a certain size, and only charged once those businesses grew. People thought it was risky. Why give it away?

Because it aligned their success with their customers' success. A tiny business could start for nothing, grow using Mailchimp, and start paying as it got bigger. Mailchimp won when its customers won. Reportedly, in the year after going freemium, their user base and revenue exploded. Giving it away for free was the thing that made them rich.

[BEAT — lift]

They kept compounding, year after year, on their own money, serving the small businesses everyone else overlooked. And in 2021, that bootstrapped side project — the one that started as a favor to web-design clients — was acquired for a reported twelve billion dollars. Twelve billion. With a B. From founders who never took a dollar of outside funding and, as a result, owned a huge share of what they sold.

[CLOSING — slow down]

So what's the honest lesson here, with no hustle-porn attached? It's not "never raise money" — plenty of great companies raise capital, and bootstrapping is slower and harder and not right for every business. The lesson is subtler than that.

Three things. First: your next big thing might be the side tool you're treating as a chore. Mailchimp's founders almost didn't take it seriously. Watch what your customers keep asking for. Second: bootstrapping isn't just a financing choice, it's a discipline. Because every dollar came from a customer, they were forced to build things people genuinely valued, and to keep them happy. Constraints made the product better. Third: align your success with your customer's success. The freemium move worked because Mailchimp only got paid more when its customers grew. Build a model where you win when they win, and you've built something durable.

Twenty years. No investors. A side project that became a twelve-billion-dollar company by serving the customers everyone else ignored, on its own money, one happy small business at a time.

That's Mailchimp — and that's the slow, unglamorous, deeply real way most great companies actually get built.

I'm your host. This has been Founders' Stories. Thank you for spending twelve minutes with a real story instead of a highlight reel. New episode every Tuesday. See you then.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Lesson(s):** Your big idea may be the side tool customers keep requesting; bootstrapping is a discipline that forces real value; align your revenue model with customer success; serving the overlooked segment is an underrated moat.
**Hook line:** "Twenty years. No investors. A side project sold for twelve billion dollars."

## Show Format (Every Episode)

**Structure (12 minutes):**
- **Intro + disclosure (45 sec)** — Hook line + brief "this is a story from public accounts" note.
- **The setup (2 min)** — Who the founder was, the ordinary moment the idea was born, the personal problem behind it.
- **The struggle / near-death (4–5 min)** — The wrong turns, the rejections, the moment it almost ended. This is the heart of the episode.
- **The turn (2–3 min)** — The decision or insight that changed everything.
- **The outcome (1.5 min)** — Where it landed, told without glorification.
- **The lesson (2 min)** — One or two concrete, usable takeaways. Explicitly anti-cliché ("not 'never give up,' here's the real one").
- **Outro (30 sec)** — Next episode teaser + cadence reminder.

**Tone:**
- Inspiring but **honest** — include the debt, the layoffs, the rejection, the luck.
- **Lesson-focused** — every story earns its place by teaching something usable.
- **Anti-hustle-porn** — no 4am-grind worship, no overnight-success myths, no "you just have to want it more."
- Conversational, like a smart friend telling you a story they can't wait to share.
- Respectful of nuance — flag where accounts differ or where luck (not just merit) played a role.
- Solo TTS narration — clear, warm, measured pacing (~140 wpm).

## Sponsor Strategy

The Founders' Stories audience is unusually valuable: people actively building or running businesses, with budgets and buying intent. Aim for **2 natural ad reads per episode** (one early, one mid-roll), integrated into the host's voice rather than dropped-in jingles.

**Premium Tier ($8–25k/mo):**
- **Business banking / fintech** — Mercury, Relay, Wise Business, Novo (founders need an account on day one).
- **All-in-one SaaS platforms** — HubSpot, Notion, Airtable (directly relevant to the audience; Notion is even an episode subject).

**Mid Tier ($3–8k/mo):**
- **Web hosting / deployment** — Vercel, Netlify, DigitalOcean, Hostinger, Squarespace (everyone needs a site).

- **Productivity & dev tools** — Linear, Loom, 1Password, GitHub.
- **Bookkeeping / finance** — QuickBooks, Bench, Wave (founders dread the books).

**Lower Tier ($500–3k/mo):**
- **Online courses / education** — Maven, Skillshare, Coursera, indie founder courses.
- **Podcast hosting** — Transistor, Buzzsprout, Captivate.
- **Formation / legal** — LegalZoom, Stripe Atlas, Clerky (LLC formation).

**Ad reads per episode:** 2 natural integrations (early-roll + mid-roll). Always disclose host-read ads as ads.
