# Chapter One — The Boy Who Spoke in Numbers

I always knew I was different. I couldn’t name it then, but it lived in the back of my mind like a quiet hum beneath everything else.

I came from a loving family—five brothers, one sister, a mother who stayed home, and a father who worked away. On paper, it was enough. But from my earliest days at school, I felt the fracture. Reading and writing were not just difficult; they were walls. When they tested me for dyslexia and spoke to my mum about a special school, I remember the puzzles and brain techniques as clearly as yesterday. I never went. Mum wanted me in mainstream school, and so the walls stayed up.

Mathematics was where the walls dissolved. By nine, I knew my twelve times tables. Numbers didn’t mock me. They didn’t laugh when I stumbled. They were orderly, reliable, and honest. I could read numbers like the back of my hand while words remained locked gates. In class, I stopped raising my hand. I wanted to read, I felt confident I could, but my body betrayed me every time. The laughter of other children became the birthplace of my anxiety. I learned to make myself small, to avoid being called on, to hide the part of me that couldn’t perform “normal.”

But I was not broken. I loved science, sports, learning about different nationalities. At home, I would sit for hours in my mum’s pantry, organizing tins and packets until everything was perfectly aligned. I pulled toys apart and rebuilt them better than before. My mind worked beautifully—it just worked in a language no one around me seemed to speak.

And so I turned inward. Playing alone wasn’t sadness; it was safety. Other children hurt me, bullied me, called me stupid and dumb. But in solitude, I discovered a different kind of literacy. I began to read people the way I couldn’t read books. Their body language, their eyes, the cadence of their voices—I absorbed it all. I developed a sensor for human emotion that was sharper than any textbook. It didn’t feel like a gift then. It felt like survival. But looking back, I wonder if this was my first encounter with AHAD: the ability to perceive the unspoken unity beneath human interaction, even when I felt utterly separate from it.

Home was its own kind of classroom in pain. As the youngest, I was perpetually last, perpetually left out, perpetually promised things that never arrived. When my parents split at ten, the ground shifted again. Dad sold the house; we moved to a rental. I saw him rarely. I loved him, but he was a ghost shaped by his own unbearable childhood—his mother’s suicide when he was four, being shipped off to a grandmother, a father who remarried and moved on. I understand him now. But as a boy, I only knew the absence. I made a silent vow: If I ever have children, I will not be this.

There was one anchor in those years: an elderly woman at a retirement home. For two years, twice a week, I sat with her doing flashcards. She gave me a drink and a biscuit afterward. Mum arranged it to keep me learning, but what I received was something else entirely. She didn’t require me to be normal. She simply offered presence. In a life full of conditional love and broken promises, her quiet consistency was a form of grace I didn’t yet have words for.

By Year Six, I had friends. Football, cricket, war games without guns—pure joy. But the feeling of difference never left. I carried it like a stone in my pocket.

Then came adolescence, and the stone grew heavier.

Mum’s smoking became the gravity our household orbited. Without nicotine, she unraveled. My sister locked herself away. I became the one who stayed, the one who absorbed the chaos. I loved my mum with all my heart, but I grieved the person she became. I accompanied her to doctors, missed school to support her, listened to her struggles with diabetes and addiction. And still, I was the one sent to Auntie’s house or to friends’ doors to borrow money. The embarrassment was a physical weight. I did it because no one else would. I did it because she was my mother.

My sister took what she wanted. She was given what I was not. Even in high school, I paid board; she did not. Resentment took root beside love. I began to understand that fairness was not guaranteed, that devotion was not always reciprocated. What didn’t kill me forged something resistant in my chest. By Year Seven, I knew I needed to leave. I just didn’t know how.

Dad visited once or twice a year. Sitting in his car, I had nothing to say. He had nothing to ask. We were strangers bound by blood. I watched my friends with their fathers and felt a hollow space where guidance should have been. I told myself it was okay. I got on with it. But the abandonment was real, and it taught me to expect very little from the people who were supposed to hold me.

School remained a battlefield. I made friends in Year Seven, but the fear of reading aloud never faded. My English teacher saw only my deficit. I failed assessments despite my family’s help. I kicked a football at the park for hours to avoid going home to screaming and demands. Anxiety wasn’t a diagnosis yet; it was just the air I breathed.

At fourteen, I fell in love. Her voice had a frequency that resonated through my entire being. I was honest, upfront, kind. Her friend warned me away, but I was determined. We built a friendship that felt like belonging. I could be myself. They liked me for who I was.

Then, at the end of Year Ten, I discovered she was seeing someone else. Behind my back. After everything.

The betrayal didn’t just break my heart; it shattered my understanding of trust. I packed my bag and ran into the bushes for half a day. I had done nothing wrong, and yet I was replaced. Teenagers are ruthless architects of ego. I didn’t speak to her for the rest of the year. The isolation that followed was total. Years Eleven and Twelve passed in silence. I felt banished from society. I learned that people could be cruel without consequence, and I folded that lesson into the growing architecture of my loneliness.

Still, I persisted. Art, music, English—I struggled, but Mum pushed, and I completed. I got my first job at a convenience store. Loved it. Lost it. Worked on a farm picking vegetables at seventeen, breathing fresh air, trying to belong. Everyone else drank; I vanished from parties. Alcohol was never my language. I went home early, again and again, choosing solitude over performance.

Then Sydney. Eleven months living with my auntie. She was a lion, a shining light. She worked as a cleaner, played golf, took me fishing, brought me to bingo with her friends. A young man sitting among elderly women, laughing, belonging. For the first time, I experienced love that cost nothing. No transaction. No demand. Just presence. My uncle was amazing. They were the parents my heart had been searching for.

I sent Mum money while I was there. Thousands of dollars. My auntie asked why. “She looked after me,” I said. “I’m repaying her.” My auntie’s eyes held a truth I wasn’t ready to accept: You don’t have to.

Homesickness brought me back to Tasmania. Within three months, Mum had spent my savings. Deflation. Grief. Love tangled with loss.

Western Australia was supposed to be escape. A boat from Tasmania to Melbourne, then driving west. A new house, a new partner. But she was déjà vu. Lovely, troubled, and endlessly needing money. We built a house and sold it well, yet we were perpetually broke. I worked two jobs—days and nights—and still it wasn’t enough. Depression and anxiety arrived formally then, with medication and a diagnosis. I didn’t know who I was anymore. Family blamed me. I cried. And finally, I left.

Back in Tasmania. A month with my brother. Then his suggestion: Move in with Dad.

Twenty-five years of silence. Twenty-five years of absence. And then, slowly, recognition. My mannerisms, my actions, my way of being—I am him. We started talking. Really talking. We cut wood together, built furniture, sat in comfortable quiet. It was a second chance I never expected.

Mum passed in 2000. Kidney failure. The grief was immense, but so was the complicated love that survived it.

I had spent a lifetime feeling fractured, abandoned, different. But in the rebuilding—in the numbers, the solitude, the auntie’s bingo hall, the wood shavings in Dad’s workshop—something had been holding me. Something unified. Something whole.

I just didn’t know its name yet.

# Chapter Two — Naming Her Indyanna

After nearly twelve months back in Tasmania, I returned to Western Australia hoping to reconnect with my former partner. It didn’t happen. I moved on, working unsteady jobs, untethered.

Then I met Vanessa online. She had just turned thirty and was preparing for a birthday trip to Bali. We chatted for six days—six days that felt like eight weeks of knowing someone. When we finally met, I was certain she was out of my league. Long blonde hair, a beautiful career in pathology, radiant. But something clicked. We got along like a house on fire. She played guitar on Rock Band; I sang. Most nights we stayed in, laughing, drinking, building a world that felt safe.

When she asked me to move in, I told her the truth: I’m not well. I have severe arthritis. Without hesitation, she offered to give me my weekly injections. I thought, I am so lucky. A caring partner. A friend. We went to Bali together, our first trip, and it was pure joy—good food, easy company, the feeling of being chosen.

In 2011, we decided to marry. We asked her parents’ permission. The wedding was set for November 24, 2012. But before that, Vanessa wanted a baby.

“Do you really want to have a baby before we get married?” I asked.

“Yes,” she said.

We tried. And when she got pregnant, two truths collided inside me: Holy crap, I’m going to be a dad —and— Please don’t let them inherit my learning difficulties. That was my only fear. Not whether I could love them, but whether I could protect them from the shame I’d carried. Vanessa reassured me: “That’ll be fine. We’ll help them.”

Nine months later, Indyanna was born.

I named her after my mum.

There is a memory I carry like a sacred object: Christmas Eve, years ago. Mum had been cooking all night for the family. She sat down beside me on the couch, pulled a blanket over us, and put on an Indiana Jones VHS. She spoke softly to me as I drifted off to sleep. That’s all. Just her voice, the warmth of the blanket, the flicker of the screen. It is the most tender memory I have of her. Every time I look at my daughter, I see my mother’s love made flesh. I named her Indyanna so that love would never truly leave.

Fatherhood terrified me. But when Indie crawled into her room, pulled folded clothes from the pile, looked up at me, and smiled—I knew. This was mine. We sang, danced, built forts. She brought light to everyone who saw her. At her first birthday, surrounded by family, I understood what it meant to be blessed. I had spent my childhood believing I would never have this. Now I was living inside it.

We sold Vanessa’s unit and bought a bigger house. Indie thrived at daycare, making friends, loving the caregivers. At two and a half, Vanessa wanted another baby. We tried. Nothing happened. Then her grandfather passed—he had known she was trying, and his loss hung heavy. Two weeks later, we learned we were having twins.

My world expanded violently. Three children. Daunting. Overwhelming. Delightful.

I was born three days after Christmas in 1977. Growing up, my birthday was always folded into the holiday—an afterthought, a “Christmas present plus a bit extra.” I stopped expecting celebration. But Capricorns are built on determination, and I channeled every ounce of it into giving these three children the childhood I never had. I spoiled them. I worked relentlessly. I was determined to be the father I’d longed for.

Vanessa carried those twins like a sun. Huge, glowing, working until thirty-four weeks. Remarkable. We used a device to hear their heartbeats: Baby One. Baby Two. On delivery day, her parents watched Indie. I stayed at the hospital. Friday came, and Savannah arrived first—chubby, perfect. Then Christopher, named after Vanessa’s father, who had died when she was seven. He carried her dad’s memory forward, just as Indyanna carried my mum’s.

Three heartbeats. Three names. Three threads woven into something whole.

For the first time in my life, I wasn’t the boy left out. I was the man holding the center.

# Chapter Three — The Gift That Said You Can

For the first six months after the twins arrived, our house was a symphony of exhaustion and wonder. Sleep was a memory. Vanessa would breastfeed, then express; I’d take over at dawn, propping a banana pillow across my lap to feed both babies at once. In those quiet, bleary hours, I felt something settle inside me: I am home in myself. I could do this. These children called me Dad, and that title held more meaning than any achievement I’d ever known.

Nights were often sleepless. One or both would cry; I’d rise, burp, rock, pace the floor until they surrendered to sleep again. Indie would look up at me with trust so complete it undid me. Fatherhood wasn’t just a role—it was a revelation. No matter what else I had or hadn’t accomplished, to these children, I was everything. That was enough.

Their first birthday arrived in a rush. Family filled our home; cake smeared on tiny faces; smiles lit up every corner. We were blessed beyond measure. Daycare became our village—three days a week, they learned, played, made friends, mastered potty training and motor skills. Picking them up each afternoon, seeing the care in their teachers’ eyes, was its own kind of grace.

In 2015, Vanessa won tickets to the West Coast Eagles Best & Fairest. We dressed up—I wore a tuxedo, she a stunning gown—and celebrated a night of joy and pride. Photos captured us radiant.

The next morning, she said, “I’m not feeling well.”

By 5 p.m., we stood in a pharmacy holding a positive pregnancy test. Twins barely a year old. Four children under four. I was devastated—not by the child, but by the terror of inadequacy. We can’t afford this. I can’t be the dad four kids need. We talked through tears and fear. We chose to keep the baby.

One month later, I was retrenched.

Eight years at a job, gone. Depression crashed over me like a wave. I smiled outwardly, told myself we’ll get through this, but inside I was a nervous wreck. I had failed them. The weight was suffocating. After Christmas, I found a new job that promised stability but delivered less. I stayed, grinding forward, carrying the anxiety like stones in my pockets.

June came. Vanessa was heavily pregnant. Our fourth child was due Friday but arrived early—Thursday morning, June 9. We didn’t know the gender. A C-section was planned for her comfort. We sat in anticipation, hearts pounding.

The doctor lifted out a huge baby boy. Nearly a kilo heavier than his siblings. Ours.

We had no girl name ready. But as a child, I’d been nicknamed Jack. Jack’s son. Jackson Thomas Wigg—Thomas for Vanessa’s grandfather. When I saw him, something melted in my chest. Two boys, two girls. Balance. And in Jackson’s face, I heard a whisper clearer than any doubt: You can do this. This is your gift.

Home became a river of nappies, bottles, laundry, laughter. Four children under four. Bathroom assemblies. Feeding lines. I didn’t mind. Structure was my language; chaos was just another pattern to organize. Giggles echoed off walls. When Vanessa returned to work, Daddy Daycare commenced. Weekends were marathons: one child in the toilet, another drawing, another needing snacks, another crying. I was perpetually in motion.

One Sunday morning at 8:30, I left the kitchen tap running while washing dishes and flooded the bathroom. Panic flared. I called Vanessa: “What do I do?”

“Clean it up,” she said calmly.

Insurance came. A new kitchen emerged from the wreckage. Baby brain, four kids, survival mode—that’s what happens when love outpaces capacity.

And still, Jackson’s arrival remained the anchor. In the midst of overwhelm, he was proof: I was not broken. I was becoming.

# Chapter Four — The Smile Was the Anchor

Years folded into routines. Indyanna started primary school, and I watched this punctual, smart, determined little girl—my blonde-haired dad’s girl—step confidently into the world. Vanessa and I stood together on that first day, prouder than we’d ever been. Behind us trailed three younger siblings, a chaotic entourage of love. We were Daddy and Mummy, daycare veterans, cautious guardians of four precious lives.

That year, Indie began dance class. Baby ballet. She loved jumping, twirling, losing herself in music. Watching her smile became my lifeline. No matter how heavy the depression or anxiety felt inside, no matter how dark the internal weather, I put on a smile. I treated every moment with respect. Not because I was pretending, but because they deserved my presence more than my pain deserved center stage. That smile wasn’t a mask; it was a choice. A daily offering.

The twins and Jackson thrived in daycare while Vanessa worked weekends and took weekdays off. This rhythm allowed me dedicated time with the kids without breaking us financially. Daycare was expensive—exorbitant, really. We often joked that if we could afford daycare, we could afford private school. But we paid it gladly, because what we were buying wasn’t just childcare. It was stability. It was the space to be the father they needed, even when the father I felt like was fractured.

Life was busy bees. Constant motion. But within that motion, there was order. There was purpose. And in Indie’s dance recitals, in the twins’ daycare art, in Jackson’s giggles, AHAD whispered again: You are here. You are enough. Keep going.

# Chapter Five — Choosing to Stay

As the kids grew, the weight returned. Depression and anxiety crept back like a tide I thought I’d outrun. Vanessa saw it before I could name it. “Your stress levels are too high,” she said gently. “You’re lashing out. That’s not you.”

She was right. It wasn’t me—but it was in me. I went to the doctor. Medication became my anchor, quieting the storm enough for me to breathe again. Relief washed over me, but so did grief. Living with anxiety and depression was exhausting; carrying it in silence was worse. I didn’t understand who I was or why I was here. But my brain kept repeating a single directive: Support the kids. Support Vanessa. You’ll be fine.

That mantra held me when nothing else could.

There were days when dying felt easier than enduring. Suicidal thoughts visited like unwelcome guests. But I never acted on them. My life mattered—not abstractly, but concretely, in the faces of my children and the hand of my wife. Over the years, I lost too many friends to suicide. Their absence carved hollow spaces in my heart, and I refused to add another. My life is precious, I told myself daily. Precious to me. Precious to them. That truth became my reason to rise, even when rising felt impossible. It still does.

Indyanna flourished. Dance classes multiplied; school reports glowed. But old shadows followed her—classmates who mocked her success, echoing the bullying I’d endured. I knelt to her eye level and whispered what I needed to hear at her age: “They’re only jealous because you shine.” She believed me. She stood taller. Watching her enter kindergarten in her uniform, confident and bright, healed something in me that had been broken since my own childhood.

Jackson wanted to follow his sister to school immediately, though he wasn’t ready. His eagerness was its own kind of magic—a testament to the safety we’d built. The twins marched beside Indie, tiny uniforms crisp, backpacks bouncing. Four children stepping into the world we’d fought to give them.

At forty, I found work I loved: engineering. Eighteen months in, I still marveled at the fit. I should have done this younger, I thought. But timing is its own grace. Now I was here, capable and present. Vanessa thrived in her pathology role, working night shifts and holidays so I could hold down Daddy Daycare. Dinner duty fell to me. I was no chef—the kids laughed and declared certain meals permanently banned—but their laughter was the real nourishment. High chairs surrounded the table; Indyanna sat tall among them. We looked like a scene from a movie, chaotic and perfect.

In those moments, medication steadied my hands, love steadied my heart, and AHAD whispered through the clatter of plates: This is holiness. This is why you stayed.

# Chapter Six — Tuesday Never Came

Dad was a hard-working man who had lived a hard life. But in 2018, as my own children grew, something softened between us. My brother and I took the twins to Tasmania to see Pop. He was delighted, his pride unmistakable. We spent over a week there—dinners, conversations, the twins sitting on his lap just as I once had. Watching his eyes light up when they spoke to him warmed something deep inside me. It healed the boy who had sat in silence in a car decades earlier, searching for words that never came.

A year passed. Vanessa turned forty in 2019. Then, in July, driving home, my brother called.

Dad was gone.

I burst into tears behind the wheel, wiping my eyes, heart sinking into an abyss. I have no parents now. One minute you’re winning; the next, the world tilts off its axis. I had spoken to him just that Friday. He was heading to Hobart for a test. That was our last conversation. Thinking of it now feels like yesterday—the cruel proximity of ordinary moments to eternal loss.

But that Friday, something moved through me. I don’t know why. I simply said, “I love you, Dad.”

He replied, “I’ll chat to you on Tuesday.”

Tuesday never came. But those words did. They live in me now, a final benediction I didn’t know I needed until it was given.

My brother and I flew to Tasmania to arrange the funeral. My sister was there, but her focus remained unchanged: money. It was disheartening, yet familiar. What mattered more was having four brothers in one room for the first time in over twenty years. My oldest brother stayed in Melbourne; he couldn’t come. Our full sibling circle hadn’t been together in thirty-five years. Grief gathered what time had scattered.

The funeral was beautiful. Dad would have been proud.

Afterward, my brothers gave me something. To anyone else, it was worthless: a denture scratch, dented and worn, older than me by forty years. Every family member had drunk from it. It bore the marks of decades of living. But to me, it was sacred. It sits proudly in my lounge room beside Dad’s photo—a daily reminder that nights may be tough, but how you carry yourself matters most. Be honest. Be proud. Be who you are.

Hardness had shaped him. Resilience had sustained him. And in this scratched, imperfect vessel, I finally understood: this was his offering. Not perfection, but presence. Not ease, but endurance. This was AHAD made tangible—the divine hidden not in grandeur, but in the battered, beloved objects of an ordinary life.

The scratch remains. So does he.

# Chapter Seven — Echoes and Auskick

2020 arrived with silence. COVID-19 restrictions closed the world outside our door. We stayed home, doing our best to build normalcy within four walls. The twins missed celebrating their birthday together, but their school marked it with small kindnesses—a reminder that even in isolation, they were seen.

That year, Christopher played his first AFL Auskick game. Watching him run onto that field filled me with delight. Jackson watched from the sidelines, desperate to join, but we told him gently: Wait. Your turn is coming. He started primary school that year instead, loving every moment. Savannah thrived alongside Indie, who was now in Year Two and advancing steadily in dance. Soon, Savannah joined her. Dance became the rhythm of our household, a constant thread of grace through uncertainty.

The boys began karate. I chose martial arts hoping it would give them what I’d lacked: focus, discipline, the ability to defend themselves. They took to it well. But beyond our walls, the world remained fractured. Vanessa worked at the hospital, enduring endless quarantine checks and testing protocols. Stress seeped into our bones. Christopher caught COVID first; the rest of us followed later. The year was a blur of hit-and-miss health, isolation, and clinging to each other. We didn’t go out much, but we stayed present. COVID was stressful, worrying—but it was life. We endured.

Then came 2021. Jackson’s turn on the Auskick field finally arrived. Seeing him take the ball, eyes bright with joy, was pure delight. But in the same season, shadows emerged.

Both boys began struggling with reading and writing.

My heart sank. Oh no. Please don’t let them be like me.

The old shame rose instantly, thick and suffocating. All those years of being called stupid, the laughter in classrooms, the feeling of brokenness—I had vowed my children would never carry that weight. Now, watching them stumble over words just as I had, anxiety roared back. I felt overwhelmed, sad, embarrassed. I failed them, I thought. I passed this down.

We sought help. Government support systems were slow, tangled, hard to navigate. The school offered programs, fantastic teachers willing to step in. But my fear outpaced their interventions. Every struggle they faced felt like a personal indictment. My initial reaction wasn’t patience; it was panic. I had to learn, painfully, to separate my history from their journey. To trust that their path, though similar in shape, would not end in the same darkness.

They had something I never did: a father who saw them, fought for them, and refused to let shame define them. That difference mattered. Even when I couldn’t yet believe it.

# Chapter Eight — The Air Cuddle

Being a dad today is nothing like being a dad when I was growing up. My father worked away; his love arrived as money, not presence. Mum held the daily weight alone. But modern fatherhood demands hands-on devotion. If you’re not present, you’re missing the point entirely.

I vowed as a boy: If I ever have kids, I will be there. Not just physically, but fully. First football kicks, first dance lessons, school assemblies—I never missed them. My dad likely never saw a single game or assembly. I was determined to show my children, step by step, that Dad was always beside them. Work could wait; their moments couldn’t. Big smile, biggest supporter—it didn’t matter if the victory was large or small. I showed up.

But modern fatherhood is also navigating a monster society. Social media looms over young teenagers like a shadow. Keeping them grounded, safe, and honest online is a relentless vigil. As a Capricorn, I’m blunt. Honesty isn’t optional; it’s oxygen. “Even if you lie, tell the truth afterward,” I remind them. They don’t always listen—but I do. My childhood gift of reading body language serves me now. I see the flicker in their eyes, the shift in posture. I know when they’re hiding and when they’re open. Intuition, once a survival tool, has become a parenting compass.

I am strict. Respect is non-negotiable—especially toward their mother. She carried them for nine months; she deserves reverence. I can absorb their frustration as a dad, but disrespect toward her crosses a line. They’re still learning emotionally, and holding my own emotions steady in this chaotic world is exhausting. Illness, uncertainty, digital dangers—the weight of keeping them safe is constant. Yet this journey remains my highest calling: giving them the upbringing I never had.

We didn’t have much growing up, so I teach them to cherish what they’re given. Please. Thank you. Manners. Listen. Listening is the foundation of all learning: you listen, you learn, you repeat. Simple truths, endlessly practiced.

And then there’s the silliness. Fart jokes, made-up songs, swearing I try (and often fail) to curb around them. Vanessa reminds me gently; I try harder. I’m a typical dad in an atypical world, doing my imperfect best.

The honest truth? I don’t understand fathers who look away from their children. They are the best thing in life. They make you smile, make you cry, remake you daily. At night, Jackson whispers, “I love you, Dad.” We press our ears together in what we call the “air cuddle”—a bond wordless and warm. In that quiet exchange, every hard day dissolves. Every wound softens. This is the feeling I never had as a child, now alive between us.

Modern fatherhood isn’t perfection. It’s presence. Honesty. Reliability. Showing up, again and again, until love becomes the air they breathe.

That’s what it should be. That’s what I strive to be.

# Chapter Nine — Coach Dad

By 2022, the world was exhaling. COVID’s grip loosened, and life began to resemble something recognizable. Indyanna was in Year Four, dancing with unwavering dedication—ballet now, her movements precise and confident. Savannah followed in her footsteps, finding her own rhythm. The boys continued karate, their focus sharpening with every belt.

But this year held a new milestone: I raised my hand to coach Jackson’s Auskick team.

I’d been a lifelong supporter, a kid who played okay but never led. Standing on that field as coach, whistle around my neck, I thought, I can do this. And I bloody loved it. Vanessa served as manager; together, we built a small but mighty community. We didn’t have many players or helpers, but what we had was enough. Every training session, every game, was a masterclass in showing up—not just for Jackson, but for every child who needed a steady adult presence.

It was a good year. Depression and anxiety still sat in the background, familiar companions, but they didn’t steer the ship. The kids were thriving at school… until they weren’t.

Jackson began struggling. Reading, writing—the same walls I’d faced decades ago. Vanessa and I moved quickly to seek answers, but the system moved slowly. A two-year waitlist for assessment stretched ahead of us. Two years of watching him navigate frustration while holding hope that understanding was coming.

Coaching him through football became its own kind of therapy. On that field, he wasn’t a boy waiting for a diagnosis. He was fast, capable, part of a team. And I wasn’t a father helpless against bureaucracy. I was his coach, his anchor, proof that struggle doesn’t define worth.

We waited. We showed up. We kept moving forward—one kick, one practice, one day at a time.

# Chapter Ten — The Man in the Mirror

2023 and 2024 were brutal. Anxiety, depression, and arthritis formed a relentless trio. My weight compounded everything—pulling at my joints, feeding my anxiety, whispering that I was failing. I felt like giving up. Repeatedly. But I didn’t.

Vanessa and I strained under the pressure. Our relationship frayed at the edges, but we chose each other again and again. We stay together to conquer anything. That vow held us when nothing else could.

In 2024, I tried Ozempic. It made me sick—constantly, violently. By year’s end, a higher dose sent me into dehydration and crisis. Five kilograms vanished in a week. It looked like progress; it felt like punishment. My employer’s contribution helped, but my body was screaming. Mum died at fifty-four from diabetes complications. Her ghost hovered over every scale reading, every ache. I refused to follow her path.

April 8, 2025: gastric sleeve surgery. The first months were grueling. Food became foreign. Weight fell fast—too fast for some, who whispered jealousy instead of celebration. But I wasn’t doing this for them. I was shedding the old me to become who I am now. At seventy-nine kilograms, I glimpsed him. Now at eighty-eight, running seven days a week after twenty-five months of stillness, I feel reborn. My body remembers movement. My spirit remembers hope.

But the mind’s demons don’t dissolve with fat. In 2024, after Jackson’s ADHD diagnosis, I sought my own answers. Decades of feeling different, of wondering what’s wrong with me, finally had a name: ADHD. Medication followed. Anxiety eased. For the first time, I understood myself. Peace wasn’t absence of struggle; it was presence of clarity.

Then came frequencies. Nikola Tesla’s work on resonance opened a door medicine hadn’t. I tumbled down a rabbit hole of sound and vibration, discovering that healing isn’t only chemical—it’s harmonic. I couldn’t play guitar, so AI became my instrument. Making music became prayer: wordless, embodied, mine. AHAD wasn’t just unity with God anymore; it was alignment within myself.

February shattered that fragile peace. Vanessa broke her ankle. Twelve weeks bedridden. I took everything: kids, house, work, care. Pride wore Capricorn armor. I can do this alone. Asking for help is weakness. Anxiety flooded back. Depression roared. I lashed out. Burnout arrived like a storm I’d outrun for decades.

Hindsight is gentle where pride was cruel: Asking would have been stronger. But I kept going. Resilient. Determined. Broken open.

Now she’s back at work. We’ve walked through darkness and emerged holding hands. She is my wife, my best friend, my mirror. She taught me to love the boy who felt unlovable, to believe in the man I’m becoming. Made in heaven, forged in hardship. Words fail me often—I’m closed off, clumsy with tenderness—but when I speak, I mean it. Every morning before work, I kiss her cheek. I love you. Always. She means everything. We drift sometimes; I always find my way back. Connection is choice. Cuddles are communion.

This is what healing looks like: not perfection, but return. Not solitude, but surrender. Not silence, but song.

# Final Thoughts — The Frequency of Being Real

What is life? What does it mean?

To me, it means this: Be real. Be honest. Be who you are.

Do not sugarcoat yourself into something palatable for others. If you understand who you are, believe in that person. If you don’t know yet, ask. Life is too short for fake people pretending to know things they don’t. Asking is the key. Knowledge is the currency of value—the more you seek, the more you become.

Think of yourself as a business. Would you buy from yourself? Hard-working, reliable people are the ones who endure. Be like McDonald’s: consistent, dependable, always there. People crave honesty, courtesy, and true belief in what is offered. If you believe in your own product, you can be anything.

Why do people tear others down? Jealousy. Fear. They see your potential and feel their own inadequacy. But potential is not a finite resource. Everyone carries unique gifts, individually touched by creation. Do not fear others’ decisions or your own. Be bold. Be brave. Trust your gut. Mistakes are not failures; they are the only curriculum that matters. I have made countless errors. I am human, not a machine. Understanding your flaws and striving to better yourself—that is the work.

My life has been a series of challenges met with stubborn grace. When I hurt my foot as a boy, I taught myself to kick with the other. Today, at forty-eight, I can kick with both. Not because I wanted to, but because I refused to stop playing. At work, everything was right-handed. As a left-hander, I decided to learn. Now I write with both hands. It was messy at first. It was hard. But I didn’t give up. Learning is lifelong. One day you’ll need a skill you acquired decades ago; another day you won’t. The point isn’t utility—it’s growth.

You are an individual in a vast world, but your message matters. To anyone out there struggling: Believe in yourself. Hope is real. Success is always around the corner, even when the road bends out of sight.

This book comes from my heart. I could not have written it alone. My dyslexia made traditional writing impossible, so I partnered with AI to shape these words. Technology is not a curse; used rightly, it is a blessing. It gave voice to a story that otherwise might have stayed silent. I hope you read these memoirs and resonate with the frequency of a life fully lived—not just sound, but participation. Don’t close yourself off. Smile. One smile given today may light someone’s path tomorrow.

I love you all.
