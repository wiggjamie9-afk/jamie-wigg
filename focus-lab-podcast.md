## Podcast Positioning

**Focus Lab** is a productivity podcast for the modern, over-stimulated brain. Each 12-minute episode takes one real concept from cognitive science and turns it into a system you can run *today* — no shame, no hustle-bro nonsense, no "just try harder." Built for people whose brains crave novelty and resist boredom, including those with ADHD traits.

The premise: your focus problem is not a character flaw. It's a mismatch between a Stone-Age reward system and a world engineered to hijack it. Once you understand the mechanism, you can build around it.

**Why it works:**
- Focus and "I can't concentrate anymore" is one of the most-searched anxieties of the decade — evergreen and growing.
- The ADHD-aware angle is underserved by generic productivity content that assumes a neurotypical brain.
- Listeners build a *habit loop* (twice-weekly, short, actionable) — exactly the retention pattern advertisers pay for.
- Premium-fit sponsors are abundant and high-LTV: focus/task apps, note tools, courses, and (handled carefully) supplement brands.
- Short + practical = high completion rate = strong ad performance.

**Tone:** energizing, practical, deeply empathetic. We talk to the listener like a smart friend who has been in the overwhelm and found a few real handholds. We celebrate small wins. We never moralize.

**Important framing rule:** ADHD is discussed as *traits, tendencies, and strategies* — never diagnosis or treatment. We do not make medical claims. We point people toward qualified professionals for anything clinical, and we say so on the record.

### **EPISODE 1: "Why Your Brain Craves Distraction (The Dopamine Trap)"**
**Air Date:** Today (Day 1)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: Bright, curious 4-second synth motif, then fade under]

HOST: Welcome to Focus Lab — the show for brains that were not built for browser tabs. I'm your host, and in the next twelve minutes we're going to do one thing: make your distractible brain make sense. Because once it makes sense, you stop fighting it and start working with it.

[MUSIC OUT]

So let's start with the moment you know too well. You sit down to do the one important thing. You open the document. And before your fingers even touch the keys, your hand has already reached for your phone. You didn't decide to. It just... happened.

Here's the first thing I want you to hear, and I mean it: that is not laziness. That is not a lack of willpower. That is a 200,000-year-old reward system doing exactly what it evolved to do — in an environment it was never designed for.

[THE SCIENCE]

Let's talk about dopamine, because almost everything you've heard about it is slightly wrong. People call it the "pleasure chemical." It's not, really. Neuroscientists like Wolfram Schultz showed back in the 1990s that dopamine isn't about the reward itself — it's about the *anticipation* of reward. It spikes *before* you get the thing, not after. Dopamine is the chemical of *wanting*, not *liking*.

That distinction changes everything. Your phone doesn't give you pleasure. It gives you the *promise* of pleasure — a maybe. A pull-to-refresh. A notification that might be something good. And here's the cruel part: psychologist B.F. Skinner figured out decades ago that the most addictive reward schedule isn't a reliable one. It's a *variable* one. A slot machine. You pull the lever and *sometimes* you win. That unpredictability is what hooks the brain hardest.

Your social feed is a slot machine. Your inbox is a slot machine. And every time you check and there's nothing good, your brain doesn't learn to stop — it learns to check *more*, because the payoff is right around the corner. It always feels right around the corner.

Meanwhile, the important task in front of you — the writing, the studying, the deep work — offers a reward that's distant, uncertain, and abstract. Your brain compares "tiny effort, instant maybe-reward" against "large effort, far-away maybe-reward," and it picks the slot machine every single time. It's not betraying you. It's doing math.

If your brain leans toward ADHD traits, this contrast is turned up even louder. Researchers describe ADHD-style brains as having a stronger pull toward what's *interesting, novel, urgent, or challenging* in the moment — and a weaker grip on things that are merely *important later*. So if you've ever felt like boredom is physically painful, you're not imagining it. Your reward system is hungrier for stimulation, and a blank document is the opposite of stimulation.

[THE PRACTICAL SYSTEM]

Okay. Mechanism understood. Now — what do we actually do? Three moves you can run today.

Move one: raise the friction on the slot machine. You will not out-willpower a variable reward. So don't try. Make the distraction slightly harder to reach than the work. Log out of the app on your computer. Move it off your home screen into a folder on the last page. Put the phone in another room — not face-down on the desk, *another room*. Every second of friction you add is a second where your rational brain can catch up to your impulsive one. You're not banning the slot machine. You're just moving it across the street.

Move two: give the boring task a dopamine hit it doesn't have on its own. Your brain wants novelty and a near-term payoff, so build them in. Set a timer for fifteen minutes and make it a game: how much can I get done before it goes off? Put on a specific album you only play while working. Promise yourself a real, small reward when the block ends. You are manufacturing the anticipation that the task was missing.

Move three — and this is the one people skip — schedule the slot machine instead of resisting it. Tell yourself: I get ten minutes of the feed at 12:30. Knowing the reward is *coming* quiets the craving now. Total denial backfires; a scheduled appointment with your phone works with the wanting system instead of against it.

[CLOSING + CTA]

So here's the whole episode in one sentence: your brain isn't broken — it's being out-engineered. And the fix isn't more discipline. It's better design.

This week, pick just one move. I'd start with the phone in another room — it's the highest-leverage thing most people never do. Try it for one work session and notice what changes.

If this reframed something for you, follow Focus Lab wherever you're listening, because next episode we go from defense to offense: how to build a block of deep, real, phone-free focus using time-blocking — even if you've never managed more than ten minutes in your life. That's Friday. I'll see you in the Lab.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Key Concepts (cited):**
- Dopamine as anticipation/"wanting," not pleasure — Wolfram Schultz reward-prediction research (1990s)
- Variable-ratio reinforcement — B.F. Skinner
- ADHD-trait reward sensitivity to novelty/urgency (interest-driven attention) — framed as traits, not diagnosis
- **Listener takeaway:** "You're being out-engineered, not weak." One action: phone in another room.

### **EPISODE 2: "Deep Work and Time-Blocking (How to Build a Focus Container)"**
**Air Date:** Day 4 (Friday)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds, fade under]

HOST: Welcome back to Focus Lab. Last episode we played defense — we got the slot machine out of arm's reach. Today we play offense. Today we build the thing almost nobody actually has anymore: a container for deep, uninterrupted focus. Twelve minutes. Let's go.

[MUSIC OUT]

[THE HOOK]

Quick gut check. When was the last time you did one single task — no tab-switching, no glancing at your phone, no "let me just quickly check" — for a solid forty-five minutes? For a lot of us, the honest answer is "I genuinely can't remember." And we've started to believe that's just who we are now. It isn't. It's a skill we stopped practicing.

[THE SCIENCE]

There's a term for the high-value version of this: *deep work*. Computer scientist Cal Newport defines it as cognitively demanding work performed in a state of distraction-free concentration — the kind that creates real value and is genuinely hard to replicate. The opposite is *shallow work*: email, Slack, busywork, the logistical noise that fills a day and leaves you exhausted but with nothing to show.

Why does deep work matter so much more than it used to? Because of a tax most people don't know they're paying, called *attention residue*. Researcher Sophie Leroy found that when you switch from one task to another, a chunk of your attention stays stuck on the first task. So if you check email "for two seconds" mid-project, the cost isn't two seconds. It's the two seconds plus the several minutes it takes your mind to fully reload the original task. Do that twenty times a day — which is normal now — and you've spent your whole day re-loading and never actually arriving.

Multitasking, by the way, is mostly a myth. What we call multitasking is really fast task-*switching*, and every switch pays that residue tax. You don't do two things at once. You do two things badly, one at a time, while feeling busy.

[THE PRACTICAL SYSTEM]

So the goal is simple to say and hard to do: fewer, longer, protected blocks of single-tasking. Here's how to build one.

Step one: time-block your calendar, don't to-do-list your day. A to-do list is a pile of wishes with no home. Time-blocking means you give each task an actual appointment: "9 to 10:30, draft the report." When a task lives on the calendar, it stops being optional background anxiety and becomes a real event. Start with *one* block tomorrow. One. Don't blueprint your whole life.

Step two — and this is for the distractible brains especially — make the block shorter than you think you need. The classic advice says ninety minutes. Ignore that for now. If a long block feels impossible, you're right, so don't start there. Start with twenty-five minutes of true focus and a five-minute break. That's the Pomodoro Technique, from Francesco Cirillo. The genius of it isn't the tomato timer — it's that twenty-five minutes is short enough that your brain agrees to start, and starting is ninety percent of the battle.

Step three: define "done" *before* you start the block. Vague tasks are focus-killers, because every few minutes your brain has to re-decide what it's even doing — and that's an opening for distraction. So instead of "work on presentation," write "build slides three through six." Concrete, finishable, obvious. When you know exactly what the target is, your brain stops negotiating and starts working.

Step four: protect the container like it's a meeting with your most important client — because it is. Phone in the other room, like we said. Notifications off. One tab. And tell the people around you, out loud if you can: "I'm heads-down until ten-thirty." A protected block isn't selfish. It's the only way the important stuff ever gets done.

And one permission slip, because I know some of you need it: you will get distracted *inside* the block. Your mind will wander. That's not failure — that is literally what brains do. The skill isn't never wandering. The skill is *noticing* you wandered and gently coming back, without the spiral of "see, I knew I couldn't do this." Coming back is the rep. Every return is a bicep curl for your attention.

[CLOSING + CTA]

Here's the whole thing in a sentence: stop trying to focus all day, and start protecting one real block. One appointment, one clear target, one closed door, twenty-five honest minutes.

Your assignment before next episode: put exactly one focus block on tomorrow's calendar. Give it a finishable target. Defend it. That's it. You're not rebuilding your life. You're proving to yourself you can hold attention for twenty-five minutes — and you can.

Next episode, we go after the monster under every productivity bed: procrastination. Why you *know* what to do and still don't do it, and the embarrassingly small trick that breaks the freeze. Follow the show so it lands in your feed. See you in the Lab.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Key Concepts (cited):**
- *Deep Work* vs. shallow work — Cal Newport
- Attention residue — Sophie Leroy ("Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work?")
- Task-switching cost / multitasking myth
- Pomodoro Technique — Francesco Cirillo
- Time-blocking vs. open to-do lists; "define done before you start"
- **Listener takeaway:** Protect one block, with one finishable target. Returning from a wander *is* the skill.

### **EPISODE 3: "Beating Procrastination (The 2-Minute Rule and the Real Reason You Freeze)"**
**Air Date:** Day 8 (Tuesday)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds, fade under]

HOST: This is Focus Lab. Today: procrastination. The thing you're maybe doing *right now* by listening to a podcast about not procrastinating. No judgment — I see you, and honestly, same. Twelve minutes to understand why you freeze, and how to unfreeze. Let's go.

[MUSIC OUT]

[THE HOOK]

Let's kill the biggest myth first, because it's the one that keeps you stuck. Procrastination is not a time-management problem. And it is *definitely* not a sign that you're lazy. People who procrastinate often work incredibly hard — just frantically, at the last second, in a panic. That's not laziness. That's something else entirely.

[THE SCIENCE]

Here's what the research actually says. Procrastination is an *emotion*-management problem. Dr. Tim Pychyl and Dr. Fuschia Sirois, who study this for a living, describe procrastination as a strategy for avoiding negative feelings tied to a task — boredom, anxiety, self-doubt, the fear it won't be good enough. When a task makes you feel bad, putting it off gives you instant relief. And that relief feels *amazing*. So your brain files away the lesson: avoiding works. Which guarantees you'll do it again.

It's the same reward loop from episode one. Avoidance is the immediate payoff. The cost comes later — and "later you" is a stranger your brain doesn't care much about. UCLA's Hal Hershfield has shown that we relate to our future selves almost like a different *person*. That's why "future me will handle it" feels so reasonable in the moment, and so brutal at 11 p.m. the night before the deadline.

For ADHD-leaning brains, add one more layer. There's a wall that shows up specifically around *starting* — the task feels enormous and shapeless, and you can't find the first physical handhold. People sometimes call this "task paralysis." You're not refusing to start. You genuinely can't see *where* to start. Big difference, and it deserves a strategy, not a lecture.

[THE PRACTICAL SYSTEM]

So here's the toolkit. Four tools, smallest first.

Tool one, the famous one: the two-minute rule. There are two flavors and both are gold. James Clear's version: if something takes less than two minutes, do it *right now*, don't even write it down. Reply, file, rinse the cup — done. But the more powerful version is for the big scary tasks: shrink the task until starting takes two minutes or less. You're not writing the report. You're opening the document and typing one ugly sentence. You're not going for a run. You're putting on your shoes. The goal isn't to finish — it's to *start*, because starting is the actual wall. And here's the magic: once you're moving, momentum usually takes over, thanks to a quirk called the Zeigarnik effect — your brain hates leaving a started task unfinished. Starting is the cheat code.

Tool two: name the feeling out loud. Since procrastination is an emotion you're avoiding, name it. "I'm avoiding this because I'm scared it'll be bad." Just labeling it takes a surprising amount of its power away — and now you can answer it. A messy first draft beats a perfect document that doesn't exist. Done is the engine of better.

Tool three: forgive yourself for last time. This sounds soft; it's one of the most replicated findings in the field. Pychyl's team found that students who *forgave themselves* for procrastinating on one exam procrastinated *less* on the next one. The shame spiral — "I always do this, what's wrong with me" — is itself a bad feeling, which becomes one more thing you want to avoid, which makes you procrastinate more. Self-compassion isn't going easy on yourself. It's removing the fuel from the fire.

Tool four, for the task-paralysis wall: make the first step embarrassingly, almost insultingly small. So small it feels silly. Open the file. Write the title. Lay out the gym clothes. You are not trying to do the task. You are trying to lower the activation energy until your brain stops seeing a cliff and starts seeing a step.

[CLOSING + CTA]

Whole episode, one sentence: you don't procrastinate because you're lazy — you procrastinate to dodge a bad feeling, so the fix is to shrink the task until starting it isn't scary anymore.

Your move before next episode: pick the one thing you've been avoiding, and define its two-minute starting step. Not the task. The first two minutes. Then do *only* that. You're allowed to stop after. You probably won't want to.

Next episode is the one a lot of you have been waiting for: building focus when your brain runs on ADHD traits — working *with* the wiring instead of pretending it isn't there. Follow Focus Lab so you don't miss it. See you in the Lab.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Key Concepts (cited):**
- Procrastination as emotion regulation — Tim Pychyl, Fuschia Sirois
- Future-self discontinuity — Hal Hershfield
- Two-minute rule — James Clear (plus "shrink the task to start" variant)
- Zeigarnik effect (unfinished tasks stay active in mind)
- Self-forgiveness reduces future procrastination — Pychyl/Wohl student study
- Task paralysis / activation energy (framed as ADHD trait, not diagnosis)
- **Listener takeaway:** Define and do only the two-minute starting step. Drop the shame spiral.

### **EPISODE 4: "Building Focus When You Have ADHD Traits (Work With the Wiring)"**
**Air Date:** Day 11 (Friday)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds, fade under]

HOST: Welcome to Focus Lab. This one's personal for a lot of you. Today we talk about focus when your brain runs on ADHD traits — the restless, novelty-hungry, all-or-nothing kind of brain. Quick and important note before we start: I'm not a doctor, this is not medical advice, and nothing here is about diagnosis or treatment. If you think you might have ADHD, talk to a qualified professional — that's a real and worthwhile step. What we're doing here is sharing *strategies* for traits a lot of people share. Okay. Twelve minutes. Let's go.

[MUSIC OUT]

[THE HOOK]

Most productivity advice was written for a brain that gets a steady, reliable drip of motivation for things that are merely important. If your brain isn't like that — if it'll happily hyperfocus for six hours on something fascinating and then absolutely refuse to answer one two-line email for three weeks — then standard advice doesn't just fail you, it makes you feel broken. You're not broken. You've been handed the wrong manual.

[THE SCIENCE]

Here's a reframe that helps. Dr. William Dodson describes the ADHD-style brain as *interest-based* rather than *importance-based*. A neurotypical brain can run on importance, rewards, and consequences alone. An interest-based brain reliably engages with a different set of triggers — often summarized as novelty, interest, challenge, and urgency. Notice "importance" isn't on that list. That's why "but it's important" has never once made you do the thing. It's not a moral failing. It's a different ignition system.

Two more pieces. First, hyperfocus: the flip side of distractibility. The same brain that can't start the boring task can lock onto an interesting one so hard it forgets to eat. Focus isn't your problem — *directing* it is. Second, what researchers call executive function: the mental tools for planning, starting, organizing, and remembering to do things. When those run light, the issue is rarely *knowing* what to do. It's *initiating* and *sequencing* it. So the strategies that work aren't about trying harder — they're about outsourcing those functions to your environment.

[THE PRACTICAL SYSTEM]

Five strategies that work *with* this wiring instead of against it.

One: manufacture the missing ignition. If your brain runs on novelty, interest, challenge, and urgency — build those in on purpose. Turn the boring task into a race against a timer to create urgency. Gamify it: points, streaks, a visible progress bar. Change your location — a café, a different room — to add novelty. You're not being childish. You're supplying the fuel your engine actually burns.

Two: body-double. This is one of the most effective and least-known tools out there. You work alongside another person — in the room, or on a video call where you both just... work. It sounds like nothing. It works astonishingly well. The quiet presence of another person provides gentle accountability and helps the brain stay anchored. There are whole apps and communities built only for this. If starting alone is impossible, don't start alone.

Three: externalize everything. An interest-based brain should not be storing tasks, times, or steps inside its own head — that's the part that runs light, and "I'll remember" is where good intentions go to die. Get it out. One capture spot for every task. Alarms for transitions, not just deadlines. Sticky notes where you'll physically trip over them. Out of sight is genuinely out of mind, so make the important things impossible to miss.

Four: respect the all-or-nothing switch, and aim it. Many ADHD-style brains don't have a dimmer — they have an on/off switch. Fighting that is exhausting. Instead, use it: when hyperfocus shows up, clear the runway and *ride it* on something that matters. And protect against its downside — set a gentle alarm so you eat, drink water, and don't blow past every other commitment in the building.

Five — the most important one: drop the neurotypical scorecard. You are going to have uneven days. A wildly productive morning and a foggy afternoon is not a failure; it's the weather. The win isn't becoming a steady, even machine. The win is building a system flexible enough that a low-fuel day doesn't burn down everything you built. Consistency for this brain looks like *resilience*, not *sameness*.

[CLOSING + CTA]

One sentence: your brain runs on interest, not importance — so stop trying to force importance, and start engineering interest, urgency, and structure into the things that matter.

This week, try the body-double. Text one friend: "Can we hop on a silent call and both just work for thirty minutes?" Or join an online focus room. Notice how different starting feels when you're not starting alone.

And again — if any of this resonated *a lot*, please consider talking to a professional. Strategies are powerful, and so is real assessment. They're not in competition.

Next episode, we tackle the device that's the source of half this struggle: your phone. Digital minimalism, and how to tame the thing without throwing it in a lake. Follow the show. See you in the Lab.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds]
```

**Key Concepts (cited):**
- Interest-based vs. importance-based nervous system; novelty/interest/challenge/urgency triggers — William Dodson
- Hyperfocus as the flip side of distractibility
- Executive function (initiation/sequencing) — outsource to environment
- Body-doubling as an accountability/initiation tool
- Externalizing memory and transitions
- **Safety framing throughout:** traits + strategies only; explicit "not medical advice / see a professional" at open and close.
- **Listener takeaway:** Engineer interest and structure; try a body-double session this week.

### **EPISODE 5: "Taming Your Phone (Digital Minimalism Without the Monastery)"**
**Air Date:** Day 15 (Tuesday)

**Script (12 min):**

```
[INTRO MUSIC: 4 seconds, fade under]

HOST: Welcome to Focus Lab. Final episode of our pilot run, and we end with the boss fight: your phone. Not to demonize it — it's genuinely useful — but to take back the steering wheel. This is digital minimalism, and no, it does not require deleting everything and moving to a cabin. Twelve minutes. Let's go.

[MUSIC OUT]

[THE HOOK]

Let's start with a number that should bother you a little. Studies of phone use put the average person somewhere around four-plus hours a day, with dozens — often a hundred-plus — pickups. Now here's the unsettling part: most people, when asked, dramatically *underestimate* it. We're not choosing four hours. We're sleepwalking into them, thirty seconds at a time. The problem was never that you used your phone. It's that you stopped *deciding* to.

[THE SCIENCE]

Two ideas frame this. The first is *digital minimalism*, again from Cal Newport: a philosophy where you use technology intentionally, in service of things you actually value, and you're ruthless about cutting the rest. The key word is *intentional*. A minimalist isn't anti-tech. They've just decided their attention is too valuable to hand to whatever app screams loudest.

The second idea explains why this is so hard — and it's the most important sentence in this episode. These apps are not neutral tools you're failing to use wisely. They are products *engineered* by large teams to capture maximum attention. Former Google ethicist Tristan Harris calls it the race to the bottom of the brainstem — companies competing to exploit the oldest, most automatic parts of your brain. The infinite scroll, the autoplay, the red notification badge, the pull-to-refresh — none of those are accidents. They're slot-machine mechanics, the variable rewards from episode one, deployed on purpose. So if you've ever felt like you were losing a fight with your phone — you were. It's a fight between you and a building full of engineers optimizing against your willpower. Willpower is the wrong weapon. *Design* is the right one.

[THE PRACTICAL SYSTEM]

So we change the design. Five moves, easiest to hardest.

One: kill the slot-machine cues. Turn off all non-human notifications. If it's not an actual person trying to reach you, it does not get to interrupt your life — no badges, no banners, no buzzes from apps. This single change quietly removes most of the random pulls to check. Most people feel the difference within a day.

Two: add friction where it's frictionless. Log out of the apps that eat you, so opening them takes a password instead of a thumb-twitch. Delete the worst offenders off the phone entirely and use them only in a browser — clunky by design, which is the point. Move everything off your home screen. Every extra second of friction is a second your deciding brain gets to wake up.

Three: go grayscale. Set your screen to black and white. Those engineered colors and red badges are bait for your visual system; in grayscale, the slot machine gets boring fast. It's a tiny setting with a surprisingly large effect, and you can flip it on right now.

Four: build phone-free zones and times, not a phone-free life. Newport's research suggests bans are more sustainable than limits, but *targeted* bans. So: no phone in the bedroom — buy a five-dollar alarm clock and charge it in the kitchen. No phone for the first hour awake, so the world doesn't grab your attention before you do. No phone at the dinner table. You're not quitting. You're carving out a few protected spaces where you, not the app, are in charge.

Five — the mindset shift that makes the rest stick: replace, don't just remove. This is where most digital detoxes collapse. If you delete the scroll but leave a void, you'll be reinstalling it by Thursday, because the boredom underneath is still there. So decide *in advance* what the time is for. A book by the bed. A walk. A real hobby with your hands. Newport's whole point is that minimalism isn't about less for its own sake — it's about clearing junk to make room for things that genuinely matter to you. Reclaimed attention needs somewhere good to go.

[CLOSING + CTA]

One sentence to close the whole series: your focus isn't failing — it's being competed for by people far better resourced than your willpower, and the answer, every single time, is to change the design instead of blaming yourself.

Tonight, do one thing: charge your phone outside the bedroom. One night. Notice how you sleep, and notice the first hour of tomorrow.

That's our pilot run — five episodes, five systems. If Focus Lab helped you see your brain a little more kindly and run it a little more skillfully, follow the show, and share it with the one person in your life who needs to hear "you're not lazy, you're out-engineered." We're back twice a week with new tools. Thank you for spending twelve minutes building a better brain with me. I'll see you in the Lab.

[OUTRO MUSIC: 5 seconds]
```

**Key Concepts (cited):**
- *Digital Minimalism* — Cal Newport (intentional tech use; targeted bans > vague limits; "remove to make room")
- Attention economy / "race to the bottom of the brainstem" — Tristan Harris
- Engineered variable-reward mechanics (callback to Episode 1)
- Concrete interventions: notifications off, friction, grayscale, phone-free zones, replace-don't-remove
- **Listener takeaway:** Change the design, not the willpower. Tonight: phone charges outside the bedroom.
