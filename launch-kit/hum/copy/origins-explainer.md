# Where HUM came from

A standalone explainer. Readable as a newsletter, an "About this app" section, an in-app lineage card, or a video voiceover. Roughly 800 words.

---

## The hook

Humming is older than writing. It is also one of the most quietly well-studied things a body can do. Twenty-five centuries of named practice on one shelf, a hundred years of nervous-system research on the next shelf, and a phone-sized gap between them that nobody had bothered to fill. HUM is what fills it.

## Bhramari, c. 15th century — and the older root it grew from

The earliest unambiguous textual codification of humming as a named yogic practice is in the **Hatha Yoga Pradīpikā** of Swāmī Svātmārāma, a Sanskrit manual usually dated to the 15th century CE. Chapter 2, verse 68: *"Breathe in quickly, making a reverberating sound like the male black bee; exhale slowly while softly making the sound of the female black bee. By this yogic practice, one becomes Lord of the Yogis and the mind is absorbed in bliss."* That is Bhramari Pranayama — *brahmar*, Sanskrit for bee — and it is the practice the app's 220 Hz target tone is named after.

Bhramari did not appear from nowhere. Its conceptual root is the closing M of AUM — the held, nasal, humming resonance discussed in the **Mandukya Upanishad** around 500 BCE, where it is treated as the symbol of the four states of consciousness. By the time Svātmārāma codified Bhramari in the 15th century, humming-as-sacred-vocalisation had been part of South Asian practice for the better part of two thousand years. The Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā (17th century) lists it among the eight major kumbhakas and frames it as a *nāda* practice — a discipline of sound itself.

So the honest framing is: humming as a named, codified yogic technique is reliably medieval. The wider tradition of humming-as-practice that fed it is much older.

## The vagus nerve, the early 20th century, and why humming became more than a meditation prop

For most of those centuries, humming was treated as a meditative and devotional act. What it does to the autonomic nervous system was not something anyone could measure.

That changed in the early decades of the twentieth century, when the parasympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system — the brake to the sympathetic accelerator — was first characterised in detail, and the vagus nerve was identified as its chief conduit. Once researchers could measure parasympathetic tone in the lab, anything that reliably activated it — slow exhalation, vocalisation, vibration in the throat and chest — became, in principle, a candidate intervention.

Humming sits exactly on top of three of those levers at once: it is a slow exhalation, it is a vocalisation, and it vibrates the larynx and pharynx whose tissues are innervated by branches of the vagus. The clinical observation that singing, humming, and gargling raise heart-rate variability is now consistent across the literature. (The polyvagal *theoretical* scaffolding around this is contested in autonomic neuroscience, but the HRV signal itself is robust — the app leans on the observation, not the theory.)

## The sinus-resonance findings, 2002 onward

The single most influential modern humming paper is **Weitzberg and Lundberg's 2002 study at the Karolinska Institute**. Ten healthy subjects, chemiluminescence measurement of nasal nitric oxide during quiet exhalation versus humming. Humming produced a **fifteen-fold increase** in nasal NO. Their two-compartment nose-sinus model showed why: oscillating airflow at vocal-fold frequencies dramatically increases gas exchange between the sinus cavities and the nasal cavity.

Nitric oxide had won the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physiology for its role as a vasodilator and signalling molecule. Independent of that, NO is broadly antimicrobial in the sinuses. Weitzberg and Lundberg's paper meant that something humans had been practising for thousands of years quietly amounted to a sinus-ventilation and NO-release intervention. **Maniscalco et al. (2003)** replicated the effect and validated humming-induced NO as a clinical assay of sinus patency, identifying around 130 Hz as the frequency that releases the most. **Eby (2006)** published a single-case report in *Medical Hypotheses* in which strong nasal humming, four times daily, terminated his chronic rhinosinusitis in four days. A case report is not a trial — but the mechanism it relies on is the same one Weitzberg and Lundberg measured directly.

More recently, **Trivedi et al. (2023)** put 23 participants on clinical-grade Holter monitors across humming, physical activity, emotional stress, and sleep. Humming produced the lowest stress index of any state measured — lower than sleep itself.

## Why an app, now

The category around humming sells the *end-state* — vagal tone, parasympathetic shift — through hardware. A prescription cervical vagus-nerve stimulator runs around US$600 a month. A consumer wellness collar with the same underlying signal is around US$500. Nobody had built the obvious thing on the other side of that gap: the app that listens to *you* humming and gets out of the way.

HUM is that app. The pitch detection is on-device. The tone is a sine wave. The breath orb is a 0.1 Hz cycle. There is no AI in the practice loop and no recording leaves your phone. The maker is one Australian developer building it on an iPhone, and the price is AU$30, once, for life.

Five thousand years of practice, one hundred years of science, one app.

HUM is the contemplative sister of the wider RHYTHMIX studio ecosystem, and over time the two will share a common practice spine while keeping their own voices. The first hum is the hardest. After that, it's just the practice.
