# THE STOIC MINUTE — Podcast Specification

> **Working title:** *The Stoic Minute* (alt: *Stillpoint*)
> **Brand-conflict note:** "The Daily Stoic" is an established brand owned by author Ryan Holiday (book, daily email, podcast, store). To avoid trademark and audience confusion, this show must **not** launch as "The Daily Stoic" or any close variant. *The Stoic Minute* and *Stillpoint* are the recommended working titles; clear either with a trademark search before launch.

**Status:** READY FOR PILOT (5 episodes scripted)
**Frequency:** Daily (every morning, 7 days/week)
**Episode Length:** 5-8 minutes (short by design — a single calm dose)
**Format:** Solo TTS narration (one steady voice)
**Target Audience:** Self-improvement-minded adults, 22-55, who want a short, grounded moment of perspective to start or reset the day
**Revenue Target:** ~$6-9k/mo by Month 6 (projection — see assumptions below)

---

## Show Positioning

*The Stoic Minute* is a short daily practice, not a lecture. Each morning, one Stoic idea — drawn from Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, or Seneca — gets unpacked in plain language, applied to an ordinary modern moment, and handed back to the listener as something they can actually carry into the day.

The format is deliberately small. No interviews, no rambling, no hustle-culture noise. A quote, a real-life application, a short reflection, and a closing line you can hold onto. Five to eight minutes, and you're done.

**Why it works:**
- **Habit-friendly.** A 5-8 minute daily show fits the morning coffee, the commute, the walk. Short shows finish — and shows that finish get re-opened tomorrow.
- **Evergreen source material.** Stoicism is ~2,000 years of public-domain wisdom. The well never runs dry and never goes out of date.
- **A calm, growing audience.** Interest in Stoicism, journaling, and "calm productivity" has been climbing for a decade. The niche is large but underserved by *short, daily, non-preachy* audio.
- **Naturally aligned sponsors.** Journals, focus and meditation apps, books, and good coffee map onto the audience's actual habits — no awkward fit.
- **Low production cost.** Solo TTS narration, public-domain texts, no guests, no licensing. High margin per episode.

**Tone guardrails:** Calm, grounded, wise but never preachy. We don't shout. We don't sell relentless optimization. We never claim Stoicism makes you emotionless — it doesn't, and saying so misreads the sources. We quote accurately and keep the listener's autonomy intact: here's the idea, here's one way to use it, the rest is yours.

### EPISODE 1 — "Control What You Can"
**Theme:** The dichotomy of control
**Air Date:** Day 1
**Runtime:** ~6 min

**Script:**

```
[SOFT INTRO TONE: 3 seconds, single sustained note]

Good morning. This is The Stoic Minute. A short, steady moment before the day picks up speed.

Today, one of the oldest and most useful ideas the Stoics left us. Epictetus opens his Handbook — the Enchiridion — with it. He writes:

"Some things are within our power, and some things are not. Within our power are our own opinions, desires, and actions. Not within our power are our bodies, our reputations, what other people do."

[pause]

That's the whole hinge of Stoic practice. The dichotomy of control. Some things answer to you. Most things don't.

Notice what Epictetus puts in your column: your judgments, your choices, your effort. And notice what he leaves out: the weather, the traffic, the email you're dreading, the opinion someone three desks over has of you. None of that is yours to command. You can influence some of it. You can't control it.

[pause]

Here's the ordinary version. You send a careful message — to a colleague, a friend, someone you care about. Then you wait. And the reply doesn't come. An hour. A morning. A day.

You can feel the mind start to spin. Did I say the wrong thing? Are they angry? Should I send another?

The Stoic move is quiet and almost boring: the message was yours. You wrote it with care — that part was in your power, and you did it well. The reply is not yours. It was never going to be. Their silence belongs to them, to their day, to a hundred things you can't see.

So you set it down. Not coldly. Just honestly. You did your part. The rest isn't yours to carry.

[pause]

Reflection for today. Sometime this morning you'll feel that familiar tightening — the urge to control an outcome that was never in your hands. When you feel it, try asking one plain question: Is this mine, or not mine?

If it's yours — your effort, your attention, your response — give it everything. If it's not yours, loosen your grip. You'll find you were holding the wrong end of the rope.

[pause]

The freedom Epictetus is offering isn't the freedom to control the world. It's the freedom from needing to.

That's The Stoic Minute. Spend well the part of the day that is yours. I'll see you tomorrow.

[OUTRO TONE: 3 seconds]
```

**Source notes (show notes):** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* / *Handbook*, ch. 1. The dichotomy of control is the foundational Stoic distinction between *ta eph' hēmin* (what is up to us) and *ta ouk eph' hēmin* (what is not).

### EPISODE 2 — "Remember You Will Die"
**Theme:** Memento mori
**Air Date:** Day 2
**Runtime:** ~6-7 min

**Script:**

```
[SOFT INTRO TONE]

Good morning. This is The Stoic Minute.

Today's idea sounds heavy, but the Stoics meant it as a gift. Two Latin words: memento mori. Remember that you will die.

Marcus Aurelius, writing privately to himself, put it plainly:

"You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think."

[pause]

He wasn't being morbid. Marcus was the most powerful man in the Roman world, and this is what he reminded himself of, alone, at night — not to frighten himself, but to clarify. The thought of death is a knife that cuts away what doesn't matter.

The point isn't to dread the end. The point is that you are alive now, and "now" is not unlimited. Seneca made the same case in his letters: it isn't that we have a short time to live, he said, but that we waste much of it. Life is long enough if you use it.

[pause]

Here's the ordinary version. Think about how you spend a free evening. How much of it goes to scrolling something you won't remember tomorrow? How much goes to a small resentment you keep rehearsing? How much to worrying about a conversation that hasn't happened?

Now hold that next to Marcus's line: you could leave life right now.

Suddenly the grudge looks expensive. The scroll looks like borrowed time. The call to someone you love looks urgent in a good way.

Memento mori doesn't make the evening dark. It makes it precious. It's the reason you pick up the phone instead of putting it off again.

[pause]

Reflection for today. You don't need to think about death all day — the Stoics weren't asking for that. Just once, this morning, let the fact of your own finiteness sharpen one decision. Ask: if my time is genuinely limited — and it is — does this deserve a piece of it?

Some things will pass that test easily. A walk. Real attention to someone in front of you. Work you actually care about. Other things won't survive the question at all. Let them go.

[pause]

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.

That's The Stoic Minute. Don't postpone your life. I'll see you tomorrow.

[OUTRO TONE]
```

**Source notes:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book 2.11 ("You could leave life right now...") and the recurring memento mori theme throughout the *Meditations*. Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life* (*De Brevitate Vitae*): "It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it."

### EPISODE 3 — "The Obstacle Is the Way"
**Theme:** Turning obstacles into fuel
**Air Date:** Day 3
**Runtime:** ~6-7 min

**Script:**

```
[SOFT INTRO TONE]

Good morning. This is The Stoic Minute.

Today, a line from Marcus Aurelius that has quietly become a kind of modern slogan — and it's worth returning to the original. In the Meditations, Marcus writes:

"The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way."

[pause]

Read it slowly. The thing blocking you doesn't just get in the way of the work — Marcus says it can become the work. The obstacle isn't the detour. Sometimes the obstacle is the road.

This is one of Stoicism's most practical claims. The Stoics believed that virtue — acting with wisdom, courage, justice, and self-control — can be practiced through anything that happens to you. So an obstacle isn't only a problem to be removed. It's raw material. A delay is a chance to practice patience. An unfair situation is a chance to practice justice. A setback is a chance to practice resolve.

The fire doesn't fear the fuel you throw on it. It uses it.

[pause]

Here's the ordinary version. A project you'd planned gets blocked — a door you expected to be open is shut. The person who could approve it is unreachable. The tool you needed breaks. Your first reaction is the natural one: frustration. This is in my way.

The Stoic asks a different question. Not "how do I get rid of this," but "what does this obstacle make possible?" The blocked door forces you to learn the thing you'd been avoiding. The delay gives you the time to do the work properly instead of fast. The breakdown shows you a dependency you didn't know you had.

You're not pretending the obstacle is pleasant. You're refusing to let it be wasted.

[pause]

Reflection for today. Pick the thing that's genuinely in your way right now — the real one, the one you keep running into. Instead of asking how to remove it, ask: what is this teaching, or forcing, or revealing? What virtue does this specific obstacle call for — patience, courage, honesty, restraint?

You may not love the answer. But you'll stop being merely stuck. The block becomes a move.

[pause]

What stands in the way becomes the way. Nothing that happens to you today has to be wasted — not even the parts you didn't choose.

That's The Stoic Minute. Use what's in front of you. I'll see you tomorrow.

[OUTRO TONE]
```

**Source notes:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book 5.20 — commonly translated "The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way." This passage inspired the title of Ryan Holiday's book *The Obstacle Is the Way*; the idea itself is original to Marcus and in the public domain.

### EPISODE 4 — "Imagine Losing It"
**Theme:** Negative visualization (premeditatio malorum)
**Air Date:** Day 4
**Runtime:** ~6-7 min

**Script:**

```
[SOFT INTRO TONE]

Good morning. This is The Stoic Minute.

Today, a Stoic practice with an intimidating name and a gentle effect. The Latin is premeditatio malorum — the premeditation of adversity. We can just call it negative visualization. And done right, it doesn't make you anxious. It makes you grateful.

Seneca described the practice in his letters. He advised rehearsing misfortune in advance — picturing exile, loss, hardship — not to dread them, but so that no blow ever lands on a mind that's entirely unprepared. "The unexpected blows the heaviest," he wrote.

And Epictetus pushed it further, into the things we love most. He suggested that when you kiss your child goodnight, you whisper to yourself that they are mortal — that this is something on loan, not something owned. It sounds harsh. It's the opposite. He's trying to make you actually see the person while they're here.

[pause]

Here's the ordinary version. Most of what's good in your life has quietly become invisible. Your morning coffee is just routine now. Your home is just where you keep your things. The people you live with are just... there. We adapt to our blessings until we stop seeing them at all. The Stoics called this getting used to good fortune. The hedonic treadmill, a modern psychologist would say.

Negative visualization breaks the spell. For a moment — just a moment — you imagine the coffee gone, the home gone, the person gone. Not to torment yourself. To wake up. Because the instant you picture losing something, you see it again. Clearly. As if for the first time.

The cup of coffee you imagined losing is suddenly, genuinely good.

[pause]

Reflection for today. Take one ordinary thing you'd normally walk straight past this morning — your health, a person, a roof, a quiet ten minutes. Just briefly, picture life without it. Let the small ache of that imagined loss arrive. Then come back.

Notice what happened. The thing didn't change. You did. It's no longer background. It's a gift you currently, actually have.

[pause]

Negative visualization is gratitude with a sharper edge. It doesn't ask you to fear loss. It asks you to stop sleepwalking past everything you'd grieve if it were gone.

That's The Stoic Minute. Look twice at what you already have. I'll see you tomorrow.

[OUTRO TONE]
```

**Source notes:** Seneca, *Letters to Lucilius* (esp. on rehearsing misfortune; "the unexpected blows the heaviest" paraphrases his counsel in *Letters* 91 and 18). Epictetus, *Discourses* / *Enchiridion* 3 — the counsel to remember a loved one is mortal ("say to yourself that you are kissing a mortal"). The modern framing of "negative visualization" is drawn from William B. Irvine, *A Guide to the Good Life* (2009).

### EPISODE 5 — "Love Your Fate"
**Theme:** Amor fati
**Air Date:** Day 5
**Runtime:** ~6-7 min

**Script:**

```
[SOFT INTRO TONE]

Good morning. This is The Stoic Minute.

We'll close this first week with the idea the Stoics built toward — the hardest and, maybe, the most freeing of all. Amor fati. Love of fate.

It's not strictly a Stoic phrase; the words come later, from Nietzsche, who admired the Stoics deeply. But the practice is pure Marcus Aurelius. In the Meditations he writes:

"Accept the things to which fate binds you, and love the people with whom fate brings you together — but do so with all your heart."

[pause]

There's a ladder hidden inside Stoic practice, and amor fati is the top rung. The first rung is endurance — you put up with what happens. The next is acceptance — you stop fighting what you can't change. But amor fati asks for something more than acceptance. It asks you to want it. To meet what actually happened as if you had chosen it.

That's a steep ask. The Stoics knew it. They didn't mean you should pretend pain is pleasant or call injustice good. They meant something subtler: that the only life available to you is the one that's actually unfolding — and you can spend it resenting reality, or you can spend it working with reality. One of those wastes the day. The other doesn't.

[pause]

Here's the ordinary version. Something today will not go the way you wanted. A plan collapses. News arrives that you didn't order. The day insists on being different from the day you scheduled.

The ordinary response is resistance — a running argument with what already happened. It should have gone differently. This isn't fair. The argument is exhausting, and you always lose, because the past doesn't negotiate.

Amor fati ends the argument. Not by giving up — by turning toward the real situation and asking: all right. This is what's actually here. What's the best, truest thing I can do with it? You stop wishing for a different hand and start playing the one you were dealt — fully, even gladly.

[pause]

Reflection for today. When the day does its inevitable swerve, catch yourself in the argument with reality. Then try the harder, lighter move: not "this shouldn't be happening," but "this is happening — so let it be for the best, and let me make it so."

You don't have to feel it perfectly. You just have to stop fighting the one life you've actually got.

[pause]

Don't merely bear what is necessary, Marcus said elsewhere — love it. That's the whole journey, in two words. Amor fati.

That's The Stoic Minute, and the close of our first week. Whatever comes today — meet it well. I'll see you tomorrow.

[OUTRO TONE]
```

**Source notes:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book 7.57 ("Love only what happens... ") and Book 6.39 ("love the people with whom fate brings you together"). The phrase *amor fati* is Nietzsche's (*The Gay Science*, 1882; *Ecce Homo*), used here to name a recognizably Stoic stance. "Don't merely bear... love it" paraphrases Marcus, *Meditations* 10.

## Show Format (Every Episode)

**Structure (5-8 minutes):**
- **Cold open / greeting (15-20 sec)** — "Good morning. This is The Stoic Minute." A single calm line that sets the tone.
- **The idea + the quote (1.5-2 min)** — One Stoic source, accurately quoted and attributed, unpacked in plain language.
- **Real-life application (1.5-2 min)** — One concrete, ordinary modern situation where the idea actually bites.
- **Reflection / the practice (1-2 min)** — A small, doable thing the listener can carry into today. Phrased as an invitation, never a command.
- **Closing line (15-30 sec)** — One memorable sentence the listener keeps, plus a quiet sign-off and tomorrow's promise.

**Production notes:**
- Solo TTS narration, one consistent warm/steady voice. Unhurried pacing; honor the `[pause]` beats with real silence.
- A single soft intro/outro tone (3 sec). No dramatic stings — this is a calm show.
- Optional very low ambient pad under narration; keep it nearly subliminal.
- Always quote sources accurately and attribute in show notes. Never invent or "improve" a quotation, and never imply Stoicism means suppressing emotion.

## Sponsor Strategy

Ad reads are **calm and honest** — one short read per episode (a brief mid-roll or a single end-of-episode mention), in the same grounded voice as the show. No hype. Ranges below are realistic for a niche daily show and scale with audience; treat them as targets, not guarantees.

**Premium Tier (~$1,500-4,000/mo per sponsor at scale):**
- **Journaling brands** — physical guided journals, Stoic journals, gratitude journals (e.g., independent journal makers, Moleskine-style brands). The single most on-brand category.
- **Meditation / focus apps** — Calm, Headspace, Insight Timer, Waking Up. Direct audience overlap.
- **Premium audiobook / book subscriptions** — Audible, Blinkist, Everand. Listeners who like ideas read books.

**Mid Tier (~$500-1,500/mo per sponsor):**
- **Focus / productivity tools** — Notion, Todoist, time-blocking and habit apps, "deep work" tools.
- **Booksellers & publishers** — Penguin Classics, Bookshop.org, indie translators of Stoic texts (great for affiliate links to the actual sources quoted).
- **Sleep / wind-down** — sleep aids, herbal teas, evening-routine products (pairs with a morning show's bookend).

**Lower Tier (~$100-500/mo, or affiliate / CPA):**
- **Coffee brands** — specialty roasters, subscription coffee. The morning-ritual fit is exact ("your morning coffee" is literally in Episode 4).
- **Stationery & desk goods** — pens, notebooks, analog tools.
- **Affiliate book links** — every episode cites a real public-domain source; link to good editions and translations (Gregory Hays' *Meditations*, etc.) for steady affiliate revenue.

**Affiliate baseline:** Because every episode quotes a real, buyable book, an Amazon/Bookshop affiliate program can run from Day 1 with zero sponsor outreach — a floor under revenue while ad inventory fills.

## Social Media Content Plan (Per Episode)

The format is exceptionally **quote-card friendly** — every episode hands you a clean, attributable quote and a one-line takeaway.

**Per episode, create:**
- **1 hero quote card** — the episode's source quote on a calm background, correctly attributed (author + work). The primary asset for Instagram, Pinterest, X.
- **2-3 vertical clips (TikTok / Reels / Shorts, 20-45 sec)** — pull the application or reflection beat; caption on screen, the closing line as the payoff.
- **1 carousel (Instagram / LinkedIn, 3-5 slides)** — Slide 1: the quote. Slides 2-3: the plain-language idea. Final slide: today's reflection prompt + show name.
- **1 X / Threads post** — the closing line as a standalone aphorism, plus "Full 6 minutes on *The Stoic Minute*."
- **1 newsletter line** — feed the day's quote + reflection into a daily email (a natural owned-audience channel for this niche).

**Example quote card (Episode 1):**
```
"Some things are within our power,
and some things are not."
            — Epictetus, Enchiridion

The whole of Stoic freedom in one line.
Today's episode: The Stoic Minute. New every morning.
#Stoicism #DailyWisdom #Mindset
```

**Visual style:** muted, warm, lots of negative space. Serif type. No stock-photo clutter. The aesthetic should feel like the show sounds — calm and uncrowded.
