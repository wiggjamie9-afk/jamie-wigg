# Reference: AE Close Strategies Prompts

## Purpose
A collection of prompts for Account Executives navigating the final stages of a deal — from trial closes and mutual action plans through pricing negotiations, urgency creation, and procurement navigation. Calibrated for complex B2B sales where closing is a sequence of micro-commitments rather than a single moment of pressure. Each prompt is designed to create forward movement without burning relationship capital. Not templates — starting points.

---

## Prompt 01 — Mutual Action Plan Builder
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** A deal has reached evaluation or proposal stage and needs a shared timeline to close
**Structure:** Target close date > buyer's internal process steps > generate a mutual action plan
**Example output:** "I want to get [Company] live by October 1st. Their process involves a security review, legal redline, and CFO approval. Build a backwards-planned mutual action plan in table format — owner, action, deadline — starting from go-live and working back to today's date of July 15th. Include one contingency buffer week."
**Why it works:** A backwards-planned MAP makes timeline risk visible to both parties immediately — the buyer can see that slipping week three breaks their own go-live goal, which makes schedule adherence self-motivated rather than AE-driven.
**Word count:** Table format; max 12 rows; include owner column with [Name] / [AE] placeholders.
**Avg. score:** 86-93

## Prompt 02 — Trial Close Sequence
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After presenting the proposal; checking for commitment before formal paperwork
**Structure:** Deal stage > last known concern > generate three progressive trial close questions
**Example output:** "I've presented the proposal to [Name] at [Company]. Their last stated concern was implementation timeline. Generate three trial close questions that progress from soft to firm: one that checks whether the value case is understood, one that tests internal alignment, and one that invites a next step with a date — without using the phrase 'are you ready to move forward?'"
**Why it works:** A progressive sequence surfaces objections at the softest possible moment — if the value question gets a hesitant answer, you've uncovered a gap before asking for the signature.
**Word count:** Three questions; one per paragraph with a brief note on what to listen for.
**Avg. score:** 84-91

## Prompt 03 — Urgency Without Discounting
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Deal is stalled with no stated objection; prospect going quiet or deferring next steps
**Structure:** Deal context > business event or cost of delay > generate an urgency reframe
**Example output:** "My deal with [Company] has gone quiet for two weeks. No stated objection. They have a Q4 hiring surge starting September and told me in discovery that onboarding inconsistency is their biggest risk. Draft a message that creates urgency by referencing their Q4 deadline — not our quarter end, not a discount, not fear tactics."
**Why it works:** Urgency grounded in the buyer's timeline is compelling; urgency grounded in the seller's quarter end is transparent and erodes trust — prospects know the difference immediately.
**Word count:** 80-100 words; no price incentive language; one direct question at the end.
**Avg. score:** 85-92

## Prompt 04 — "Let Me Think About It" Handler
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Prospect says "I need some time to think" at or near proposal stage
**Structure:** What's been discussed > likely unstated concern > generate a response that surfaces the real issue
**Example output:** "After presenting the proposal, [Name] said 'I need to think about it.' We've covered value, timeline, and pricing. Draft a response that acknowledges their need for time, offers two possible real concerns they might be weighing, and invites them to confirm which — without pressuring or sounding scripted."
**Why it works:** Naming two possible concerns forces a binary — the buyer either confirms one (revealing the real objection) or rejects both (at which point they often volunteer the true hesitation themselves).
**Word count:** Under 70 words; conversational tone; ends on a genuine either/or question.
**Avg. score:** 83-90

## Prompt 05 — Procurement Navigation Brief
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Deal has been handed to procurement and the champion has stepped back from the process
**Structure:** Contract value > vendor category > generate procurement question anticipation and response guide
**Example output:** "Our $90K deal at [Company] has gone to their central procurement team. We sell SaaS software, so we expect security questionnaire, insurance certificates, and data processing agreement requests. Draft a procurement brief I can send our champion with: (1) what procurement will likely ask, (2) our standard responses, and (3) what [Name] should pre-empt to speed up the process."
**Why it works:** Arming the champion with a procurement preview transforms them from a passive observer to an active accelerant — they can answer questions before they're asked, compressing a 4-week process into 2.
**Word count:** Three-section brief; max 300 words; bullet format for questions and responses.
**Avg. score:** 82-89

## Prompt 06 — Negotiation Anchor Response
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Procurement or the economic buyer opens with an aggressive price reduction request
**Structure:** Their opening ask > your walk-away position > generate a counter that preserves value without caving
**Example output:** "Procurement at [Company] asked for 30% off our $90K proposal. Our floor is $81K with extended payment terms. Draft a response that declines the 30% discount, acknowledges their cost pressure, and offers two alternative deal structures — one involving a longer contract term, one involving a reduced initial scope — that each meet our floor without purely caving on price."
**Why it works:** Offering two alternatives shifts the negotiation from a price-cutting conversation to a value-structure conversation — buyers feel agency without extracting pure margin.
**Word count:** 100-120 words; no apologetic language; present alternatives as equally attractive.
**Avg. score:** 84-91

## Prompt 07 — Champion Activation Script
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** A deal is stalling because the champion is not advocating internally; needs activation
**Structure:** Champion profile > deal status > internal obstacle > generate a champion activation script
**Example output:** "[Name] is my champion at [Company] — they believe in the product but have gone quiet since passing it to their VP. I suspect they're waiting for the VP to ask rather than proactively recommending. Write a message I can send [Name] that gives them a specific talking point to take to their VP, framed as 'here's what you could say' — not as instructions."
**Why it works:** Champions often stall not because they've lost conviction but because they don't know what to say next; giving them a specific talking point is a political gift, not a push.
**Word count:** One talking point paragraph they could copy-paste internally; under 80 words.
**Avg. score:** 83-90

## Prompt 08 — Competitive Displacement Close
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Prospect is currently using a competitor and needs to justify switching internally
**Structure:** Incumbent vendor > switching cost concerns > generate a switching cost reframe
**Example output:** "[Company] is on [Competitor] and [Name] is worried about the disruption of switching. Our implementation is 4 weeks vs. their typical 3-month rollout. Draft a one-page 'cost of staying' argument — the continuing cost of the status quo vs. our 4-week ramp — that [Name] can use to justify the switch to their CFO without it reading like our marketing copy."
**Why it works:** A "cost of staying" argument flips the switching cost question on its head — the real risk is the 10 months of opportunity cost while they delay switching, not the 4-week implementation.
**Word count:** Two columns: cost of status quo / cost of switching; narrative summary under 80 words.
**Avg. score:** 85-92

## Prompt 09 — Multi-Stakeholder Alignment Close
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Multiple stakeholders have different concerns and no single decision-maker is driving to close
**Structure:** Stakeholder list > each person's concern > generate an alignment narrative
**Example output:** "I have four stakeholders at [Company]: the VP of Sales (wants faster ramp), the CFO (wants ROI clarity), the IT Director (wants security certification), and the CRO (wants forecast accuracy). Draft a 150-word closing summary email that addresses each concern in one sentence, references what we've already provided for each, and names a single next step with a deadline."
**Why it works:** A stakeholder-aware closing email signals to each person that their concern was heard — when everyone reads it and feels seen, the social pressure to object individually drops significantly.
**Word count:** One sentence per stakeholder + two-sentence next step; total under 150 words.
**Avg. score:** 84-91

---

## Prompt 10 — End-of-Quarter Executive Reach
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** A stalled deal needs an executive-to-executive outreach from your side to theirs
**Structure:** Deal background > executive sponsor name > prospect executive > generate an exec outreach message
**Example output:** "My VP wants to reach out to the CRO at [Company] to unblock a stalled $90K deal. Our VP and their CRO have no prior relationship. Draft a peer-to-peer message — not salesy, not urgent-feeling — that frames the outreach as executive alignment on a shared outcome, references the business case we've built, and invites a 15-minute call."
**Why it works:** An unsolicited executive message lands only when it's genuinely peer-level in tone — any hint of escalation pressure signals desperation and triggers the CRO to protect their team from being pushed.
**Word count:** 100-130 words; first-person from the AE's VP; no discount offers; ends on a specific ask.
**Avg. score:** 82-89
