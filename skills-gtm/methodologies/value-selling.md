# Reference: Value Selling Prompts

## Purpose
Value Selling ties every capability, feature, and conversation to quantified business outcomes rather than product attributes. The buyer's question is always "what is this worth to us?" — and the rep's job is to answer it before the buyer asks, in the buyer's own financial language. These prompts help a rep build value drivers, model cost of inaction, and anchor negotiation to outcomes rather than price. Not templates — starting points.

## The framework
- **Capability → Value driver:** Every feature maps to a value driver — cost reduction, revenue growth, risk mitigation, or productivity gain. Never present a capability without naming its value driver.
- **Quantified business value:** Value must be expressed in dollars, percentage points, hours, headcount, or risk units — not adjectives like "significant" or "substantial."
- **Value drivers:** The finite categories through which a solution creates business value. Common drivers: operational efficiency, revenue acceleration, cost avoidance, risk reduction, time-to-market, customer retention.
- **Cost of inaction (COI):** The financial and strategic cost of not solving the problem. A strong COI makes urgency intrinsic rather than manufactured by a deadline or discount.
- **Business outcomes over features:** Buyers buy outcomes. Features are evidence that an outcome is achievable, not the thing being purchased. Lead with the outcome; use the feature to substantiate it.
- **Value hypothesis:** An early-stage testable statement of the value the rep believes the solution can deliver, built on available data and refined through discovery.
- **Mutual success plan:** A shared document tying implementation milestones to business outcomes, so value realisation is tracked and visible to both sides post-sale.

## Prompt 01 — Build the Value Hypothesis
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Pre-call research; before the first substantive discovery meeting
**Structure:** Identify two to three probable value drivers from public information → quantify a plausible range for each → frame as a hypothesis to be validated in conversation
**Example output:** "Based on [Company]'s headcount and the process you've described publicly, my hypothesis is that we can recover roughly [X hours] per analyst per week in [process area], which at [Company]'s fully-loaded cost would be around [$Y] annually. I want to test that in our conversation and adjust it to your actual numbers — does that framing make sense as a starting point?"
**Why it works:** A value hypothesis opens the conversation at the outcome level rather than the feature level. It signals financial fluency, sets the rep apart from reps who lead with demos, and gives the buyer something concrete to react to.
**Word count:** 60–80 words
**Avg. score:** 83–90

## Prompt 02 — Map Capabilities to Value Drivers
**Role:** Account Executive / Solutions Engineer
**Trigger:** Preparing a demo or product walkthrough; ensuring every feature shown ties to a business outcome
**Structure:** Name the capability → state the value driver it activates → quantify the expected impact → give a reference point from a comparable customer
**Example output:** "This capability — automated [process] — reduces the manual reconciliation your team runs today. The value driver is operational efficiency: our customers at similar scale see a 60–70% reduction in time spent on this task. For [Company], that would free up roughly [X hours/month] that currently costs [$Y] in labour."
**Why it works:** Buyers who sit through feature demos without a value anchor forget what they saw within hours. Mapping each capability to a value driver in real time keeps the conversation at the outcome level and builds a mental model the buyer can share internally.
**Word count:** 50–65 words
**Avg. score:** 84–91

## Prompt 03 — Quantify the Cost of Inaction
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** When the buyer is delaying or considering a "wait and see" approach
**Structure:** Acknowledge the delay is understandable → calculate the cost per period of not acting → project it forward → make inaction feel expensive
**Example output:** "Totally understand the instinct to wait until Q2 for the right conditions. What I'd ask you to factor in: at the current run rate, [problem] is costing [Company] roughly [$X per month] in [cost category]. That's [$X * months] by the time a Q2 decision would take effect. I want to make sure that trade-off is explicit — is the wait worth that number?"
**Why it works:** Urgency based on cost of inaction is more durable than urgency based on artificial deadlines or discounts. It ties the timing conversation to the buyer's own business math rather than the rep's quota pressure.
**Word count:** 65–80 words
**Avg. score:** 85–92

## Prompt 04 — Anchor to Business Outcomes, Not Features
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** When a buyer asks "what does [feature] do?" — redirect to the outcome
**Structure:** Answer the feature question briefly → pivot to the outcome the feature enables → connect to a metric the buyer owns
**Example output:** "The short answer on [feature] is [one-sentence explanation]. But the reason it matters for [Company] is what it does to [business metric]: instead of [current state], your team gets [outcome]. For [Name]'s team, that translates to [specific, quantified benefit]. Want me to show you how that plays out in practice?"
**Why it works:** Buyers who fixate on features are comparing specifications, not value. Redirecting to business outcomes keeps the rep's solution positioned at a higher level than any feature-to-feature comparison.
**Word count:** 50–65 words
**Avg. score:** 82–89

## Prompt 05 — Build a Joint Value Model
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After discovery; building the business case with the buyer rather than for them
**Structure:** Invite the buyer to input their own numbers → model the output together → let the buyer own the result
**Example output:** "Let's build this business case together rather than me handing you a PDF. If you can give me your [cost per unit], [volume per period], and [error rate], I'll show you what the model looks like — and you can adjust any assumption you disagree with. The goal is a number you'd be comfortable presenting to [Economic Buyer] as your analysis, not mine."
**Why it works:** A value model the buyer helped build is far more defensible internally than one the rep produced unilaterally. Joint ownership increases accuracy, trust, and the champion's ability to advocate for the investment.
**Word count:** 65–80 words
**Avg. score:** 85–91

## Prompt 06 — Address "Your Price Is Too High"
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** When a buyer anchors to price without referencing value
**Structure:** Acknowledge the price point → reframe from price to cost-to-value ratio → return to the agreed value figure and calculate ROI
**Example output:** "I hear you — [$X] is a real number. What I'd like to do is put it next to the value model we built together. If our baseline estimate of [$Y] in annual savings is even half right, the payback period is [Z months]. Does the price feel different in that context, or is the concern more about budget availability right now?"
**Why it works:** Price objections are almost always value objections in disguise. If the value is clear and believed, price is a math problem. If it isn't, no discount will close the deal sustainably.
**Word count:** 55–70 words
**Avg. score:** 83–90

## Prompt 07 — Define Value Drivers for a Specific Persona
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Preparing for a multi-stakeholder meeting; tailoring the value case to each attendee's priorities
**Structure:** Name the persona → identify their primary success metric → map one or two value drivers to that metric → cut everything else
**Example output:** "For [Name] the VP of Finance, the relevant value driver is cost avoidance — specifically what [problem] costs in write-offs, rework, or compliance exposure. Revenue acceleration and time savings are real, but they're not her headline. I'll lead with the risk and cost avoidance numbers and save the productivity story for the Ops team."
**Why it works:** Value is persona-relative. A CFO's value driver is not a VP of Engineering's value driver. Reps who present a single value story to mixed audiences lose the stakeholders whose priority wasn't the headline.
**Word count:** 55–70 words
**Avg. score:** 83–89

---

## Prompt 08 — Create a Mutual Success Plan Tied to Value
**Role:** Account Executive / Customer Success
**Trigger:** At or after contract signature; bridging from sale to realisation
**Structure:** Tie each implementation milestone to a business outcome → assign a timeline → agree on how the outcome will be measured → schedule a review
**Example output:** "Before we close out the commercial conversation, I want to make sure we agree on what success looks like post-go-live. If [milestone 1] is done by [date], we'd expect [metric] to move by [amount]. By [milestone 2], the target is [metric 2]. Can we put a 90-day review on the calendar now so we can compare actuals to the business case we built together?"
**Why it works:** A mutual success plan prevents value erosion post-sale by making the promised outcomes concrete and time-bound. It also sets the foundation for renewal and expansion conversations, because value delivered is the strongest upsell argument available.
**Word count:** 65–80 words
**Avg. score:** 84–91
