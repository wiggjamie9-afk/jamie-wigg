# Reference: Gap Selling Prompts

## Purpose
Gap Selling (Keenan) rejects needs-based selling in favour of problem-centric diagnosis. The rep's job is to understand the buyer's current state, define the desired future state, and make the gap between them — and its root cause — so vivid that purchasing becomes the logical next step. These prompts help a rep conduct rigorous discovery, diagnose root causes, and quantify the value of closing the gap. Not templates — starting points.

## The framework
- **Current state:** A precise picture of how the buyer operates today — tools, processes, outcomes, metrics. Not vague; specific and often uncomfortable to say out loud.
- **Future state:** Where the buyer wants to be — measurably better, with specific outcomes. Must be defined in the buyer's own terms, not the rep's aspirational language.
- **The gap:** The distance between current and future state. The wider and more costly the gap, the stronger the buying motive. No gap = no deal.
- **Technical impact:** How the current-state problem manifests in systems, data quality, process failures, or operational metrics.
- **Business impact:** What the technical impact costs the business in revenue, margin, time, or competitive position.
- **Personal impact:** What the problem costs the individual buyer — stress, reputation, career risk, wasted effort.
- **Root-cause diagnosis:** Keenan insists that most reps address symptoms. Gap Selling requires identifying why the problem exists, not just what it is. Treating the wrong root cause means the problem returns.
- **Problem-centric:** Features are irrelevant until the problem is fully diagnosed. Pitching before diagnosing is malpractice.

## Prompt 01 — Establish Current State
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Opening discovery; the first 15–20 minutes of any substantive conversation
**Structure:** Ask how a specific process works today → ask what tools are involved → ask what the output or result looks like in concrete terms
**Example output:** "Walk me through how [process] actually works at [Company] today — from the moment [trigger event] happens to when [outcome] lands. What tools are in the flow, and what does the output look like in terms of numbers or reports your team actually uses?"
**Why it works:** Gap Selling requires an accurate current-state picture before anything else. Reps who rush to future state or solution without mapping current state are guessing at the gap — and often selling to the wrong problem.
**Word count:** 40–55 words
**Avg. score:** 81–87

---

## Prompt 02 — Probe Current-State Problems
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After the buyer has described their current process; surfacing what is broken
**Structure:** Ask what goes wrong → ask how often → ask who notices first → ask what the fallout looks like
**Example output:** "In that process you just described, where does it most commonly break down? Is it a consistency issue, a timing issue, something else? And when it does break, who feels it first — and what does the cleanup look like for them?"
**Why it works:** Keenan argues that buyers are often not fully conscious of how bad their current state is until a rep surfaces it systematically. Probing frequency and fallout makes the problem real and urgent rather than theoretical.
**Word count:** 40–55 words
**Avg. score:** 83–89

## Prompt 03 — Define the Future State
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After current-state problems are on the table; building the bridge to motivation
**Structure:** Ask what the buyer wants the outcome to look like → quantify where possible → ask what "success" would mean for their team and for them personally
**Example output:** "If you could wave a wand and fix this — not just patch it, but actually solve it — what would [process] look like six months from now? What numbers would be different? And what would that mean for you personally in terms of how you spend your time?"
**Why it works:** Gap Selling requires a future state that is specific and personally meaningful. A vague future state ("better, faster, cheaper") produces a weak buying motive. Specificity creates urgency.
**Word count:** 45–60 words
**Avg. score:** 83–89

---

## Prompt 04 — Quantify the Gap
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After both current and future state are defined; before building a business case
**Structure:** Place current and future state side by side → calculate the difference → assign a value per period → confirm the buyer's agreement on the numbers
**Example output:** "So today, [metric] sits at [current number], and the target you've described is [future number]. That's a gap of [delta]. If we close it over 12 months, what does that translate to in dollars — or in hours your team gets back? Let's put a number to it together so we're working from the same picture."
**Why it works:** The gap is the deal. If the rep and buyer cannot agree on its size and cost, there is no shared rationale for purchasing. Quantifying the gap together aligns both parties on the value before price ever comes up.
**Word count:** 50–65 words
**Avg. score:** 85–91

## Prompt 05 — Diagnose Technical Impact
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After the buyer names a business problem; before jumping to a solution
**Structure:** Ask how the problem shows up in systems or data → ask what breaks downstream → build a technical picture of the failure mode
**Example output:** "When [problem] happens, how does it show up in your systems? Is it bad data, a missing field, a manual workaround that someone has to apply after the fact? And when that workaround fails or gets skipped, what downstream system or report is affected?"
**Why it works:** Keenan insists root cause is always technical, business, and personal simultaneously. Technical impact diagnosis shows the buyer that the rep understands how their world works — not just how the symptom feels.
**Word count:** 40–55 words
**Avg. score:** 82–88

---

## Prompt 06 — Surface Business Impact
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After technical impact is mapped; amplifying the stakes for leadership
**Structure:** Connect the technical failure to a business metric the buyer owns or reports on → quantify the cost → ask who in the organisation is exposed to this impact
**Example output:** "If the data quality issue is producing [X% error rate], and your team closes [N] deals a month based on that data, how many deals a month are you walking into with wrong assumptions? What does one miscalculated deal cost [Company] on average — and who owns that P&L line?"
**Why it works:** Business impact is what gets budget approved. Connecting a technical problem to a P&L line or a KPI owned by the economic buyer transforms a process complaint into a financial decision.
**Word count:** 45–60 words
**Avg. score:** 85–91

---

## Prompt 07 — Identify Personal Impact
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** When the buyer seems intellectually engaged but not emotionally invested; building personal urgency
**Structure:** Ask how the problem affects the buyer's day-to-day → ask what they have to do because of it that they wish they didn't → ask what it would mean for them personally if it was solved
**Example output:** "Beyond the business numbers — what does this problem actually cost you personally? Is it late nights, fire drills, difficult conversations with [stakeholder], or something else? If this was gone by Q2, what would you do with that time or energy instead?"
**Why it works:** Keenan is explicit: buyers buy for personal reasons as much as business reasons. A rep who only speaks to business impact misses the emotional driver that actually motivates a champion to fight for budget internally.
**Word count:** 40–55 words
**Avg. score:** 83–89

---

## Prompt 08 — Root-Cause Diagnosis
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** When the buyer has named a symptom and is likely to buy the wrong solution for it
**Structure:** Acknowledge the symptom → introduce the concept of root cause → probe the underlying cause → confirm before proposing a solution
**Example output:** "The [symptom] you're describing is real — and it's painful. But before I talk about how we'd address it, I want to make sure we're solving the actual problem rather than the symptom. In most cases we see this pattern, the root cause is [likely root cause]. Is that true here, or is something else driving it? The answer changes what you actually need."
**Why it works:** Keenan's central argument is that reps who treat symptoms create customers who churn when the symptom returns. Root-cause diagnosis builds trust, prevents buyer's remorse, and ensures the solution actually closes the gap.
**Word count:** 60–75 words
**Avg. score:** 86–92
