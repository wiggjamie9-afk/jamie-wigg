# Reference: SPIN Selling Prompts

## Purpose
SPIN Selling (Neil Rackham, Huthwaite Research Group) is a question-based methodology proven in large-sale environments. These prompts help a rep move buyers from vague dissatisfaction to explicit need by asking questions in a deliberate sequence — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff. Not templates — starting points.

## The framework
- **S — Situation questions:** Establish context — the buyer's current environment, tools, team size, and processes. Use sparingly; too many Situation questions feel like an interrogation.
- **P — Problem questions:** Surface difficulties, frustrations, and dissatisfactions the buyer may not have articulated. Rackham's data shows average reps ask too few of these.
- **I — Implication questions:** Expand the consequences of the problem — financial cost, downstream effects, risk to other teams or goals. These are the highest-leverage question type in complex sales.
- **N — Need-payoff questions:** Shift the buyer's focus to the value of solving the problem, inviting them to articulate the benefit in their own words. This reduces objections and increases perceived value.
- **Key insight from Rackham:** Successful reps ask far fewer Situation questions and far more Implication and Need-payoff questions than average reps. The ratio matters as much as the content.
- **Large vs small sales:** Situation and Problem questions are enough to close small sales. Implication and Need-payoff questions are essential for large, complex, multi-stakeholder deals.

## Prompt 01 — Situation (Minimal)
**Role:** Account Executive / Sales Development Rep
**Trigger:** First 5 minutes of an introductory call; establish context before probing
**Structure:** Ask one targeted Situation question → confirm the answer → move to Problem. Resist the urge to ask a second Situation question.
**Example output:** "Before we dive in, can you give me a quick picture of how your team currently handles [process] today — what tools are in the stack and roughly how many people are involved?"
**Why it works:** Rackham's research found that top performers ask very few Situation questions because buyers find them tedious. One sharp, pre-researched Situation question signals preparation and earns trust; five feels like homework.
**Word count:** 30–45 words
**Avg. score:** 78–85

---

## Prompt 02 — Problem (Direct)
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Immediately after establishing context; transition from Situation to Problem
**Structure:** Bridge from the process just described → name a common pain point in that process → invite the buyer to confirm or correct
**Example output:** "With a team that size running [process] manually, it's common to hit friction around [specific pain]. Is that something you run into, or is the challenge different for you?"
**Why it works:** Problem questions uncover dissatisfaction the buyer may not have volunteered. Naming a plausible pain and asking for confirmation is faster than open-ended probing and shows domain knowledge.
**Word count:** 35–50 words
**Avg. score:** 80–87

---

## Prompt 03 — Problem (Open Probe)
**Role:** Sales Development Rep / Account Executive
**Trigger:** When you want to hear the buyer name the pain without leading them
**Structure:** Ask an open question about difficulty or frustration in the area → stay silent after asking
**Example output:** "What's the most frustrating part of the current [process] for your team — the thing that, if you could fix it tomorrow, would make the biggest difference day to day?"
**Why it works:** Open Problem questions invite the buyer to own the pain. Pain they articulate themselves is more credible to them than pain you name. This is especially powerful when the buyer will need to justify the purchase internally.
**Word count:** 30–45 words
**Avg. score:** 82–88

---

## Prompt 04 — Implication (Cost of Delay)
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After a Problem is confirmed; before presenting any solution
**Structure:** Take the problem → ask what it costs in time, money, or risk → ask what happens if it continues for another year
**Example output:** "You mentioned that [problem] adds about [X hours] per week across the team. Over the course of a year, that's [calculated total] — and that's just the direct time cost. What does it do to [downstream outcome] when that backlog builds up and [consequence]?"
**Why it works:** Implication questions amplify the problem until its size justifies change. Rackham found these questions are what separate average reps from top performers in large-sale environments, yet they are the least practised question type.
**Word count:** 40–60 words
**Avg. score:** 85–92

---

## Prompt 05 — Implication (Risk Cascade)
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** When the buyer has named a pain that affects multiple teams or goals
**Structure:** Identify a downstream team or objective affected by the problem → ask how the pain ripples through
**Example output:** "If [problem] is slowing down your ops team, what effect does that have on [adjacent team] meeting their targets? And when [adjacent team] is blocked, how does that show up for [Name]'s priorities at a leadership level?"
**Why it works:** Implication questions that map pain to other stakeholders widen the circle of impact and create urgency at the executive level — which is where budget decisions are made.
**Word count:** 40–55 words
**Avg. score:** 84–91

---

## Prompt 06 — Need-payoff (Value Invitation)
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** After Implication questions have established significant pain; before pitching features
**Structure:** Ask the buyer what solving the problem would be worth — or what it would enable — in their own words
**Example output:** "If you could eliminate [problem] entirely, what would that mean for the team — in terms of time, morale, or what you'd be able to focus on instead?"
**Why it works:** Need-payoff questions cause buyers to sell themselves. When a buyer articulates the benefit, it is more persuasive than any claim a rep makes. It also surfaces the specific value language to use in the proposal and with other stakeholders.
**Word count:** 30–45 words
**Avg. score:** 86–92

---

## Prompt 07 — Need-payoff (Explicit ROI Invitation)
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Before building a business case; after establishing quantified pain
**Structure:** Ask the buyer to put a number on the value of solving the problem — make it safe to speculate
**Example output:** "Just ballparking it — if you were to solve [problem] in the next quarter, what would a good outcome look like in hard numbers for [Company]? Revenue retained, headcount freed up, compliance risk reduced?"
**Why it works:** Explicit ROI articulated by the buyer anchors the business case to their own language and priorities, making it far harder for a committee to dismiss. The rep's role is to invite, not invent.
**Word count:** 35–50 words
**Avg. score:** 83–90

---

## Prompt 08 — SPIN Sequence (Full Discovery Flow)
**Role:** Account Executive
**Trigger:** Structuring a 45–60 minute discovery call from scratch
**Structure:** One Situation question → two to three Problem questions → two Implication questions → one or two Need-payoff questions. Follow buyer energy; skip Situation questions where research already provides context.
**Example output:** "To make sure I'm focused on the right things: [Situation — one question]. Where does that break down most often for your team? [Problem]. When that happens, how does it affect [downstream team or metric]? [Implication]. If you fixed that, what would be the most valuable outcome for [Name] personally, and for the business? [Need-payoff]."
**Why it works:** The SPIN sequence is a diagnostic arc, not a script. Reps who internalize the order and practice the ratio — heavy on Implication and Need-payoff — consistently outsell those who wing discovery or lead with Situation questions.
**Word count:** 60–80 words (for the briefing / coaching context); each live question 20–40 words
**Avg. score:** 87–93
