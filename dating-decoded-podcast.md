# Dating Decoded Podcast Specification

**Status:** Production-Ready  
**Format:** 12-minute weekly episodes  
**Target Audience:** Singles 18–45 (dating curious, relationship-seekers, psychology enthusiasts)  
**Tone:** Conversational, research-backed, non-judgmental, occasional humor  
**Distribution:** Spotify, Apple Podcasts, YouTube, TikTok, Threads  
**Narration:** TTS (Kokoro or ElevenLabs) with sound design  
**Production Cycle:** 1 episode per week (120 min scripting + 20 min audio production)

---

## 1. Show Positioning

### Why "Dating Decoded" Works

**Market Gap:** Dating advice podcasts are either aspirational ("find your soulmate NOW") or cynical ("dating is broken"). Dating Decoded bridges that gap by grounding advice in attachment theory, neuroscience, and behavioral psychology—making dating *understandable* instead of anxiety-inducing.

**Unique Angle:**
- **Psychology-first:** Every episode starts with a peer-reviewed research insight (Attachment Theory, Dopamine, Mirror Neurons, etc.)
- **Real listener stories:** 2–3 anonymized caller/listener scenarios per episode that illustrate the psychology in action
- **Actionable frameworks:** Listeners leave with a specific tool (e.g., "The 3-Question Filter for Red Flags," "Attachment Style Communication Script")
- **No gatekeeping:** Interviews with relationship psychologists, certified dating coaches, neuroscientists—but always translated for the everyday dater

**Listener Outcomes:**
- Understand *why* they're attracted to certain people (neuroscience)
- Recognize their own attachment patterns and communicate them
- Identify green flags and red flags earlier
- Build confidence in their dating decisions
- Feel less alone in common dating struggles

**Comparable Success:** Podcasts like *Where Should We Begin* (Esther Perel), *Modern Love*, and *How to Fail* prove there's massive appetite for psychologically grounded relationship content. Dating Decoded targets a younger, more active dating demographic (18–35) than Esther Perel's 35–55 skew.

## 2. Show Format (12-Minute Structure)

**Total Runtime:** 11:45–12:00 (includes intro/outro)

### Segment Breakdown

| Time | Segment | Content | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:30 | **Cold Open** | Hook (listener story, surprising stat, or question) | 30s |
| 0:30–1:15 | **Intro + Guest Intro** | Show title, host name, episode title, guest bio (if guest) | 45s |
| 1:15–3:00 | **Psychology Deep Dive** | Research foundation (1–2 key studies or theories; neurobiological angle) | 1:45 |
| 3:00–7:30 | **Story + Framework** | 2–3 real listener scenarios; apply psychology to each; introduce decision framework | 4:30 |
| 7:30–10:00 | **Caller Q&A / Expert Riff** | One deep-dive Q from listener or expert unpacks a nuance | 2:30 |
| 10:00–11:00 | **Actionable Takeaway** | 1–2 specific tools/scripts listeners can use this week | 1:00 |
| 11:00–11:45 | **Outro + CTA** | Recap, call-to-action (leave a voicemail, share on Threads, next week's topic), sponsor read (mid-roll only for premium sponsors) | 0:45 |

### Audio Design

- **Host Voice:** Warm, slightly higher energy than a therapist, lower than a true-crime podcaster. Conversational pacing (not rushed).
- **Background Music:** Subtle, lo-fi hip-hop or indie instrumental (Epidemic Sound licensing). Fade down during story segments; underscore during psychology deep-dive.
- **Sound Effects:** Minimal. Short transition whoosh between segments. Heartbeat or "ding" for framework reveals (e.g., "Here's The Green Flag Filter…").
- **Podcast Intro Signature:** 8–10 second sonic branding (ukulele riff + subtle synth, fading into host greeting).

You're literally telling your vagus nerve—your 'calm system'—that you're safe. Then, *during* the date, if you notice you're talking too much, pause. Ask them a question. Listen to the answer. I'm flying solo again, but we're diving into attachment theory with neuroscientist Dr. Sue Johnson's research, plus three real listener stories that'll hit different once you understand your attachment blueprint."

---

**[PSYCHOLOGY DEEP DIVE – 1:45]**

**HOST:** "Attachment theory comes from John Bowlby, a British psychologist who studied infants and their caregivers in the 1950s. But here's the part that changed dating forever: The way your caregiver responded to you as a baby didn't just affect your childhood. It created a *template* for how you relate to romantic partners.

There are four main attachment styles:

**Secure:** Your caregiver was responsive and consistent. You grew up believing you're worthy of love and that other people can be trusted. In dating, you can ask for what you need without shame, and you can give without resentment.

**Anxious:** Your caregiver was inconsistently available. Sometimes they showed up; sometimes they didn't. You learned that love means *working harder*, proving you're worthy. In dating, you often over-pursue, over-share, and struggle with jealousy.

**Avoidant:** Your caregiver was emotionally distant or overbearing. You learned that closeness = loss of self. In dating, you pursue independence fiercely, you pull away when things get intimate, and you often say, 'I just don't believe in needing people.'

**Fearful-Avoidant:** Your caregiver was chaotic or frightening. You want connection but you're terrified of it. You pursue intensely, then push away hard. Your dating looks like a pendulum.

Here's the neuroscience part: These attachment patterns live in your amygdala and your vagus nerve—your *survival system*. So when you date someone, you're not just choosing them consciously. Your nervous system is recognizing them as *familiar*, and familiar *feels* safe, even if it's actually dangerous.

That's why you keep choosing the same person. Your nervous system is trying to *finish the story*. To get the love you didn't get as a kid. And even though you *know* it's not working, your amygdala says: *'But what if THIS TIME, it does?'*"

---

**[STORY + FRAMEWORK – 4:30]**

**HOST:** "Let me show you how this plays out in real dating. Scenario One, from a listener named Alex:

*'I'm 31, and I've dated four people in the last three years. Every single one was emotionally unavailable. I knew it from the start. I told my friends it was a bad idea. And I dated them anyway. What is wrong with me?'*

Nothing is wrong with Alex. Alex has an *anxious attachment style*. Here's what's happening: Anxiously attached people are often drawn to avoidantly attached people. Why? Because unavailable feels *familiar*. It feels like the emotional distance Alex experienced with a parent or caregiver. And on an unconscious level, Alex believes: *'If I can make them love me, then I'll finally have earned the love I never got.'*

It's a setup. But it's not a character flaw. It's a pattern. And patterns can be interrupted.

Here's the framework: **The Attachment Mismatch Matrix.** If you have an anxious attachment style, you're often attracted to avoidant. If you're avoidant, you're often attracted to anxious (because they don't pressure you to be close). If you're fearful-avoidant, you bounce between both.

For Alex, the tool is: **Before you pursue someone, ask three questions:**
1. Do they pursue me back?
2. Do they initiate plans?
3. Do they follow through?

If the answer to all three is no, they're avoidant. And an anxious-avoidant pairing is *really* hard to make work, because what the anxious person needs (consistency, reassurance) is exactly what the avoidant person can't give without feeling suffocated.

Scenario Two, from Jordan:

*'I'm avoidant, I know I am. But I also hate being alone. So I date people, and after a few months, I panic and leave. Then I regret it. Why am I like this?'*

Jordan is describing *fearful-avoidant* attachment. And here's the neuroscience: Jordan's nervous system is saying, 'I want connection, but I'm not safe.' So Jordan gets close, and then the threat alarm goes off, and they flee. Rinse, repeat.

The thing is: Jordan isn't broken. Jordan needs to date someone *secure*. A secure partner can handle Jordan's push-pull because they have enough self-worth to not take it personally. They can say, 'I see you're scared. I'm not going anywhere.' And that consistency actually rewires the nervous system over time.

Scenario Three, from Casey:

*'I'm secure, but I keep dating insecure people. And I'm exhausted. I feel like I'm always the one doing the emotional work.'*

This is important. Secure attachment is *attractive*. You're calm, you're responsive, you're not needy. But if you're consistently choosing insecure partners, ask yourself: Are you drawn to the idea of *fixing* them? Are you unconsciously attracted to the chase?

Here's the thing about secure attachment: it's not just being the healthy one. It's also *setting boundaries*. It's saying, 'I love you, but I can't be your emotional support system if you won't do your own work.'

For Casey, the framework is: **The Compatibility Filter.** Before you commit to someone, check:
- Can they regulate their own emotions?
- Do they take responsibility for their behavior?
- Are they willing to go to therapy or do inner work?

If the answer is no, they're probably not secure, and the relationship will exhaust you."

**[CALLER Q&A / EXPERT RIFF – 2:30]**

Great question. And here's the truth: most people are on a *spectrum*. You might be secure in some relationships and anxious in others. You might be avoidant with intimacy but anxious with commitment. Attachment isn't a box; it's more like a dial.

The research from Sue Johnson and Esther Perel shows that even secure people can become anxious or avoidant in the *wrong relationship*. If you're dating someone who's dismissive, you'll start to anxiously pursue them. If you're dating someone who's clingy, you'll pull away.

So instead of asking, 'What's my attachment style?' ask: 'What does my nervous system need to feel safe in this relationship?' For some people, it's reassurance. For others, it's space. For secure people, it's usually both—and they can ask for both without shame.

And here's the most important part: Your attachment style *can change*. Neuroscience shows that relationships are where we can rewire ourselves. If you date a secure partner, or if you do therapy work, your nervous system can learn a new pattern.

So don't get stuck in a label. Use attachment theory as a *flashlight*, not a *ceiling*."

Three months later, you realize your gut was screaming. Here's the thing: your intuition isn't always right. But your nervous system is *always* picking up on something. Today we're decoding the difference between a real red flag and an anxiety signal—so you can trust your gut without spiraling."

*[Intro signature]*

**HOST:** "I'm Morgan. This is Dating Decoded."

**[INTRO + GUEST INTRO – 45s]**

I'm going solo, but we're diving into neuroscience research on interoception—your ability to sense what's happening inside your body—plus three real scenarios where listeners had to decide: Is this a red flag or am I spiraling?"

---

**[PSYCHOLOGY DEEP DIVE – 1:45]**

**HOST:** "Here's the neuroscience: Your body knows things before your brain does. Antonio Damasio, a neuroscientist, calls this the 'somatic marker hypothesis.' Basically, when you encounter danger—real or imagined—your body triggers a response: a tightness in your chest, a flutter in your stomach, a sense of 'something's not right.'

But here's where it gets complicated: Your body *also* triggers these responses when you're anxious, insecure, or activated by your attachment wounds. So how do you know which one it is?

**Red flag:** A pattern of behavior that suggests someone is dishonest, disrespectful, or dangerous. Examples: They lie about small things. They're rude to service workers. They don't respect your boundaries. They make you feel small or wrong. They're inconsistent—they say they care, but their actions don't match.

**Anxiety signal:** Your nervous system is activated because something feels *unfamiliar* or because it resembles a past hurt. Your body is triggered, but the person hasn't *done* anything wrong. Examples: They're a little quieter than you expected. They have a different love language. They move slower in relationships than you do. They remind you of an ex.

Here's the key difference: **With a red flag, you can point to specific behaviors.** With an anxiety signal, you're mostly experiencing a *feeling*.

The neurobiological part: Your amygdala (threat detection) and your insula (body awareness) are working together. But they don't know the difference between a real threat and a *perceived* threat. So you have to use your prefrontal cortex—your rational mind—to sort it out.

That's hard. Especially because your amygdala is *faster* than your prefrontal cortex. It's trying to protect you from the pain it remembers. And sometimes it overreacts."

---

**[STORY + FRAMEWORK – 4:30]**

**HOST:** "Let's look at three real scenarios. Scenario One, from Samantha:

*'I went on a date with someone, and within two hours, they tried to kiss me without asking. I felt violated. My friends said, 'That's normal! He's just confident!' But I can't stop thinking about it. Is this a red flag or am I being uptight?'*

Samantha, this is a *red flag*. Not because kissing is bad. But because they didn't check in. They didn't respect your consent. Your body knew this before your brain did. And here's the thing: someone who doesn't respect your boundary at hour two will violate other boundaries later. This is how patterns start.

The framework for this: **The Consent Filter.** Someone who respects you will:
- Check in before physical escalation
- Listen if you say no
- Never pressure you into affection

If they do these things, they're signaling: 'I care about your comfort more than I care about my wants.' That's secure behavior. If they don't, they're signaling: 'My needs matter more than your boundaries.' Red flag.

Scenario Two, from David:

*'I'm dating someone and I really like them. But they're kind of quiet. They don't text me back right away. My ex was super responsive and loving, and I felt so secure. But this person is more reserved. Is the quiet-ness a red flag, or am I just used to a different love language?'*

David, this is an *anxiety signal*, not a red flag. And here's the neuroscience: David's nervous system learned from his ex that responsiveness = safety. So when his current partner is quieter, his amygdala starts screaming, 'Danger! They don't care!'

But that's not true. Different people have different communication styles. Some people are reflective. Some people batch their texts. Some people show love through *presence* rather than constant check-ins.

Here's the tool: **The Behavior Pattern Check.** Instead of looking at one text, look at the pattern over two weeks:
- Do they follow through on plans?
- Do they ask about your day?
- Do they show up when it matters?
- Are they apologetic when they drop the ball?

If the answer is yes to most of these, they're secure or avoidant—not dangerous. They're just *different*. And difference is not a red flag.

Scenario Three, from Morgan (different Morgan):

*'I'm dating someone and they have this habit: they get defensive when I bring up anything slightly critical. Like, if I say, 'Hey, I felt hurt when you didn't ask about my presentation,' they immediately get angry or shut down. It doesn't feel safe. Is this a red flag?'*

Morgan, this is a *red flag*. Not because defensiveness is shameful. But because defensiveness + lack of repair = a pattern where you can't be honest about your feelings. And intimacy *requires* that you can be honest.

Here's the pattern: Healthy people can hear criticism. They might feel hurt, but they can stay engaged. Unhealthy people defend, blame, or withdraw. And if they can't recover from a difficult conversation, the relationship can't deepen.

The tool: **The Repair Test.** After a conflict, does this person:
- Apologize, even if it's not fully their fault? (Not fake apology—real accountability.)
- Ask what you need?
- Change, even slightly?

If no to all three, they're showing you that conflict = punishment in their nervous system. Red flag. Because conflict is *inevitable* in relationships. If they can't handle it, the relationship won't survive.

The crucial distinction: **You can't fix a red flag.** You can work on an anxiety signal. If someone is quiet but consistent, you can learn to trust that. If someone is defensive, *they* have to do the work. You can't make them safe."

---

**[CALLER Q&A / EXPERT RIFF – 2:30]**

**HOST:** "Voicemail from a listener named Chris: 'Morgan, I have really bad anxiety. How do I know if my gut feeling is intuition or just my brain malfunctioning?'

This is *the* question. And here's the truth: if you have anxiety or trauma, your threat-detection system is *overactive*. It's like your amygdala has a smoke detector that's too sensitive—every little thing sets it off.

So here's how to calibrate it: **Ask yourself three things:**

1. **Is this specific or vague?** Real red flags are specific. 'They lied about their job' is specific. 'I have a weird feeling' is vague. Vague usually means anxiety.

2. **Can other people see it?** Show the situation to a trusted friend with no attachment wounds. If they also think it's a red flag, it probably is. If they say, 'Seems fine to me,' you might be spiraling.

3. **Is there a pattern?** One incident might be a mistake. A pattern is a personality.

And here's the part I really want you to hear: Even if you have severe anxiety, your body is *still* picking up on something real. It might be magnifying it. But there's usually a kernel of truth.

So don't dismiss your gut. Just get curious about it. Journal about it. Talk to a therapist about it. Give yourself permission to investigate instead of rushing to conclusions."

**[ACTIONABLE TAKEAWAY – 1:00]**

**HOST:** "Here's your tool for this week: **The Red Flag vs. Anxiety Decoder.**

2. **Can I point to a pattern?** (Did this happen once or multiple times?)

4. **What do I need to feel safe?** (Do I need them to explain? Do I need reassurance? Do I need space?)

5. **Can they give me what I need?** (Have they shown they can change in the past?)

If you can answer all five, you'll know. And if you still can't decide, here's permission: You don't have to date them while you figure it out. Your gut doesn't need to be 100% sure. It just needs to be suspicious enough that you're willing to slow down."

## 4. Tone + Voice Guidelines

- **Conversational:** Like talking to a smart friend who happens to have a therapy degree. Use contractions. Use occasional humor (not forced). Acknowledge the messiness of dating.

- **Occasional self-disclosure:** Host has *experience*. It's okay to reference past dating struggles or therapy breakthroughs. Builds trust and relatability. But keep it brief—this is about the *listener*, not the host.
- **Empathy-first:** Always lead with validation. 'This is painful. This is normal. Here's what's happening.'
