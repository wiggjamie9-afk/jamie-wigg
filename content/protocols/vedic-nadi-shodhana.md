## Mythology

The Sanskrit translates as "channel purifying." In the Hatha Yoga Pradīpikā, written in the fifteenth century but compiling much older oral material, the body is described as a network of seventy-two thousand subtle channels — *nadis* — through which the breath, or *prana*, moves. Two of them dominate: *ida*, the lunar channel that runs along the left side, and *pingala*, the solar channel along the right.

When the breath flows unevenly between the nostrils — and it does, in most people, on a roughly ninety-minute cycle called the *nasal cycle* — the body is described as out of balance. Nadi Shodhana balances it by deliberate alternation.

What surprised the early anatomists was that the yogis had described something real.

## Mechanism

Nadi Shodhana is alternate-nostril breathing. One nostril is closed gently with a thumb or finger while the other is breathed through; the closure alternates each cycle.

The mechanism rests on two physical realities:

1. **The nasal cycle.** The nasal turbinates engorge and decongest in a slow rhythm controlled by the autonomic nervous system. Right-nostril dominance correlates with sympathetic activity; left-nostril dominance with parasympathetic. Forcing alternation engages both branches.

2. **Slow nasal breathing.** Nasal breathing is slower than mouth breathing by structural necessity, and nasal slow breathing has been shown in HRV studies (Telles et al., 2014) to increase parasympathetic indices and decrease heart rate compared to baseline.

The 4-4-6 ratio favours a slightly longer exhale, biasing toward parasympathetic recovery while preserving the balancing structure of the alternation.

Subjective effects commonly reported: a centred clarity, reduced rumination, an evened-out sense of the body's left and right halves.

It is a practice, not a treatment. Skip this protocol if you have a heavy cold or congested sinuses — alternate-nostril work requires clear passages.

## Script

```
[00:00] — opening

Bring your right hand to your face. The thumb closes the right nostril.
The ring finger closes the left. The other fingers rest on the forehead.

If holding the hand to the face is uncomfortable, you can swap hands
or use a different finger combination. The technique is the same.

[00:30] — first round

Close the right nostril with your thumb. Breathe in through the left
for four counts.

In. Two. Three. Four.

Close both nostrils. Hold for four.

Hold. Two. Three. Four.

Release the right. Breathe out through the right for six counts.

Out. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

[01:00] — reverse

Now in through the right.

In. Two. Three. Four.

Hold both. Two. Three. Four.

Out through the left. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

That is one full cycle. We'll do eighteen.

[01:30] — guided fade

Stay with the alternation. If you lose track of which side you're on,
exhale fully through both and start fresh on the left.

[02:00] — silent practice with pacer

(narration silent, pacer + frequency layer only, 4 minutes)

[06:00] — close

Drop the hand. Two normal breaths through both nostrils together.

Notice the difference between left and right. They should feel more
even than when you started.
```

## Practice notes

This is the most tactile of the Codex protocols — your hand is on your face the whole time. Some users find this uncomfortable at first. The discomfort fades inside a few sessions.

Beginners sometimes get confused about which side they're on. The pacer in the app will show a left/right indicator alongside the breath count. If you're learning, watch the screen for the first round and close your eyes for the rest.

Don't strain the holds. The four-count hold is a target, not a maximum.
