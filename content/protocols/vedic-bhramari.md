## Mythology

*Bhramari* — from the Sanskrit *bhramara*, "bumblebee." The yogis named it after the sound it makes.

The texts are clear: this is the breath you use when nothing else is working. The Gheranda Samhita and the Hatha Yoga Pradīpikā both place it in the section reserved for practitioners who need to settle the mind directly, immediately, in the body, without intellectual setup. The bee breath is described as the practice that quiets the inner chatter "as if a curtain were drawn across it."

Modern research caught up. Humming, it turns out, does several measurable things to the body that no other contemplative practice replicates.

## Mechanism

Bhramari is a slow nasal inhale followed by a sustained humming exhale through the nose. The mechanisms are stacked:

1. **Vagal stimulation through laryngeal vibration.** Humming activates the vagus nerve via afferents in the laryngopharynx. Studies have documented increased HRV indices and reduced sympathetic activity following humming protocols.

2. **Nitric oxide production.** Eddahibi et al. (2002) and Lundberg et al. (2003) demonstrated that humming dramatically increases nitric oxide production in the paranasal sinuses — by roughly fifteen-fold compared to quiet exhalation. Nitric oxide has vasodilatory and bronchodilatory effects.

3. **Auditory feedback loop.** The hum is heard via bone conduction as well as air conduction, producing a continuous internal sound that anchors attention. The Sanskrit texts describe this as "filling the mind so it has nowhere to go."

The 4-second inhale and 8-second hum produces three breaths per minute with a sustained 2:1 exhale-to-inhale ratio, both individually associated with parasympathetic dominance.

Subjective effects commonly reported: a strong "settling" in the chest and head, a buzzing sensation in the face and lips, immediate quieting of mental noise.

It is a practice, not a treatment. The hum should be comfortable — do not strain the voice. If you experience throat irritation, shorten the hum or skip a session.

## Script

```
[00:00] — opening

This is the bee breath. You will hum. Not loud — somewhere between a
spoken voice and a whisper. Through the nose. The lips stay closed.

If you're somewhere you can't make sound, save this protocol for later
and use the Vagus Release instead.

[00:30] — settle

Four counts in through the nose. Eight counts humming out through the
nose. Twenty-five rounds.

[00:50] — cycle 1, demonstration

Inhale slowly. Two. Three. Four.

Now hum on the exhale. (the narrator demonstrates: "mmmmmmmm" for eight
seconds, soft, low pitch)

That's one cycle. Again.

[01:30] — guided fade

Stay with the hum. Find a pitch your face can feel. Most people land
somewhere between the spoken pitch of their voice and a third below it.

If your throat starts to tickle, lighten the hum. The goal is the
vibration in the face, not volume.

[02:00] — silent practice with pacer

(narration silent, pacer + frequency layer only — user hums; 3 minutes)

[05:00] — close

Two breaths without the hum, through the nose, at your own pace.

Notice the sensation in your face. Around the lips, the cheeks, the
forehead. That's the residue of the practice.
```

## Practice notes

Find your pitch. Too high and the throat strains; too low and the vibration is faint. Most users settle a few notes below their normal speaking pitch.

The vibration in the face is the marker of the protocol working. If you can't feel it, lengthen the hum slightly or close your mouth more firmly so more of the airflow exits the nose.

Don't use Bhramari in public unless you've practiced it enough to keep the hum quiet. The "right" volume in private is wrong in public.
