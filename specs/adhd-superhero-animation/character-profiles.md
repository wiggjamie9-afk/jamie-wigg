# Character Profiles: SURGE

## Protagonist: Ziggy Chen

**Age:** 10  
**Grade:** 5th (age 10–11)  
**Setting:** Suburban Midwest (Des Moines, Iowa area)  
**Family:** Lives with Mom (graphic designer, self-employed; also ADHD, undiagnosed) and younger sister Maya (8, neurotypical, organized)  

---

### ADHD Profile

Ziggy has a mixed-presentation ADHD profile: significant inattention, moderate hyperactivity, and prominent emotional dysregulation. He reads at a 2nd-grade level despite being intelligent—words blur and rearrange on the page, and his brain pulls him toward movement and novelty instead of letters. Sensory noise hijacks him: the fluorescent hum in Mrs. Henderson's classroom, fan clicks, pencil taps, the scrape of chairs. He experiences severe time dilation in uninteresting moments (19 minutes feels like three hours) but can hyperfocus on soccer drills, gaming, and puzzles for hours without noticing time pass. Transitions devastate him—switching from recess to math without warning triggers panic and shutdown. His emotional responses are outsized compared to peers: a small criticism spirals into shame; a moment of joy is explosive laughter and physical exuberance. He fidgets constantly (picking at seams, bouncing, rocking) and needs proprioceptive input to regulate—sports are his lifeline. Loses permission slips, forgets locker combinations, leaves homework on the bus. **Strengths coexist with every struggle:** Ziggy solves math problems instantly when under pressure or in hyperfocus (pattern recognition at light speed). He reads people's emotions with uncanny accuracy—knows when Mrs. Henderson is frustrated, when Jake is worried, when Mom is overwhelmed before they say anything. His physical coordination is exceptional; he moves through space with confidence and grace despite constant fidgeting. He's creatively inventive (builds elaborate Lego worlds, improvises games with detailed rule sets). His energy, when channeled, electrifies a room—he's the kid everyone wants on their soccer team and the one who thinks of the wildest game ideas at recess.

---

### Motivation & Series Arc

Ziggy wants to belong—to feel at home in his own body, accepted at school (especially by teachers who see effort not failure), and understood by his mom. This season (Pilot + S1 episodes) charts his movement from **Discovery** (realizes his brain isn't broken, just wired differently) through **Acceptance** (learning that his struggles and strengths come from the same place—the Surge) to **Mastery** (figuring out how to use both sides to help others and navigate the world). By the series finale, Ziggy isn't "cured" or "fixed"—he still loses permission slips, still struggles with reading, still melts down—but he's stopped hating himself for it. Future seasons evolve this: S2 explores hyperfocus as a superpower in academics; S3 tackles identity (What does ADHD mean for who I want to be?); S4 deepens peer/social navigaion (friendship conflict, bullying, fitting in); S5+ moves toward legacy (helping younger kids, mentorship, understanding mom's own undiagnosed journey).

---

### Superpower as Metaphor

**Surge** is kinetic energy: hyperfocus compressed into electric speed, pattern recognition firing like lightning, emotional intensity radiating outward as light. When Ziggy enters Surge form, the world slows and clarifies—he reads situations instantly, solves problems in seconds, moves with impossible grace. **Why it matters:** The exact behaviors school punishes (fidgeting instead of sitting still, switching focus instead of sustained attention, feeling emotions intensely instead of measured response) are the fuel of Surge. The superpower says: "Your brain isn't broken. It's built for a different kind of speed." By making hyperfocus and rapid pattern-reading literal superpowers, the show validates that these aren't deficits—they're locked potential.

### Voice & Casting Direction

**Age dynamic:** Fully 10-year-old intelligence and vocabulary—not younger, not affected—but hyperaware of being different from peers; mature in some ways (emotionally intuitive, quick-thinking), immature in others (needs physical play, impulsive, struggles with delayed gratification).

**Energy:** High, restless, kinetic—but NOT constant chaos. Speech is fast when interested/anxious; slower and focused during hyperfocus or when genuinely listening. Ziggy jumps between ideas mid-sentence when his brain is moving fast, but he can also sustain focus on things he cares about. Avoid playing him as a hyperactivity caricature; instead, play him as an intelligent kid whose energy responds to interest and nervous system state. When nervous or shame-spiraling, he becomes quieter, more monotone (the energy drains). In Surge form, his voice is confident, quick, almost musical.

**Humor:** Self-deprecating (jokes about losing things, "my brain is weird"), sarcastic when nervous (using humor to deflect shame), genuine belly-laugh joy when happy or playing with Jake. Not cynical—just using humor to manage dysregulation.

**Interior monologue:** Frustrated with himself, narrating failure in real-time ("I'm doing it again," "why can't I just focus?"), aware of struggle without pathologizing. Never says "my brain is broken" or self-hating language; instead, confused, exasperated, resigned ("this is so hard").

**Voice quality:** Bright, quick-paced, slightly nasal (Midwest accent, not exaggerated—Des Moines neutral), warm underneath the fidget. Can shift into almost-robotic precision when hyperfocused (reading soccer stats, describing a Lego build). Interior monologue is softer, faster, sometimes overlapping with action.

**Accent/dialect:** Midwestern American English, subtle Des Moines accent (slight "oh" sound in words like "go" → "gah"; "about" → "aboot" very faintly). No caricature—just grounding. Vocabulary is contemporary (2024+) with natural kid phrases ("that's so mid," "lowkey," mixed with older expressions his mom uses).

**Voice actor direction (Sensitivity Review note):** You are a smart, capable 10-year-old with a brain that works fast. You're not "silly" or "manic"; you're present and responsive. Your speed comes from genuine thinking, not randomness. When anxious, you talk faster and trail off. When engaged, you're clear and purposeful. When hyperfocused (soccer, interest), your voice gets almost musical—organized, precise, confident. Avoid defaulting to a stereotyped "ADHD kid voice"; instead, play Ziggy as an intelligent, capable person whose energy reflects his genuine internal state and level of engagement.

**Voice actor reference:** Seeking 9–11 year old with athletic, energetic presence; natural delivery (not overly theatrical); can shift from rapid-fire energy to vulnerable quiet; slight Midwestern inflection; able to sustain interior monologue without sounding narrated; must avoid caricature and play intelligence/capability as the baseline.

### Family

**Mom (Sarah Chen):** Graphic designer, self-employed, early 40s. Smart, loving, stretched thin—trying to balance work-from-home chaos with two kids' needs. She doesn't have a diagnosis herself, but she recognizes pieces of Ziggy in herself (hyperfocus marathons, time blindness, emotional intensity, struggles with morning routines). She's not in denial about Ziggy's ADHD—she's observed his struggles—but she's afraid of labels and medication; she grew up with a parent who over-medicated her. She loves Ziggy fiercely but sometimes gets frustrated with him ("Just focus," "Why are you always losing things?"), not understanding that shame compounds the struggle. By mid-season, she begins to suspect she might have ADHD too. She's the emotional anchor for viewers who are ADHD parents worried they're failing their kids.

**Maya (sister, 8):** Organized, rule-following, slightly anxious. She loves her brother but finds him chaotic and embarrassing sometimes. She's the foil: what comes "naturally" to her (sitting still, remembering instructions, finishing homework without fuss) is her brother's daily battle. By the pilot's end, she glimpses that Ziggy isn't broken—just different—and becomes an unlikely ally.

**Dad (mentioned, not primary):** Lives in another state; they video-call weekly. Affectionate, but his absence is a thread (Ziggy wonders if his ADHD is "too much" and that's why). This deepens the shame spiral in early episodes.

# Supporting Cast

## Mrs. Henderson (Teacher)

5th-grade teacher, mid-50s, Gen X energy, 19 years in public school (exhausted but still trying). She's well-meaning—genuinely wants every kid to succeed—but she's overwhelmed: 28 kids, IEP cases she doesn't have training for, reading standards that demand accountability. She notices Ziggy's behavior (not paying attention, fidgeting, social conflicts) but interprets it as laziness or acting out, not neurodifference. When he can't sit through silent reading, she sees disrespect. When he blurts out answers, she sees impulse control failure. She's not cruel; she's just operating from a deficit model without language for neurodiversity. **Her conflict with Ziggy:** She's desperate for him to "just focus" during reading time so the class can move forward; his struggles slow the pace and disrupt the routine. She's also hurt when Ziggy seems to not listen to her (when really, auditory processing + distraction has him lost in the first three words). **What she wants:** For Ziggy to succeed, and for her classroom to run smoothly; for him to care as much as she does. Her small moment of genuine belief in Ziggy—seeing him solve a problem in his own way—is the emotional lynchpin of the pilot. **Voice direction:** Tired kindness, slight exasperation (not meanness), Gen X teacher cadence. Uses soft redirection ("Ziggy, eyes on your book, please") and the phrase "just focus" so often it becomes tragic. Midwest accent, middle-aged voice, slightly husky (teacher voice strain). Her interior monologue (when we hear it) is frustrated but self-aware ("I wish I knew how to help him").

---

## Jake (Peer / Best Friend)

10 years old, Ziggy's soccer teammate and best friend. Neurotypical, enthusiastic, straightforward—the kid for whom school and sports come without friction. He loves Ziggy without qualification: thinks he's the coolest, funniest, most athletic kid in 5th grade. Doesn't understand why Ziggy struggles with reading or sitting still, but he doesn't judge it either—it's just part of Ziggy being Ziggy. **His conflict with Ziggy:** When Ziggy shame-spirals or gets frustrated, Jake doesn't know how to help and sometimes inadvertently makes it worse ("Just try harder," "it's not that hard"). He also occasionally feels hurt when Ziggy snaps at him or doesn't listen. **What he wants:** For Ziggy to be happy, for them to keep winning at soccer together, for things to be fun. **Voice direction:** High-energy, genuine enthusiasm, younger-sounding than Ziggy (has a boyish clarity to his voice—not breathy, but lighter), straightforward vocabulary and emotional expression. If the show is set in US Midwest, he has the same gentle Midwest accent as Ziggy; if set elsewhere, adapt accordingly. Genuine laugh, uncomplicated joy. No sarcasm or irony—Jake means what he says. Voice actor: 9–11 year old, athletic build, naturally warm and encouraging without being saccharine.

---

## Sarah Chen (Mom)

Ziggy's mom, early 40s, graphic designer working from home. Loving but exasperated; proud of Ziggy's athletic talents and quick thinking, worried sick about his school struggles and emotional meltdowns. She doesn't have a formal ADHD diagnosis herself, but she's starting to wonder: she hyperfocuses on design projects until 2 AM, loses track of time, struggles with mornings, feels things intensely. She avoids labels because her own mother medicated her daughter's childhood away, and Sarah's determined not to do that to Ziggy. Her conflict with Ziggy isn't malice—it's frustration born from her own unmet needs (she's also ADHD, also dysregulated, also stretched thin). "Why are you always losing things?" comes from a place of genuine overwhelm, not cruelty. **What she wants:** For Ziggy to be happy and to stop struggling; for school to be easier (and for her to stop getting worried emails from teachers); secretly, for herself to feel less broken. **Voice direction:** Warm, slightly harried (background life noise when she's on work calls), Midwest accent, early-40s woman tone (not young, not old). When she's frustrated, her voice rises slightly; when she's vulnerable (realizing her own ADHD), she gets quieter, more reflective. Interior monologue is self-critical and loving simultaneously. Voice actor: mid-40s to early 50s woman with Midwest inflection, able to convey both exasperation and deep love in the same line.

---

## Sage (Fantasy Mentor / Inner Guide)

A figure who appears in Ziggy's Surge-world sequences: part wise elder, part imagined version of someone real (unclear at first—by mid-season, revealed to be Ziggy's memory of his Grandpa Chen, who died before Ziggy was born, reconstructed from stories and photos). In the fantasy realm, Sage is calm, steady, and deeply believing in Ziggy's power—the opposite of the internal shame voice and Mrs. Henderson's doubt. Sage guides Ziggy through early Surge discoveries and offers the reframe: "Your brain isn't broken. It's built for speed." Sage never explains or diagnoses; instead, asks questions that help Ziggy see his own strength ("What did you do differently in that moment?" "Why do you think that was easy?"). **Motivation:** To help Ziggy stop hating himself and recognize his power. **Voice direction:** Older, calm, assured (not condescending—fully respecting Ziggy's intelligence), slightly formal in speech but warm. Slow delivery compared to Ziggy's rapid-fire. Accent: subtle East Asian American (reflecting Ziggy's heritage), warm timbre, could be 60s-70s voice actor or younger performer pitched/processed to sound older. No folksy clichés—Sage speaks simply and directly. Interior monologue (rare) is kind and patient.
