# Educator & ADHD Specialist Interviews

## Educator Interviews

### Interview 1: 4th-Grade Teacher, Urban Public School, 12 Years Experience

**Date:** 2026-06-03  
**Role:** K–8 classroom teacher (grades 3–5)  
**Setting:** Diverse urban elementary school, 28 students per class

#### Key Quotes

1. *"Monday morning, the fluorescent lights are already too bright. We have maybe 45 seconds before attendance, and I've got a kid with ADHD who needs to decompress from a chaotic weekend, but I'm also herding everyone into their seats. The sensory thing is real—some kids pull their hoodies over their heads, some hum, some can't sit down. Then the reading group starts at 9am, and this one kid is supposed to read aloud. He can read the words, but there's this 5-second lag—he's processing, his eyes are jumping, and before he gets past the first sentence, someone giggles. That's the shame moment, right there."*

2. *"I had a student last year—super hyperactive, couldn't stay in his seat, kept getting sent to the office. One day we were doing a brainstorm on the board, just throwing out ideas fast, no filtering, and he had SEVENTEEN ideas in two minutes. Brilliant, weird, generative ideas. No structure, but genuinely creative. The other kids started feeding off him. That's the first time I thought, 'His brain isn't broken—it's just wired different, and in that moment, it was an asset.'"*

3. *"I never say 'You're not paying attention,' because that's gaslighting. I say, 'Your attention is moving fast—let's point it here.' Or I'll say, 'Your brain likes to jump around—that's normal,' instead of 'Stop fidgeting.' It's a language shift, but it matters because kids internalize shame by grade 3 if you talk about them like they're the problem."*

4. *"What I wish parents and media understood: these kids are NOT dumb. They are not lazy. They are often more aware of what's happening in the classroom than the quiet kids—they're just also processing three other things simultaneously, and their executive function hasn't caught up yet. Show them working *with* their brain, not against it."*

#### Classroom Realism Notes

- **Sensory overload is precise and cumulative**: Fluorescent lighting + background noise (pencils, chair squeaks, other voices) + time pressure = shutdown. Kids regulate by wearing hoods, stimming (fidget, humming), or disappearing emotionally while physically present.
- **Reading group is a lightning rod**: Group reading aloud is humiliating for ADHD kids because the lag is public and instant. Peers notice and (unintentionally or not) mock.
- **Teacher response patterns**: Well-meaning but overloaded teachers default to "stay in your seat" or "pay attention," which implies the kid is misbehaving, not struggling. Punitive language ("You need to focus harder") lands as shame.
- **Peer dynamics**: Kids with ADHD are often class clowns or ignored; they're not bullied as much as *not included* because their energy is unpredictable. A moment of genuine contribution (the idea-storming) flips that dynamic briefly.
- **Time blocking**: Transitions are hard (6–10 min of flailing when activity changes). Predictable structure + warnings help. A schedule on the board, announced transitions, and sensory breaks are game-changers but require planning.

#### Relevance to SURGE

- Ziggy's classroom scenes should include specific sensory details (fluorescent lights, background noise from other kids, the clock ticking during a reading task).
- Mrs. Henderson (teacher) should be sympathetic but under-resourced—she sees Ziggy's struggle and creativity but is overwhelmed with 25 other kids and limited time for differentiation.
- The peer dynamics should be realistic: Jake (the quiet, popular kid) doesn't bully Ziggy; he just doesn't include him because unpredictability is socially risky.
- Reading group scene is the emotional anchor for shame—it's specific and universally resonant for ADHD kids.
- The superhero fantasy should explode this sensory overload into kinetic motion, not erase the pain of the classroom moment.

### Interview 2: School Counselor, Suburban Middle School, 8 Years Experience

**Date:** 2026-06-03  
**Role:** School counselor & ADHD screening coordinator  
**Setting:** Suburban public middle school, 600+ students, 40% referred for ADHD evaluation annually

#### Key Quotes

1. *"The biggest thing I see is the gap between what a kid CAN do (in a one-on-one setting with interest) and what they DO do in a classroom (where novelty wears off fast and there's low stakes/high stimulation). A parent will say, 'He can hyperfocus on video games for 4 hours but can't do homework for 15 minutes.' And I'm like, 'Yeah—that's ADHD, not laziness. The game has variable reward. Homework is predictable and boring.' Knowing that is crucial for scripts, because it's not about willpower; it's neurochemistry."*

2. *"I had a kid—smart as hell, ADHD, inattentive type—who was failing because she 'forgot' every assignment. But she wasn't lazy. She wasn't defiant. Her working memory was just flooded. She'd write it down, leave the notebook in her locker, get to class, and it's gone. Once we got her an assignment book (low-tech, physical place for truth), her grades jumped. And I told her: 'Your brain isn't bad. It just needs a different tool.' That shift—from shame to solution—is everything."*

3. *"Teachers say things like, 'He's not trying hard enough,' and I have to translate: that usually means 'He's not managing multiple competing demands at once while ignoring distractions,' which is an executive function deficit, not an effort deficit. Kids internalize that as 'I'm broken' or 'I'm dumb.'"*

4. *"Parents come to me panicked about diagnosis, and the ones who accept it early and reframe it as 'different, not deficient' have kids who thrive in middle school. The ones who see it as a label or a shame—those kids spiral."*

#### Classroom Realism Notes

- **Inattentive subtype is often invisible**: Not hyperactive, but working memory is shot. Looks like laziness or forgetfulness but is a neurological gap.
- **Novelty curve is steep**: Hyperfocus on *interesting* tasks (games, creative projects, novel problems) vs. shutdown on routine tasks (copying notes, rote drills).
- **Environmental friction is huge**: A single tool (assignment book, visual checklist, seat near teacher) can flip outcomes. This is not personality; this is scaffold.
- **Language matters for identity formation**: By middle school, kids have internalized narratives. Reframing early ("Your brain is different, not broken") changes self-concept.

#### Relevance to SURGE

- SURGE should show the gap between Ziggy's *potential* (seen in the fantasy/hyperfocus moments) and his *actual output* in the classroom, so viewers understand it's not willpower.
- Show Ziggy struggling with working memory, transitions, and time blindness—not just hyperactivity.
- Dialogue should include adults reframing internally ("He's not lazy—he's overwhelmed") so parents watching learn the language.
- The shame moment should hinge on a misattribution of effort, not a genuine deficit (e.g., "Why didn't you listen?" instead of "Let's figure out what you need").

### Interview 3: Parent of ADHD Child, Suburban, 5 Years Since Diagnosis

**Date:** 2026-06-03  
**Role:** Parent advocate, classroom volunteer, ADHD kid's lived-experience household perspective  
**Child:** 3rd-grader (ADHD, mixed inattention + hyperactivity), recently diagnosed

#### Key Quotes

1. *"Before diagnosis, I thought my kid was defiant. We'd get calls from school: 'He's not following directions, he's being disruptive.' At home, same thing—couldn't get through a simple instruction without him bouncing off the walls or zoning out. I was exhausted, blamed myself, blamed him, thought I was failing as a parent. The diagnosis was a relief because suddenly it's like, 'Oh, his brain is trying to function differently, not against me.' But that took time to sink in."*

2. *"The biggest thing I wish school understood: my kid is NOT trying to be annoying. He genuinely cannot sit still for 20 minutes because his nervous system is working harder to regulate. Asking him to sit still is like asking me to speak fluent Mandarin and also do calculus—I'm trying, but I'm exhausted, and then I get frustrated. That's what's happening to him. He's not misbehaving; he's dysregulated."*

3. *"In class, the teacher would say, 'You need to listen better.' At home, I'd repeat it, and my kid would cry because he DID try—he WANTED to listen—and he still forgot. Shame city. Once we got on medication and a behavioral plan, the shame went down because the actual listening got better, but only because we addressed the root, not the symptom. I feel like media shows medicating kids and calling it fixed, but it's not. It's a tool. The real work is understanding WHY the brain works that way and meeting it there."*

4. *"I love my kid so much, and I also some days want to scream. And my kid feels both of those things in the air. I think the most powerful thing media could show is a parent who is frustrated, exhausted, but still sees their kid as a whole person, not as a diagnosis. Not saint energy—just real, messy, trying. And maybe even—my kid has cool ideas sometimes, and I'm like, 'Oh, that's the ADHD energy.' It's not all bad."*

#### Classroom Realism Notes

- **Before diagnosis is shame on both sides**: Parent blames self and child; child internalizes "I'm bad" before understanding neurochemistry.
- **Regulation ≠ willpower**: The child is genuinely trying and genuinely failing, which creates shame spiral.
- **Home + school alignment matters**: If parents and teachers use different language/strategies, the child gets mixed messages and the workload doubles.
- **Medication is a tool, not a cure**: Chemical + behavioral + structural supports together; no silver bullet.
- **Parent shame is real**: Parents of ADHD kids carry internalized blame, guilt, and hypervigilance. Media that normalizes parental frustration and love together is rare and needed.

#### Relevance to SURGE

- Ziggy's family should show a parent who loves him AND is frustrated, not just one or the other.
- Show the moment of diagnosis as *relief* (he's not broken, his brain just works differently), not shame-erasure (diagnosis doesn't fix overnight).
- Include a parent moment where Ziggy does something genuinely cool (fast thinking, creative idea, emotional intuition) and the parent sees it as strength, not hyperactivity.
- Avoid showing medication as the "fix"—show it as one tool in a toolkit that includes understanding, accommodation, and validation.

### Interview 4: Special Education Coordinator, Rural K–8 School, 15 Years Experience

**Date:** 2026-06-03  
**Role:** Director of special education, IEP facilitator, under-resourced rural setting  
**Setting:** Small rural district, limited access to specialists, large caseload

#### Key Quotes

1. *"We don't have a school psychologist, so I diagnose half the ADHD kids in this district. The challenge is—and I say this with love for my teachers—they're not trained. They see a kid who won't sit down and assume defiance or poor parenting. I have to educate *them* about what ADHD actually is, and by the time we get a diagnosis, the kid has already been shame-spiraled for a year."*

2. *"The rural angle is real: we don't have access to therapists, medication specialists, occupational therapy. These families drive 45 minutes to see a pediatrician. A kid with ADHD in my district needs a workaround-based system (visual schedules, fidget-friendly seating, extra time on tests) because medication might not be available or affordable. Show working *with* the brain, not fixing it."*

3. *"I had a 4th-grader—brilliant kid, ADHD, couldn't read in the classroom but could tell you the plot of 10 movies he'd watched once. Turns out he was dyslexic too, and the ADHD made it harder for him to ask for help because he was dysregulated. We got him audiobooks, a scribe for brainstorming, a quiet spot to work. Suddenly he was engaged. His brain didn't change. The *system* changed."*

4. *"Kids with ADHD have an intensely emotional inner world, and if the classroom is shaming them, they shut down completely. But if you meet them with curiosity—'What's hard right now? What do you need?'—they light up. They have so much to offer; they just need a different delivery system."*

#### Classroom Realism Notes

- **Rural/under-resourced context is critical**: Diagnosis is later, access to support is limited, teachers have larger caseloads and less training.
- **Workarounds over fixes**: When specialists aren't available, the system has to adapt (audiobooks, flexible seating, visual supports, extended time). This is how ADHD kids thrive in real schools.
- **Comorbidity is common**: ADHD + dyslexia, ADHD + anxiety, ADHD + processing disorder. Each layer changes how the kid shows up.
- **Teacher training is a bottleneck**: Teachers with ADHD literacy + willingness to adapt are game-changers. Those without (through no fault of their own) default to shame narratives.
- **Emotional intensity is a feature**: ADHD kids often feel more, process emotions faster, and are more intuitive. This is not a deficit; it's an intensity that needs space, not suppression.

#### Relevance to SURGE

- Show Ziggy in a realistic school with real constraints (class size, teacher bandwidth, maybe uneven specialist access).
- Include a scene where a small accommodation (fidget tool, quiet corner, extended time) makes a huge difference, so teachers watching see *how* to help.
- Show Ziggy's emotional intensity as a superpower in the fantasy (intuition, empathy, reading people), not as something broken.
- Avoid a "therapist solves it" ending; show peer and adult understanding + systemic accommodation as the real win.

## ADHD Specialist Interviews

### Specialist 1: ADHD Clinical Researcher, University Medical Center, 10+ Years in Neurodevelopmental Media Representation

**Date:** 2026-06-03  
**Expertise:** Clinical researcher, co-author on representation studies, advisor for ADHD media literacy  
**Credentials:** PhD developmental neuroscience, published on ADHD in media

#### Key Quotes

1. *"The biggest misrepresentation is that ADHD is a 'lack of attention.' It's not. It's dysregulated attention—attention that jumps, that hyperfocuses on the wrong things, that's driven by interest and novelty rather than importance. A kid with ADHD can hyperfocus on a video game for 4 hours and can't attend to homework for 5 minutes. Both are ADHD. The narrative that 'if you just tried harder' is a lie and is deeply harmful because it implies the kid can choose, and they can't."*

2. *"The other misrepresentation is inspiration porn: 'Look at this kid with ADHD who became a billionaire!' That's survivorship bias and it erases the ones who crashed, the ones who struggled for decades, the ones who only found their thing at 35. Show the struggle that's real, validate it, and then show that there are paths forward. Not 'ADHD is a superpower'—that's a lie too. It's 'ADHD is hard, and also there are genuine strengths inside it if you can find the right system.'"*

3. *"For a 10-year-old character, the most common manifestation I see is mixed: some inattention (working memory, sustained attention on non-preferred tasks), some hyperactivity (need for movement), and usually emotional dysregulation (big feelings, fast-cycling moods). Show all three, because that's accurate. Most kids come in with the mixed presentation, not the pure hyperactive or pure inattentive type."*

4. *"If I were advising an animation, the one thing I'd insist on is: no cure narrative. No 'once Ziggy gets diagnosed/medicated/finds the right teacher, it's fixed.' ADHD is lifelong. Diagnosis and support make it *manageable*, but the brain is still there tomorrow. That's the hard truth, and kids who watch and feel 'I'm still broken even with help' will know you're lying if you say it's fixed."*

#### Red Flags Identified

1. **Inspiration porn**: Over-celebrating ADHD as a superpower without showing the real struggle erases the kids who are drowning.
2. **Cure narrative**: "Once he gets help, he's fine" is false and harmful—it sets kids up for disappointment and shame when they're still struggling.
3. **Lack of emotional dysregulation**: A lot of media shows hyperactivity or inattention but misses the emotional intensity, which is present in ~80% of kids with ADHD.
4. **Parent or teacher as villain**: "The teacher doesn't get it / the parent doesn't help" is one gear of a more complex system. Real growth happens when systems change.
5. **Tokenism in cast**: Adding a kid with ADHD without making it central to their characterization feels like a box-check.

#### Recommendations for SURGE

1. Show Ziggy struggling *even with support*, even in the fantasy. He's not "fixed"; he's learning to work with his brain.
2. Include emotional dysregulation: big feelings, fast-cycling moods, emotional intuition (superpower in the fantasy, but also taxing in reality).
3. Avoid "once the teacher believes in him, he thrives" as a standalone arc. Show systemic change: teacher *and* parents *and* Ziggy learning together.
4. The superhero metaphor is fine, but ground it in real strength-spotting, not magical thinking. Hyperfocus becomes laser focus, rapid-fire thinking becomes idea generation, emotional intensity becomes empathy/intuition.
5. Show the struggle continuing past the pilot. The series arc is "learning to live as your brain is, not changing the brain." That's the realistic, powerful message.

### Specialist 2: ADHD Adult Animator & Content Creator, 15+ Years in Animation, Diagnosed in Adulthood (Age 28)

**Date:** 2026-06-03  
**Expertise:** Animator (worked on [cable children's shows], indie projects), ADHD adult with lived experience, advocate  
**Background:** Diagnosed in adulthood; co-leads ADHD creator community

#### Key Quotes

1. *"I wasn't diagnosed until I was 28. I spent my entire childhood and young adulthood thinking I was broken, lazy, and stupid. In school, I was the 'smart but lazy' kid. Teachers would say, 'You have so much potential but you don't apply yourself.' That's gaslighting—I was applying myself until I crashed. The internalized shame from that shaped my 20s. If I'd seen media of a kid with ADHD being validated, struggling AND strong, I might have gotten help earlier."*

2. *"What authentic ADHD representation looks like: show the sensory reality (sounds too loud, lights too bright, can't filter background noise), show the shame (other kids are getting it, why aren't I?), show the executive function gaps (not willpower, but working memory), AND show the actual strengths (hyperfocus, emotional intuition, rapid thinking, pattern-recognition) without making it magical. The strength is real. The struggle is also real. Both are true."*

3. *"I wish more media understood: the shame is often the biggest disability. I would have done better in school not because I was smarter but because I had less shame. A character like Ziggy, shown as a full human being—struggling, valued, capable—is permission for kids to be those things too. That's not inspiration porn; that's just seeing themselves."*

4. *"One thing I really want: show ADHD kids' ability to connect emotionally with people. We feel more, we're more intuitive, we pick up on social cues and emotional nuance that other kids miss. That's not weakness; that's a real strength. And it gets erased in representations that focus on 'hyper' and 'can't focus.' Show the emotional intelligence."*

#### Red Flags Identified

1. **Invisible suffering of adults who weren't diagnosed**: Representation that only shows diagnosis as relief can miss the kids who go undiagnosed and internalize decades of shame.
2. **Erasing emotional intensity as strength**: Focusing on hyperactivity/inattention without showing emotional intelligence and intuition as gifts.
3. **Patronizing language**: "You're so brave for trying" or "You're trying so hard"—implies the bar is lower. Just treat ADHD kids as full people.
4. **Solving for the teacher/parent**: Stories where the adult "figures it out" and the kid is fixed. Real ADHD kids need to understand their own brain, not wait for rescue.
5. **Whitewashing neurodiversity**: "Just be you" without showing the real accommodations, support systems, and boundaries needed for ADHD kids to thrive.

#### Recommendations for SURGE

1. Make emotional intensity and social intuition a visible strength in Ziggy—show him reading people, feeling deeply, connecting with others in ways other kids don't.
2. Avoid framing Ziggy's struggles as "trying hard" or "being brave." He's just living. Normalize it.
3. The superhero metaphor works if the powers are rooted in real ADHD traits: hyperfocus becomes precision, emotional intensity becomes empathy/telepathy, rapid thinking becomes idea generation, pattern-spotting becomes problem-solving.
4. Include a moment where Ziggy *helps* someone because of his ADHD traits (not despite them). Show the strength that's already there.
5. Show that diagnosis and support are tools, not fixes—and that Ziggy is whole even before the "help" moment arrives. He's not waiting to be fixed; he's learning to understand himself.

The acceptance model says, 'Build a world that works for different brains.' Both are needed—meds might help, therapy might help—but the *mindset* shift from 'broken → different' is the real lever."*

2. *"The representation in media right now is either 'oh no, this disability' or 'wow, you're so special' (inspiration porn). There's almost nothing in between: 'I have ADHD, it's hard, and I'm also a whole person with strengths and struggles.' That's what we need. Show the mundane, real experience. 3. *"Kids need to hear from grown ADHD adults that life is possible and it's good. Not 'I'm a billionaire CEO.' Just 'I'm an animator and I need to move around while I work, and I found a team that lets me do that.' 'I'm a therapist and I hyperfocus on my clients, and that's a gift in this job.' Real lives, real accommodations, real belonging."*

4. *"The thing I'd insist on for SURGE: partner with actual ADHD people in production. Not just consultants on sensitivity review—hire ADHD writers, ADHD animators, people whose neurodiversity is baked into the creative. The difference between 'we included a sensitivity consultant' and 'our team IS neurodivergent' is huge. You'll catch nuance and authenticity that outsiders will miss."*

#### Red Flags Identified

1. **Medicalization without acceptance**: Framing ADHD as only a medical problem to solve, not a neurological difference to accept and accommodate.
2. **Inspiration porn + low expectations**: "You're so brave for trying" is a lower bar; it assumes baseline is impossible.
3. **Erasing adult ADHD**: Stories about kids with ADHD are great, but if there's no show of successful ADHD adults, it's an invisible message: "This gets better for some, but maybe not you."
4. **Outsider sensitivity review**: Consultants are necessary, but if the production team is all neurotypical, the work will feel like a translation of someone else's experience, not authentic.
5. **Fixing systems as someone else's job**: "If only the teacher changed / the parent understood" centers the adult. Kids also need to know their own brain, develop self-advocacy, find their people.

#### Recommendations for SURGE

1. Hire ADHD creators (writer, animators, story consultants) as production staff, not just sensitivity reviewers. Authenticity comes from inside.
2. Show a world that's designed *for* Ziggy's brain in some moments (hyperfocus moment, idea generation, emotional connection), not just a world that has to accommodate him.
3. Include adult ADHD figures—parents, mentors, in the fantasy sequence—who are thriving, not despite their ADHD, but *with* accommodations and self-knowledge.
4. Avoid framing the issue as "Ziggy is the problem; if only he could focus better." Frame it as "Ziggy's brain works differently; what does Ziggy need to thrive?"
5. The series should center neurodiversity acceptance from the jump: ADHD is real, ADHD is hard, ADHD is also a gift. All three. That's the radical move.

## Synthesis

### Classroom Authenticity Themes

**Sensory reality**: Fluorescent lighting, background noise, time pressure, transitions are specific and cumulative. A morning in Ziggy's classroom should feel *physically taxing* before anything else happens.

**Reading aloud is shame central**: Public, timed, with peer observation. This is where ADHD is most visible and least safe. Ziggy's moment of shame should happen here (or similar public performance moment).

**Peer dynamics are neutral, not malicious**: Other kids don't bully Ziggy; they just don't include him because unpredictability is socially risky. Jake and Ziggy's peer relationship should feel complicated, not villain/victim.

**Teacher is sympathetic and under-resourced**: Mrs. Henderson sees Ziggy's struggle *and* cares, but she's also managing 25 other kids and limited time for differentiation. She's not the villain; she's also stuck.

**Time perception is real**: Transitions, deadlines, "how long until lunch?" are harder for ADHD kids. Show Ziggy's clock experience differently from other kids' (speeding up, slowing down, disappearing).

**Accommodation changes outcomes faster than willpower**: A fidget tool, a quiet spot, extra time, visual checklist—these are game-changers, not "giving up." Show them working.

### Character Voice Direction (for Ziggy + Supporting Cast)

**Ziggy's dialogue**:
- Fast, jumping between ideas, mid-sentence tangents, but also moments of deep emotional clarity
- Speech pattern that's energetic but not cartoonishly hyper—feels like a real 10-year-old with ADHD
- When dysregulated: shorter sentences, more stimming (humming, fidgeting, sounds), harder to focus on one thing
- When hyperfocused or emotionally connected: articulate, specific, thoughtful; the real brilliance
- Include moments of self-awareness shame ("I did it again, didn't I?" "I can't focus") mixed with moments of unselfconsciousness (just being himself in the fantasy)

**Mrs. Henderson (teacher)**:
- Kind voice, but tired undertone (not bitter, just human)
- When she's frustrated, it shows (sigh, quick tone), but she catches herself and resets
- She uses supportive language ("Let's try this differently") but is learning; some default old language slips in
- She notices Ziggy's strengths and surprises her (the idea storm, the emotional insight)
- NOT a saint; not a villain; just a teacher trying

**Jake (peer, possibly)**:
- Socially competent, not cruel, just distant
- Polite to Ziggy, but doesn't risk social capital including him
- In the fantasy, Jake either doesn't appear or appears as a witness to Ziggy's strength (neutrally impressed)
- Voice is confident, friendly, unremarkable

**Family (implied or on-screen)**:
- Parent is tired, loving, frustrated, all at once
- Parent notices Ziggy's strengths and struggles equally; no hierarchy
- Parent uses new language ("Your brain works fast," not "You need to calm down") but isn't a therapist-parent (realistic tone)
- If there's a sibling, show normal sibling dynamics (annoyance + love)

### ADHD Strength Validation

**Themes from specialists**:

1. **Hyperfocus is real and valuable** — not laziness on "unimportant" tasks, but genuine neurochemical difference in how interest drives attention
2. **Emotional intensity is a superpower** — ADHD kids feel more, read people faster, intuitive, empathetic; this is a gift, not weakness
3. **Rapid idea generation** — the ability to make novel connections, brainstorm fast, see patterns others miss
4. **Resilience and adaptability** — ADHD kids develop problem-solving and workarounds because they have to; this builds real skills
5. **Authenticity and directness** — less filtering, more honest; can be refreshing and connecting
6. **Pattern recognition** — spotting anomalies, noticing details in chaos
7. **Living in the moment** — less rumination, more presence (when not dysregulated)

**The "superpower" angle for SURGE**:
- Don't make ADHD the superpower; make ADHD traits the foundation of superpowers (hyperfocus → laser power, emotional intensity → empathy/telepathy, rapid thinking → rapid solution generation)
- Show the superpower *feeling* good to have, not just functional—that's crucial for self-worth
- But ground it in real accuracy: the superpowers are exaggerated ADHD traits, not magical
- In the pilot fantasy sequence, Ziggy should feel *capable* and *valued* for the first time—not broken, not rescued, just *understood*

### Red Flags to Avoid

**From all interviews, consolidated list:**

1. **Inspiration porn** — "Look at this ADHD kid who did the impossible!" (survivorship bias, erases struggle)
2. **Cure narratives** — "Once he gets help/finds the right teacher/gets diagnosed, it's fixed" (false, sets up shame when it's not)
3. **Willpower framing** — "He just needs to try harder / focus more" (implies choice where there is neurological difference)
4. **Patronizing language** — "You're so brave for trying" (implies low bar)
5. **Villain-izing adults** — "The teacher doesn't get it / the parent is unsupportive" (simplifies systemic issues)
6. **Erasure of emotional dysregulation** — Showing hyperactivity/inattention without emotional intensity (~80% of kids have it)
7. **Outsider-only representation** — Media made by neurotypical creators about ADHD without ADHD creators' input (feels like translation, not authenticity)
8. **Tokenism** — Adding an ADHD character without making their neurodiversity central to characterization
9. **Lack of adult ADHD visibility** — No show of successful, thriving ADHD adults (implicit message: "This might not get better")
10. **Fixing systems as someone else's job** — Centering the adult (teacher, parent) fixing the kid, not centering the kid understanding themselves
11. **Shame erasure without validation** — "Your ADHD is a gift!" without acknowledging "...and also it's hard right now"
12. **Medical-only framing** — Medication/therapy as the only solutions, without showing accommodation, acceptance, systemic change

## Key Tensions & Disagreements

**Tension 1: Struggle vs. Strength**

*Educator perspective*: "Kids with ADHD are genuinely struggling in the classroom right now. Validate the struggle first."

*Specialist perspective*: "Yes, AND they also have real strengths. If you only show struggle, they learn shame."

*Resolution for SURGE*: Show struggle as *specific* and *sensory* (the classroom is hard, reading aloud is hard), and show strength as *real and present* (Ziggy's hyperfocus, idea generation, emotional intuition are happening now, not waiting for help). Both are true.

---

**Tension 2: Individual vs. Systemic Fix**

*Educator perspective*: "Teachers need more resources and training. A single caring teacher can't fix a broken system."

*Specialist perspective*: "Ziggy also needs to understand his own brain and develop self-advocacy. It's not just system, it's also self."

*Resolution for SURGE*: Show both. Mrs. Henderson is learning and making small changes; Ziggy's family is learning about his brain; Ziggy is learning about himself. No single hero moment; multiple people changing.

---

**Tension 3: Diagnosis as Relief vs. Ongoing Struggle**

*Parent perspective*: "Diagnosis was a relief, but it didn't fix everything. We still struggle, just with better understanding."

*Researcher perspective*: "Diagnosis is information, not solution. The belief that 'once diagnosed, all is well' harms kids who are still struggling post-diagnosis."

*Resolution for SURGE*: Show diagnosis (implied or explicit) as opening a door to understanding and tools, not as a fix. Ziggy's struggles continue; his *shame* decreases because he understands they're not moral failings.

## Acceptance Criteria for Authenticity

Based on interviews, the script should score ≥4/5 on the following:

1. **Classroom authenticity** (4–5: specific sensory details, realistic peer/teacher dynamics, no stereotype solutions)
2. **ADHD accuracy** (4–5: mixed presentation shown; emotional dysregulation included; working memory gaps visible; no cure narrative)
3. **Character distinctness** (4–5: Ziggy, Mrs. Henderson, family/peer have distinct voices and motivations; no cardboard cutouts)
4. **Strength validation** (4–5: Ziggy's actual gifts (hyperfocus, emotional intensity, rapid thinking) are shown as real, not magical; grounded in ADHD neurology)
5. **Language & framing** (4–5: no "lazy" language; no "trying hard" patronizing; no inspiration porn; shame is named and contextualized)
6. **Adult representation** (4–5: Mrs. Henderson and family are sympathetic, imperfect, learning; not villains or saviors)
7. **No red flags** (5: none of the 12 consolidated red flags present; or if one is present, intentionally and with context)

**Passing threshold**: ≥4/5 average across all criteria. If any single criterion scores <3, it's a revision gate.
