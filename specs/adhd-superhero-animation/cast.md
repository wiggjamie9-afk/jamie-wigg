# Supporting Cast: SURGE

## Cast Overview

The following four supporting characters anchor Ziggy's pilot episode and shape the emotional landscape for the series. Each brings a distinct voice, conflict, and stake in Ziggy's journey.

---

## Mrs. Henderson (Teacher)

**Role:** 5th-grade teacher, Ziggy's primary antagonist in the classroom scenes (not a villain, but the source of institutional pressure and shame).

**Conflict with Ziggy:** She interprets his neurodivergence as laziness or behavioral choice. When he can't sustain focus during silent reading, she sees defiance. When he fidgets, she sees disrespect. She's desperate for classroom order and academic progress; Ziggy's self-regulation struggles feel like personal rejection of her authority. Her well-meaning redirection ("Ziggy, eyes on your book, please") compounds his shame because he's *trying* and failing, and he knows it.

**What she wants:** For Ziggy to succeed academically and behaviorally (so her classroom runs smoothly and he doesn't fall further behind); to be the teacher who reaches him. She's not cynical—she genuinely cares—but she lacks the framework to see neurodivergence as neurological difference rather than choice.

**Voice direction:** Mid-50s Gen X teacher energy, tired but kind. Slight Midwest accent, middle-aged woman voice (some vocal strain from years of talking), warm underneath the exasperation. Speech is measured and deliberate (trying to stay calm). When frustrated, a subtle edge creeps in. Interior monologue (when we hear it) is self-aware: "I wish I knew how to help him." No caricature—she's a real teacher doing her best with tools she doesn't have. **Emotional anchor:** Her moment of genuine seeing (noticing Ziggy solved a problem brilliantly, in his own way) is the pilot's turning point. This small shift from deficit to strength is what gives Ziggy—and the audience—hope.

---

## Jake (Peer / Best Friend)

**Role:** Ziggy's unconditional friend, soccer teammate, the neurotypical foil who reflects the ease of typical development and the value of neurodivergent friendship.

**Conflict with Ziggy:** Jake doesn't struggle with school or self-regulation, so he occasionally dismisses Ziggy's pain ("Just try harder," "It's not that hard")—not from cruelty, but from genuine incomprehension. He also feels hurt when Ziggy snaps at him during shame spirals, not understanding that Ziggy's frustration isn't with Jake but with himself. Their friendship survives these micro-conflicts because it's rooted in genuine affection, not codependence.

**What he wants:** For Ziggy to be happy and to keep being his best friend and soccer teammate. He wants things to be fun and easy the way they are for him.

**Voice direction:** 10 years old, high-energy, genuine enthusiasm, straightforward emotional expression. Younger-sounding than Ziggy (lighter, boyish clarity—not breathy, but not Ziggy's rapid-fire intensity). Midwestern American English (same setting), uncomplicated vocabulary, natural laugh. No sarcasm or irony; Jake means what he says. When he's thinking, there's a slight pause—he's earnest, not quick. Voice actor: 9–11 year old, athletic, naturally warm and encouraging, capable of genuine joy and genuine confusion without cynicism. **Emotional anchor:** Jake's loyalty and refusal to shame Ziggy models peer acceptance. His simple "you're awesome at soccer" becomes medicine in moments when Ziggy's ashamed.

---

## Sarah Chen (Mom)

**Role:** Ziggy's primary caregiver, emotional core for parent viewers, and the living embodiment of undiagnosed ADHD in adulthood.

**Conflict with Ziggy:** She loves him deeply but is also dysregulated herself—stretched thin between single parenting, freelance work with irregular income, and her own untreated ADHD. When she says "Why are you always losing things?" it comes from exhaustion and real overwhelm, not malice. Ziggy perceives it as rejection; she perceives his struggles as another fire to put out. They're both right, and both operating without the language to understand that they share the same neurotype.

**What she wants:** For Ziggy to be happy and successful; for school to stop being a battle; for herself to feel less broken. By mid-season, she begins wondering about her own ADHD (hyperfocu on design work until 2 AM, time blindness, emotional intensity, morning chaos) and starts to see Ziggy's struggles as a mirror rather than a failure.

**Voice direction:** Early 40s, warm but harried, Midwest accent, woman-voice that reflects both competence and exhaustion. When she's frustrated, her voice rises slightly; when she's vulnerable (especially around her own ADHD suspicion), she becomes quieter, more reflective. Background-life noise (email notifications, kid sounds) sometimes interrupts her presence. No performance—just a real parent. Voice actor: mid-40s to early 50s woman with Midwest inflection, able to convey exasperation and deep love in the same breath, capable of believable self-discovery. **Emotional anchor:** Her moment of realization—seeing Ziggy's strengths clearly, and recognizing them in herself—expands the show's empathy to parents. "I think you might be like me" becomes a reframe for both characters.

---

## Sage (Fantasy Mentor / Inner Guide)

**Role:** Guide figure in Ziggy's Surge-world sequences, eventually revealed as an imagined version of Ziggy's grandfather (Grandpa Chen, deceased), reconstructed from family stories and photos.

**Conflict with Ziggy:** None, directly. Sage's function is to counter the external shame voices (Mrs. Henderson, Ziggy's own critical thoughts). Sage believes in Ziggy without doubt—not naively, but grounded in deep knowing. The conflict is between Sage's worldview ("your brain is built for speed") and the world's narrative ("you're broken").

**What Sage wants:** To help Ziggy stop hating himself and see his own power. Sage also—by mid-season reveal—becomes a way for Ziggy to process grief (Grandpa died when Ziggy was too young to remember; Sage is the relationship Ziggy constructs and reconstructs as he grows).

**Voice direction:** Older, calm, assured without condescension. Speech is slow and deliberate compared to Ziggy's rapid-fire—every word lands. Warm timbre, East Asian American accent (reflecting heritage and grounding Ziggy's identity). Could be 60s-70s voice actor, or a younger performer pitched/processed slightly to sound weathered and old-souled. No folksy wisdom-keepers clichés—Sage speaks simply and directly, asking questions more than providing answers. Interior monologue (rare) is gentle and patient, sometimes melancholic (missing Ziggy, proud of Ziggy from wherever he is). **Emotional anchor:** Sage is the voice of unconditional acceptance Ziggy doesn't hear elsewhere. By late season, Sage becomes the internalized voice Ziggy can access on his own—the beginning of self-compassion.
