Welcome back to The Parenting Playbook. Today's topic might be the reason you're tired right now. Because today we're talking about sleep. Getting your kid to sleep, keeping them asleep, and getting your evening back — without it turning into a nightly negotiation that drains you both.

Before we start: if bedtime in your house is a battle right now, you're not doing it wrong. Sleep is genuinely one of the hardest parts of parenting. Almost every family struggles with it at some point. Let's make it easier.

The scene: seven-thirty. Bath, teeth, story — all done. And suddenly your child is wide awake, thirsty, scared of the dark, needs to tell you one more thing, can't find the right stuffed animal. An hour later, you're still in there, exhausted and a little resentful.

What's going on: Kids' bodies run on internal rhythms — circadian rhythms — that tell them when to feel sleepy and alert. When that clock is out of sync, or when a child is overtired, sleep gets harder, not easier. An overtired child often looks wired, not drowsy. The brain needs a runway: the brain can't go from full speed to asleep in one step. Neither can they.

The single most reliable tool: a consistent, calming bedtime routine. Not fancy — consistent. The same steps, in the same order, around the same time, most nights. Predictability signals the brain: we're heading toward sleep now.

Principles that hold up:

One. Protect the wind-down. The thirty to sixty minutes before bed should get calmer, dimmer, slower — not louder and brighter. Lower the lights, lower your voice, lower the energy.

Two. Mind the screens. Bright screens close to bedtime make it harder to feel sleepy. A thirty to sixty minute screen-off buffer before bed helps.

Three. Keep wake-up time steady, even on weekends. The body clock is anchored more by when we get up and see light than by when we fall asleep. Wildly different weekends mean rough Sunday nights.

Four. Same place, same cues. A consistent sleep environment — cool, dark, quiet, same comfort object — helps the brain associate that space with sleep.

Five. Build the routine so it has a clear end. A set number of stories, one song, one last cuddle, then lights out. When steps are predictable and finite, there's far less to negotiate.

Scenario One: The endless one more thing. Water, bathroom, a question, a fear. Use a small bedtime checklist the child helps make — water, bathroom, two books, lights off. Everything they might ask for is handled inside the routine, on purpose, before lights out. Once the list is done, it's done. You're not being mean; you're being predictable.

Scenario Two: The overtired toddler who fights sleep hardest. Counterintuitively, try an earlier bedtime, not a later one. An overtired child gets a second wind that makes everything harder. Watch for early sleepy signals — eye rubs, zoning out, sudden crankiness — and start the routine before the wired stage hits.

Scenario Three: The older kid — six, seven, eight — who stalls and resists. Give them some ownership: let them choose the books, set up the room, pick the order within the routine. Autonomy inside clear limits reduces power struggles.

Night wakings, bedtime resistance, and rough patches are normal and come and go with growth spurts and big changes. A bad week is not a broken kid.

And there are many reasonable approaches to sleep. Families land in different places based on values, child temperament, and circumstances. There's no single correct method.

But sleep matters for health. If your child snores heavily, seems to stop breathing, is persistently exhausted during the day, or if sleep problems are severe and ongoing — talk to your pediatrician.

Pick three or four steps, in the same order, run them at the same time every night for a week. Don't change anything else yet. Just be boringly consistent — and let the routine start doing the work for you.

You deserve your evenings back. Your child deserves the calm, predictable runway to sleep.
