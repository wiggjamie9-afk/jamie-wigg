The episode opens with Marcus Aurelius's private reflection: You could leave life right now. Let that determine what you do and say and think.

The host explains that Marcus wasn't being morbid — he was clarifying. The thought of death is a knife that cuts away what doesn't matter. Seneca made the same case: it isn't that we have a short time to live, but that we waste much of it. Life is long enough if you use it.

Think about how you spend a free evening. How much goes to scrolling something you won't remember tomorrow? How much to rehearsing a small resentment? How much to worrying about a conversation that hasn't happened? Now hold that next to Marcus's line: You could leave life right now. Suddenly the grudge looks expensive. The scroll looks like borrowed time. The call to someone you love looks urgent in a good way.

Memento mori doesn't make the evening dark. It makes it precious.

You don't have to think about death all day. Just once this morning, let the fact of your own finiteness sharpen one decision. Ask: if my time is genuinely limited — and it is — does this deserve a piece of it? Some things will pass that test easily. A walk, real attention to someone in front of you, work you care about. Other things won't survive the question at all. Let them go.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
