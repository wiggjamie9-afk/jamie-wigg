Welcome back to Dating Decoded, the show where we take the messy, confusing world of modern love and look at what's actually going on underneath. I'm your host. Today we're talking about dating apps — not whether to use them, because most of us already are, but how to use them in a way that actually works. Because right now, a lot of people are swiping daily and feeling like they have nothing to show for it, and that is not bad luck. That is strategy.

Let's start with what the apps actually are. They are not magic portals that deliver compatible humans to your door. They are tools. And like any tool, the results depend almost entirely on how you use them. A hammer used poorly doesn't drive nails — it bruises thumbs. Dating apps used poorly don't build relationships — they drain your energy and make you feel like love is impossible.

The first thing most people get wrong is the volume approach. More swipes, more matches, more dates. The thinking is that it's a numbers game, so the more people you interact with, the better your odds. There's a surface-level logic to this, but it falls apart quickly. When you are treating it like a numbers game, you stop being a real person and start being a pattern of behavior. Matches feel interchangeable. Conversations feel like scripts. You stop actually looking at the profile in front of you. And the other person can feel that — they're doing the same thing, and neither of you is actually here.

Quality over quantity is not a cliche. It is the actual strategy. Twenty conversations where you're fully present will produce far better results than two hundred half-hearted ones. So try this. Set a limit. Pick a manageable number of matches and actually invest in those conversations before opening more. Be genuinely curious. Ask a real question based on something they actually said. Read the bio. Notice what made you swipe. Say something about it.

Now let's talk profiles, because yours is doing more work than you realise, in both directions.

The biggest mistake in profile photos is choosing the ones that look the most impressive rather than the ones that look most like you. The photo that shows you at your best from three years ago sets up every date as a disappointment. The ones that capture your actual energy — the way you laugh, the things you do, the life you live right now — those do the real work. Authenticity in photos is not settling. It's filtering for people who are attracted to who you actually are, which is the only useful kind of match.

On bios. Most bios are a list of nouns with no personality. Loves hiking, coffee, travel, dogs. Everybody loves those things. Write a sentence that only you could have written. A specific observation. A small weird fact about yourself. Something that invites a question. Your bio is not a resume. It's a door. Leave it slightly open.

Here is something nobody tells you about how to handle the matching phase: slow down the escalation. There's an instinct, especially when you're excited about someone, to push things forward fast. Daily messaging, long threads, planning dates in the first hour. But depth requires time to develop. A slower build — where you actually learn something about each other before meeting — tends to produce better first dates and better outcomes. Rushing creates an illusion of closeness that can collapse the moment you're actually in a room together.

Meet in person sooner than you think you need to. This sounds like the opposite of what I just said, but it isn't. The goal is not to have long app conversations. The goal is to get enough of a sense of someone to want to meet them, and then actually meet them. App chemistry and in-person chemistry are not the same thing. The only way to know if something is real is to be in the same physical space. So: slower build, but don't stall indefinitely.

Let's talk about the emotional side of this, because the app experience can do a number on your self-worth if you're not careful.

The matching algorithm and your value as a person are not connected. They are not measuring the same thing. An algorithm measures swipe rates and engagement patterns. It knows nothing about what makes you compelling to talk to, what you're like when you laugh, how you make people feel, the specific qualities in you that will make one person — the right person — feel like they've found exactly what they've been looking for. None of that shows up in a database. So stop letting match count tell you anything about yourself.

Take breaks. Seriously. If you have been on the apps continuously for months and feel worse about yourself than when you started, that is information. Step back. Live your life offline for a while. Get reminded of who you are outside of a profile. Come back when you feel like it, not because you feel like you have to.

The apps work best when they are a supplement to a full life, not the center of it. The people who meet someone meaningful on an app are almost always the ones who weren't desperate for it. They had a life. They were okay. They were just open.

That's it for today's Dating Decoded. Next time, we're talking about something that comes up in every relationship eventually — setting boundaries without blowing everything up. Until then, be honest, be kind, and text them back. You'll be okay.
