The Discipline Dilemma. Every dad I know has a version of this story. You set a rule, your kid breaks it, you react in a way you are not proud of, and then you spend the rest of the evening wondering if you handled it right.

I have been there more times than I can count. And what I have learned — slowly, painfully, through a lot of trial and error — is that discipline is not really about punishment at all. It is about teaching. The word itself comes from the same root as disciple. A learner. Someone being guided. That reframed everything for me.

When I started thinking of myself as a teacher in those moments instead of an enforcer, my approach changed completely. A teacher does not lose it when a student gets the answer wrong. A teacher asks, okay, what happened here, what did we miss, and how do we do it differently next time?

That does not mean there are no consequences. There absolutely are. Consequences are how kids connect actions to outcomes. If my son leaves his bike in the rain and it rusts, that is a consequence. If my daughter stays up too late and is a disaster the next morning, that is a consequence. Natural consequences are powerful teachers and they cost you nothing except the discipline to let them happen rather than swooping in to rescue.

Where I had to do more work was on consistency. Kids are incredibly good at finding the exceptions. They know exactly which parent to ask, which mood to catch you in, which sibling to recruit. And if consequences are inconsistent — if the same behaviour gets a different response depending on the day — they learn the rule is negotiable. Which means they will keep negotiating.

So my wife and I spent time getting aligned. Not perfectly — you never get there perfectly — but enough that our kids generally hear the same answer from both of us. That took honest conversations about what we actually valued and what we were willing to enforce versus what was just noise.

The other thing that helped enormously was separating the behaviour from the kid. I never want my son to think he IS a bad kid. I want him to know that what he DID was not okay, and that he is completely capable of doing better. Those are two different messages and kids hear them very differently.

The moments I am most proud of as a dad are not the ones where I held the line perfectly. They are the ones where I repaired something. Where I went back and said, I did not handle that well earlier and I am sorry. Modelling the ability to own a mistake is one of the most powerful things you can do.
