Teaching Life Skills Without Turning Into a Lecture. Welcome to Episode twenty one. This one is close to my heart because I nearly ruined it.

I am a big believer that the most important things I can teach my kids have nothing to do with school. I want them to know how to cook a proper meal. How to handle money. How to fix basic things around the house. How to have a hard conversation. How to apologise properly. How to read a map — yes an actual map, not just a phone — and how to keep going when something is genuinely difficult.

The problem was that early on I delivered these lessons like a university lecturer. Sit down, here is the curriculum. Today we are covering budgeting. Eyes front please.

My kids looked at me like I had lost my mind. Nobody asked for a budgeting lecture at the dinner table. Nobody asked for the seventeen-step process for changing a tyre delivered as a formal presentation on a Saturday morning.

What I have figured out since then is that life skills are best transferred in the moment, not in the classroom.

My son learned to cook because he kept hanging around the kitchen when I was making dinner and I started handing him things to do. Stir this. Chop that. What do you think it needs? Before long he was making the whole thing. He does not know he had a cooking lesson. He thinks he just likes cooking now. Which is exactly right.

My daughter learned about money because when she wanted something expensive I said, great, here is how we are going to work that out, and we sat down together and looked at her savings and worked backwards from what she wanted to what she needed to do to get there. That is a maths lesson and a goal-setting lesson and a delayed-gratification lesson all in one, and she experienced it as just solving a problem she actually cared about.

The key seems to be this: the skill needs to connect to something they want. If the motivation is yours and not theirs, the lesson will not stick. But when they want the outcome, they will absorb the skill that leads to it without even realising it is happening.

I also try not to do things for my kids that they can do for themselves. That is hard because it is so much faster to just do it. But if I always tie the shoes, nobody learns to tie the shoes. If I always pack the bag, nobody learns to pack the bag. The short-term inconvenience of letting them struggle a little is worth the long-term reward of a kid who can figure things out.

That is the goal, ultimately. Not a kid who needs me to handle everything, but a kid who one day handles everything well — and who, somewhere in the back of their mind, knows that I showed them how.
