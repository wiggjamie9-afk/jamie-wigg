Today we are sitting with anxiety — not to fight it, not to push it away, but to understand it a little better. Because anxiety is not your enemy. It is a signal. And like all signals, it becomes much more manageable when you know how to listen to it without being overwhelmed by it.

Anxiety is the body's alarm system. It was designed, through millions of years of evolution, to protect us. When our ancestors faced a predator, the alarm would sound: heart rate rising, muscles tightening, breath quickening. All of it designed to get them running or fighting fast.

The problem is that the same system activates for modern threats that pose no physical danger at all. A looming deadline. A difficult conversation. A worry about money. The brain cannot always tell the difference between a predator and an email from your boss. It fires the same alarm regardless.

So your anxiety is not a weakness. It is not a character flaw. It is a protective system doing its best — sometimes too enthusiastically.

Here is the first thing that helps: name what you are feeling.

Research in neuroscience has found that the simple act of labelling an emotion — saying to yourself, even silently, I am feeling anxious right now — actually reduces the intensity of that emotion. Naming it moves activity from the reactive part of the brain toward the part that thinks and reasons. It creates a tiny bit of distance. Just enough to breathe.

Try it now if something is sitting with you. What do you feel? Where is it in your body? Give it a name. Not a story — just a name.

The second thing that helps is the body. Anxiety lives in the nervous system, and the nervous system responds to physical cues.

One of the most powerful tools you have is your exhale. Longer exhales activate the parasympathetic nervous system — the part of you that signals rest and recovery. The part that slows the heart and loosens the chest.

Let us practice. Breathe in for a count of four. Hold briefly. Now breathe out slowly for a count of six or eight. Feel the difference as you release. That is your body's natural calming response, available to you at any moment.

The third thing that helps is questioning the story anxiety is telling you.

Anxiety almost always comes with a narrative. Something terrible is about to happen. You are not capable of handling this. Things will go wrong. These narratives feel true because they arrive with such physical force — the tightened chest, the shallow breath. But feeling true and being true are very different things.

Gently ask: What is actually happening right now, in this moment? Not what might happen. Not what could go wrong. What is actually present, right here?

Often the answer is: not much. A sensation in the body. A thought on the mind. And beneath that — a moment that is, in itself, quite manageable.

This is not about dismissing your concerns. Your worries may be real and valid. It is about separating what is genuinely happening now from the amplified projections anxiety adds on top.

You do not have to resolve everything today. You do not have to have every answer. You only have to be here, in this breath, in this moment.

That is always enough.
