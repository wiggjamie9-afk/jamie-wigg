Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. If you have more than one child, you already know the soundtrack of today's episode. The shriek from the next room. The thud. The wail of, that's mine, give it back. And then the two of them appearing in the doorway, both certain they're the wronged one, both demanding you act as judge, jury, and referee. Today, we're talking about sibling rivalry.

Let me start by saying something that might take a weight off your shoulders. Brothers and sisters fighting is not a sign that something has gone wrong in your home. It is one of the most normal things in family life. In fact, the home is the one place where your children get to practise conflict, sharing, jealousy, and making up, all within the safety of people who will love them no matter what. The squabbling isn't the failure. It's the rehearsal.

So let's look at what's really going on underneath. At the heart of most sibling rivalry is a single quiet worry. Is there enough? Enough love, enough attention, enough of you. To a young child, love can feel like a pie with only so many slices. When a brother or sister gets a slice, it can feel like there's less left for them. So a lot of the fighting you see isn't really about the toy at all. It's two children competing for the thing they want most, which is you.

Here's the first principle, and it's a big one. Try not to compare them. When we say, why can't you be tidy like your sister, or your brother never made this much fuss, we think we're motivating. But what the child hears is, you love them more. Comparison pours fuel on rivalry. Each child needs to feel they are seen as themselves, not measured against the one standing next to them.

The second principle. Aim for fair, but understand that fair does not mean identical. Children have a radar for unfairness, but giving everyone exactly the same thing is a trap you can never win. Instead of equal, think individual. Give each child what they need, when they need it. A four-year-old needs help with shoes. A nine-year-old needs you to listen about a hard day at school. That's not unequal. That's meeting each child where they are.

The third principle. When the fighting starts, resist the urge to become the judge. The moment you start investigating who started it, you've stepped into a courtroom you can never leave, because there is almost never a clean answer. Instead of finding the guilty one, name the problem. Say, two children, one tablet, and a lot of frustration. That's a hard one. Let's solve it together. You're not handing down a verdict. You're handing them the skill of working it out.

Let me give you a few real scenes. Scene one. The new baby. An older child who was sweet and settled suddenly becomes clingy, defiant, or starts acting much younger than their age. This isn't bad behaviour. It's a child who's frightened of being replaced. The medicine here is one to one time. Even ten minutes a day, just you and them, with no phone and no baby, doing something they choose. That small, protected pocket of attention refills the tank.

Scene two. The constant tit for tat between two close siblings. Here, try not to get pulled into every single complaint. When it's safe, and it's bickering rather than real harm, you can step back and let them practise sorting it out. You might say, I trust you two to find a fair way through this. Coming in too fast every time can accidentally teach them that the way to get your attention is to fight.

Scene three. When it tips into hurting, hitting, biting, real cruelty. Here you do step in, calmly and firmly. You tend to the hurt child first, which quietly removes the reward of your attention from the one who lashed out. Then, once everyone's calm, you address the behaviour without shaming the child. We don't hurt. I won't let anyone in this family be hurt. Firm on the action, gentle on the child.

A word of reassurance before we close. Some rivalry, some jealousy, some squabbling, this is the normal weather of a house with more than one child, and it usually softens with time and maturity. But if the conflict feels relentless, if one child seems genuinely miserable, frightened, or persistently targeted, or if your gut tells you something deeper is wrong, please talk to your doctor, health visitor, or a family specialist. Asking for support is a strength, never a failure.

And here's my last thought for you tonight. One day, these two who fight over the front seat and the last biscuit may well become each other's closest people. The squabbles you're refereeing now are where they learn to share, to forgive, to repair, and to belong. You're not failing when they fight. You're raising people who are learning how to love each other. Be gentle with them, and be gentle with yourself. You're doing better than you think. I'll see you next time on Parenting Playbook.
