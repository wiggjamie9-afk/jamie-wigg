The episode opens with the host narrating the legend of the Bermuda Triangle — the ocean region roughly between Miami, Bermuda, and Puerto Rico that has long been associated with mysterious disappearances. The centerpiece of the legend is Flight Nineteen: five United States Navy bomber aircraft that took off from Florida in December nineteen forty-five on a training run, became disoriented when their compasses failed, reported the ocean doesn't look right, and ultimately vanished along with fourteen men. A rescue seaplane sent to find them also vanished.

The host then presents three theories, ranked by evidence.

The Skeptic's Case — the strongest. The Bermuda Triangle is statistically no more dangerous than any other heavily-trafficked stretch of ocean. Lloyd's of London and the United States Coast Guard have both concluded this. Flight Nineteen can be explained by a disoriented flight leader, deteriorating weather, low fuel, and darkness over open water. The Cyclops, a Navy cargo ship that disappeared in nineteen eighteen, was likely overloaded or caught in a storm. Many vanished without a trace stories, when examined closely, turn out to have traces or to have happened nowhere near the Triangle.

The Natural-but-Unusual. The region does have real hazards. The Gulf Stream is fast and can scatter wreckage quickly. The area is prone to sudden violent storms and waterspouts. Methane hydrates — pockets of gas frozen in the seabed — could theoretically reduce water density enough to sink a ship rapidly or disturb air enough to trouble a low-flying plane.

Further Out. Magnetic anomalies, rogue waves, lost Atlantis, alien activity, and doorways in the sea. These persist because the Triangle is a perfect screen onto which we project our fear of the deep.

The unresolved ending: The statistical case says the Bermuda Triangle is unremarkable. Yet Flight Nineteen still ends in silence; the Cyclops left no wreck, no bodies, no message. Probably is doing real work — the grounded explanations are probably right, but not certain. The true mystery may be us: the way we gather scattered tragedies, draw a line around them, and decide the line means something. The sea doesn't keep a tally. We do.
