Welcome back to Dating Decoded, the show where we take the messy, confusing world of modern love and look at what's actually going on underneath. I'm your host. Today we're talking about red flags. The word is everywhere right now — it's on social media, in dating advice, in group chats where someone is telling you about a person they've just met. But I think the way we talk about red flags is actually getting in our way, so today I want to decode what they really are, why we're wired to ignore the obvious ones, and how to build the skill of trusting yourself before it's too late.

Let's start with a definition, because the word gets applied to almost everything now. Someone orders a salad — red flag. Someone doesn't like the same music as you — red flag. Someone is close with their family — red flag. The problem with labelling everything is that it dilutes the actual signal. When everything is a red flag, nothing is. You can't see a real warning in a forest of invented ones.

A genuine red flag is a pattern of behaviour, or sometimes a single significant behaviour, that signals something meaningfully incompatible or genuinely unsafe. It's not about preference — I wish they liked hiking more. It's about character. How they treat service workers. How they speak about their exes. How they handle a moment when things don't go their way. What they do when you say no to something.

Let me give you some of the real ones. Not the internet list, but the deeper categories.

Contempt. If someone speaks about others with contempt, if they mock, belittle, or sneer at people who aren't in the room, pay close attention. Contempt isn't just dislike. It's a fundamental feeling that someone is beneath you. And research consistently shows that contempt is one of the most corrosive things that can exist in a relationship. It erodes respect. A person who shows contempt for others will eventually show it to you.

Refusing accountability. Everyone makes mistakes. The question is what someone does after. Can they say I got that wrong, I'm sorry, without immediately steering the conversation back to your flaws? Someone who cannot take even partial responsibility for their own behaviour in an argument, who always finds a way to make it your fault, is showing you a pattern that doesn't get easier over time. It gets harder.

Moving too fast. This one sounds romantic when it's happening, which is precisely why it's on the list. When someone pushes hard to escalate intimacy — emotional, physical, or in terms of commitment — before you've had time to know each other properly, it's worth slowing down and asking why. Genuine connection builds at its own pace. Pressure to speed up can sometimes mean someone wants the feeling of closeness without doing the work of actually earning trust. Take the pace you need.

Inconsistency between words and actions. They say they want something serious but they're unavailable. They say they value honesty but are vague about things that should be simple. They say they care about you but their behaviour tells a different story. Actions and words can be out of sync in any direction, and when they are, the actions are always the truth. Words are easy. How someone shows up when it costs them something is the data.

Now — why do we ignore these things? Because we absolutely do. We see them, we feel the unease, and then we find a reason to explain it away. They had a rough childhood. They're stressed at work. That's just how they are when they don't know someone yet. They'll be different once they feel safe.

There are a few reasons we do this. One is that we're optimistic about people we're attracted to. The brain that's in early-stage romantic interest is actually different in measurable ways from its normal state. It is more generous, more inclined to explain away negatives, more focused on everything that feels good. This is beautiful in many ways. It's also the thing that lets a real warning sign slide.

Another reason is sunk cost. The longer you've been in something, the harder it is to acknowledge that what you've seen might mean you need to leave. You've introduced them to people. You've made plans. Admitting a red flag feels like admitting you wasted time, which you didn't — every relationship teaches you something — but the fear of that admission can keep you in something past the point where you should have left.

And another reason, maybe the most honest one, is that we sometimes don't trust our own judgement. Past experiences where we misread a situation, or were talked out of our instincts by a partner, or where our concerns turned out to be unfounded, can make us doubt the signal. I might be overreacting. I've been wrong before. Who am I to judge?

Here is something I want you to carry with you: the discomfort you feel in your body when something is off is not nothing. It's not overreacting. It is your whole self processing information faster than your conscious mind can articulate it. You don't need a list of reasons before you're allowed to feel uncomfortable with something. The feeling is already a reason.

Trusting yourself is a skill. You build it by naming what you notice — even just to yourself — rather than immediately looking for an explanation that makes the concern go away. You build it by taking your first impression seriously before the attraction rewrites it. You build it by acting on mild concerns early, when acting is easy, rather than waiting until a pattern is fully established and far harder to walk away from.

Red flags are not verdicts. They're data. Some can be discussed and addressed by two people who are honest about them. Some cannot. The skill is knowing the difference — and being honest enough with yourself to say what you actually see.

You deserve to be in something that doesn't require you to talk yourself out of your own discomfort. That's not high standards. That's the minimum.

That's it for today's Dating Decoded. Next time we're going deep on attachment styles revisited — specifically, how to recognise your own patterns and start actually changing them, not just naming them. Until then, be honest, be kind, and text them back. You'll be okay.
