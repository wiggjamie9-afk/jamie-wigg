School Transitions. Starting a new school, moving from primary to secondary, changing to a different class — these moments are enormous for kids. Enormous and almost invisible to adults who have already lived through them and forgotten how big they felt.

I remember dropping my eldest off on the first day of secondary school. He was trying very hard to look like he was fine. The jaw was set. The shoulders were back. He did not want me to make a thing of it. So I did not make a thing of it. I just said, you are going to be great, I will be here at three thirty, and I drove away.

And then I sat in the car park for five minutes feeling all the feelings he was not letting himself feel yet.

What I have learned about transitions is that kids need different things at different ages, but a few things stay constant.

They need information ahead of time. Uncertainty is the enemy of confidence. If your kid is starting a new school, take them there before the first day if you can. Walk the route. Find the bathrooms. Know where the lockers are. That kind of preview work sounds boring but it reduces the mental load on day one when there is already plenty to manage.

They need a safe place to debrief. And this is important: that place is not always you. Sometimes it is an older cousin. Sometimes it is a mate. Sometimes it is literally just their bedroom with the door shut. What matters is that they know you are available when they are ready. If you push for the debrief before they are ready you will get nothing and they will get worse at coming to you next time.

They also need the long game acknowledged. Transitions are hard at the start and then they settle. Your job is to hold that knowledge when your kid cannot. When they come home miserable after week two and say they hate it, you do not panic. You say, that sounds really hard, and these first few weeks are the hardest, and it does get better. And you say it calmly because you actually believe it.

The thing nobody tells you about school transitions is that you go through them too. Your anxiety about how your kid is going gets mixed up with theirs and it can make things messier. Do your own work on that. Talk to another parent. Remind yourself that your kid is tougher than you think.

They are. I am always surprised by how tough they are.
