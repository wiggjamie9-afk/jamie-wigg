Welcome to Stoic Minute. I'm Harry.

Seneca, writing to a friend who couldn't stop fretting about the future, put it this way: We suffer more often in imagination than in reality.

Sit with that for a moment, because it indicts almost all of us. Think of the things you have dreaded this year. The difficult conversation, the bill, the result, the change you couldn't see past. Now ask honestly, how many of them, when they finally arrived, were as bad as the version you rehearsed at two in the morning? Some, perhaps. But most arrived smaller than feared, or never arrived at all.

The mind is a remarkable machine for manufacturing catastrophes that never happen. Seneca saw that fear borrows trouble from a future that may never come, and charges full interest in the present. You pay, again and again, for disasters that exist only as scenes you keep replaying.

The Stoics had a curious antidote. Rather than push the fear away, they walked toward it. They asked, calmly, what is the worst that could realistically occur? And then, if it did, what would I actually do? Almost always, you find there is a response. A next step. A way through that the panic had hidden from view. The fear shrinks the moment you give it a shape and a plan, instead of letting it loom, vague and enormous, in the dark.

So today, take one worry you've been carrying. Name the worst realistic outcome out loud, and then name what you would do if it came. You are not inviting disaster. You are taking back the time you'd otherwise spend suffering it in advance.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
