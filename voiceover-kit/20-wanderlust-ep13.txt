Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we are climbing high, into thin cold air and ancient stone, to a country that holds one of the great wonders of the human world. Peru. A nation on the western edge of South America where the land rises in giant steps, from the Pacific coast, up through the towering spine of the Andes, and down again into the steaming green vastness of the Amazon rainforest. Close your eyes and picture a stone city perched on a ridge, high among the mountains, wrapped in morning cloud. As the sun climbs, the mist begins to lift and burn away, and slowly, terrace by terrace, temple by temple, the lost city reveals itself, exactly as its builders left it five centuries ago. This is Machu Picchu, and it is the reason most travellers come to Peru. But it would be a great mistake to think it is the only reason.

Let me set the scene of the country. Peru was the heart of the Inca empire, the largest empire the Americas ever knew, and before the Inca, a dozen older civilizations rose and fell here over thousands of years. The result is a country layered with history the way few places on earth are. Most journeys begin in the capital, Lima, on the coast, but the real magic lives up in the mountains, in the old Inca heartland around the city of Cusco.

Cusco is where you must begin properly, and where you must do the one thing every traveller is tempted to skip. You must slow down and let your body adjust to the altitude. Cusco sits at around eleven thousand feet above the sea, high enough that the air is genuinely thin, and if you arrive and immediately rush off to hike, the altitude will lay you flat with headaches, nausea, and breathlessness. So give Cusco two or three gentle days first. It is no hardship at all. The city is beautiful, a place where massive Inca stone foundations, fitted so precisely that no blade can slip between the blocks, hold up the elegant Spanish colonial buildings raised on top of them. Wander its steep cobbled lanes. Drink the coca tea the locals offer you, the traditional remedy for the altitude. Eat slowly and rest often.

When your body is ready, move into the Sacred Valley, the green and fertile heart of the old empire, following the river between the mountains. Here are the great Inca sites with a fraction of the crowds of the famous one, terraced hillsides, salt pans that have been worked since before the Spanish came, market towns where the descendants of the Inca still sell their weaving in colours so bright they seem lit from within. And then, at last, Machu Picchu itself. You can reach it the gentle way, by train through the dramatic gorge, or you can earn it the hard way, by walking the legendary Inca Trail, four days on foot over high mountain passes along the original stone road, arriving at dawn through the Sun Gate as the city emerges from the mist below you. If your knees and your lungs are willing, the walked arrival is one of the great experiences in all of travel.

The food of Peru deserves its own chapter, because Lima is, quietly, one of the great food cities of the entire world. This is the home of ceviche, fresh raw fish cured in lime juice with chilli and red onion, bright and sharp and clean. It is the home of more varieties of potato than anywhere on earth, thousands of them, in every colour. Try the lomo saltado, a sizzling stir-fry of beef that tells the whole story of Peru's mixed heritage on a single plate. And if you are brave, try cuy, which is guinea pig, an Andean delicacy for thousands of years.

Now, the practical map. Number one, and most important, respect the altitude. Ascend slowly, drink enormous amounts of water, avoid heavy meals and alcohol on your first days up high, and never be too proud to rest. Altitude sickness does not care how fit you are. Number two, book Machu Picchu in advance, genuinely far in advance. Entry numbers are strictly limited now, with timed tickets, and the Inca Trail in particular sells out months ahead. This is not a place you can decide to visit on a whim. Number three, carry cash in small notes. Outside the cities, in the markets and the villages, cards are useless, and breaking a large note can be impossible. Number four, the seasons. The dry season, roughly May to September, is the best time for the mountains and the trails, clearer skies, firmer paths. The wet season is greener and quieter but the trail can close.

And the thing to carry home from Peru. There is a particular humility that comes from standing in a city built by hand, without iron tools, without the wheel, high in mountains that would defeat most modern machinery, and realising it has stood for five hundred years and will stand for five hundred more. We are not the first clever people to walk this earth. Peru reminds you of that, gently and completely.

So your homework this week. Before you go, learn the Quechua greeting, not the Spanish one. Quechua is the language of the Inca, still spoken by millions in the Andes. The greeting is allillanchu, which roughly means, are you well. Offer it to a weaver in a mountain market, in the old language of her ancestors, and you will see a recognition cross her face that no amount of Spanish would ever earn you.
