Welcome to Calm Mind. Take a moment to arrive. Let your shoulders drop, just a little.

Today we are looking at perfectionism. Not the mild preference for doing good work, but the version that makes starting things feel impossible, that turns a finished task into a disappointment before you have even shown it to anyone, that whispers: this is not good enough, you are not good enough.

If that inner voice is familiar to you, take a breath with me first.

In slowly. And out.

Perfectionism is often described as a high standard. But underneath it, many people find something quite different. Fear. Fear of judgment. Fear of failure. Fear that without perfect output, they themselves are inadequate. The drive for perfection is not really about the work. It is about trying to feel safe.

And it is exhausting.

Perfectionism keeps people from submitting the application until it is perfect, which means it never gets submitted. It keeps people revising the email until they miss the moment. It keeps people from trying new things, because new things are uncomfortable, and uncomfortable means imperfect, and imperfect means visible, and visible means vulnerable.

Here is something worth sitting with.

The standard you hold yourself to is probably not the standard you hold anyone else to. Think of a friend who shows you their work in progress. You do not pick it apart with the same precision you use on your own. You see the effort. You see the intention. You see a person trying.

Could you offer that to yourself?

Not lower standards. Not carelessness. Just the same basic warmth you extend to others without even thinking.

Perfectionism and self-compassion cannot fully coexist. When one grows, the other tends to shrink. And self-compassion does not make you lazy or complacent. Research consistently shows the opposite. People who are kinder to themselves when they fail are more likely to try again, not less.

There is also something called the good enough standard worth considering. Not sloppy. Not rushed. But a deliberate acknowledgment that most things in life — most emails, most meals, most conversations, most creative attempts — do not need to be exceptional. They need to be done. Sent. Shared. Lived.

Done is often better than perfect. Because done means you actually participated.

If perfectionism has been with you for a long time, it may have roots in early experiences — environments where love or approval felt conditional on performance. Unpacking that tends to go faster with a good therapist than alone. If it resonates, it may be worth exploring.

Take a breath. In. Hold. And slowly out.

The version of you trying hard and getting it imperfectly right is more real, more relatable, and more connected to the rest of us than the flawless version you are aiming for. That flawless version cannot be known. The imperfect one can.

Let yourself be knowable.
