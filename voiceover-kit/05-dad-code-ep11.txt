# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 11
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. Today we're going loud, and we're going somewhere I've behaved badly, so strap in. Today we're talking about sport. Competition. Winning and losing. And that very particular madness that comes over a perfectly normal dad the moment his kid steps onto a pitch.

Let me paint you the picture, and I want you to know it's a true story and I'm not proud of it. Under-eights football. A Saturday morning. Drizzle. And there I am on the touchline, a grown man, shouting tactical instructions at a seven-year-old who is, at that exact moment, picking a daisy. I'm yelling about pressing the space and holding the line and he is having a lovely time with a daisy. And it hit me, in a horrible flash. This is not for him. This is for me.

That's the thing nobody warns you about. The sideline holds up a mirror, and you do not always like what you see. All that stuff we tell ourselves about teaching them grit and teamwork, sometimes it's a cover story. Sometimes we're standing there trying to win something we lost a long time ago, through a child who just wants a snack and a daisy.

So let me say the uncomfortable bit out loud. Your kid can feel the difference between you enjoying their game and you needing them to win. They can feel it from forty yards away in the rain. And when they sense that your mood, your face, your whole afternoon depends on them scoring, you've just handed a small child a weight they were never meant to carry. The game stops being play and becomes a job. A job where the boss is their own dad, and the wages are whether he's happy on the drive home.

The drive home. Let's talk about the drive home, because that car is where dads either build a kid up or quietly take them apart. You know the temptation. You start the debrief. You should've passed there. Why didn't you track back. And the kid just shrinks into the seat. So here's a rule I had to learn the hard way. The drive home is not for coaching. The drive home is for one sentence. I love watching you play. That's it. That's the whole speech. I love watching you play. Not you played well. Not here's what to fix. Just, I love watching you. Win or lose, dead last or man of the match, the message is the same and it never changes.

Right, the practical bit. Three moves.

One: separate the result from the relationship, hard, in your own head, before you ever get to the ground. Decide in advance that your warmth on the way home does not move one single inch based on the score. If you only know that intellectually, it'll slip. So make it a vow. The hug is the same after a loss. Especially after a loss.

Two: praise the effort, never the talent. Don't say you're so gifted, you're a natural. That sounds nice but it's a trap, because the day they struggle, they'll think the gift has run out and quit. Instead, praise the thing they actually control. You didn't give up when you were two goals down. You ran for the whole game. That's the stuff that builds a kid who tries hard things, instead of a kid terrified of the day they stop looking talented.

Three: let losing happen, and let it sting, without rushing in to fix it. We're so quick to soften it. Never mind, you'll win next time, who cares about silly football. But sometimes a kid needs to just sit in the disappointment for a minute and find out it doesn't kill them. Losing a match at eight is a cheap, safe, brilliant rehearsal for losing things that matter at twenty-eight. Don't rob them of the practice.

Here's what I want you to sit with. Almost none of our kids are going to be professional anything. The odds are tiny and you know it. So the sport was never really the point. The point is what they learn about themselves while they're doing it. Whether they learn that they're loved for the effort, or only for the win. Whether they learn that Dad is a safe place to fail, or another judge to perform for.

You get to decide which. And you mostly decide it in the car, in four words, in the rain.

Next week we go gentle and we go deep. The hard questions. Death, fairness, why is the world like this. The conversations you can't google your way out of. See you then.

This has been Dad Code. Now go tell your kid you love watching them play. Even if they were picking daisies.
