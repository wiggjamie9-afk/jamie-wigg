Welcome back to Dating Decoded, the show where we take the messy, confusing world of modern love and look at what's actually going on underneath. I'm your host. Today we're coming back to something we introduced earlier in this series — attachment styles — but this time we're going deeper. Because I've noticed something in the conversations people have about this topic. They learn what their style is, they tell everyone about it, and then nothing changes. Today we're talking about what it actually takes to move toward the version of yourself you want to be — not just in theory, but in how you actually behave the next time things get hard.

Quick recap for anyone who needs it. Attachment styles are patterns in how we connect, shaped largely by early experiences with caregivers. The three main patterns are secure — comfortable with closeness and independence; anxious — craving connection and sensitive to any sign it might be pulled away; and avoidant — valuing independence so much that closeness starts to feel like pressure. There's also a fearful-avoidant style that contains elements of both anxious and avoidant.

The important thing I want you to hold onto from the start of this conversation is something the internet version of attachment theory consistently undersells. These are tendencies, not identities. They are the grooves your nervous system learned to run in. They can change. Slowly, with effort, and often with the right support — but they absolutely can change.

So let's talk about the anxious pattern first, because it's probably the most commonly identified one, at least in the spaces where people talk about this stuff.

If you tend toward anxious attachment, your nervous system is wired to be very alert to signs of distance or disconnection. A late reply reads as withdrawal. A quieter-than-usual conversation reads as something being wrong. The gap between sending a message and receiving one can be genuinely uncomfortable, in a way that feels out of proportion to the situation but doesn't feel chosen — it just happens, fast. And the usual response is to reach out. Check in. Ask if something's wrong. Try to close the gap.

The problem is that the reaching, when it's driven by anxiety rather than genuine connection, often produces the opposite of what it's seeking. It can feel like pressure to the other person, who wasn't actually withdrawing at all. And so the anxious person learns, incorrectly, that they need to reach even harder.

The way through this pattern isn't to stop feeling anxious — you can't decide that. The way through is to pause between the feeling and the action. Not forever. Just long enough to ask yourself: is this feeling telling me something real is happening, or is this my nervous system running an old program? Most of the time, honestly, it's the old program. And naming that gives you a split second of choice about what to do next. Over time, with practice, that split second gets wider.

Alongside that, the real work for anxious attachment is building self-sufficiency in a very specific sense. Not closing yourself off, not needing less from people — but cultivating sources of security that live inside you, not only in the presence or absence of another person. Your own sense of your value. Your own capacity to soothe yourself when you're worried. Your own life outside the relationship that exists and matters when the relationship is quiet. That internal foundation is what makes the anxiety lose power.

Now let's talk about the avoidant pattern, because people with this style often find it harder to see in themselves, precisely because pulling back feels like a reasonable response rather than a reaction.

If you tend toward avoidant attachment, closeness past a certain point starts to feel like a loss of self. You might genuinely enjoy early dating when things are light and undemanding. But when someone wants more — more consistency, more vulnerability, more commitment — something inside you registers that as a threat to your independence, or a demand you don't know how to meet. And the response is to create distance. To get busy. To find something about the person that justifies stepping back.

The avoidant pattern is not cold. People with avoidant attachment feel deeply — they've just learned that expressing those feelings, or depending on someone, usually leads to disappointment or intrusion. So they protect themselves by needing less. Or at least appearing to.

The growth edge here is letting something be uncomfortable without immediately exiting it. The moment you feel that pull to withdraw — to cancel plans, to text less, to find the flaw — that is the moment worth pausing at. Not to bulldoze through it, but to get curious about it. What am I actually scared of right now? Usually it isn't the person. It's the vulnerability. It's the possibility of needing them and being let down.

Tolerating that discomfort in small doses, staying in the conversation a little past the point where instinct says leave, is how you expand your capacity for closeness. It doesn't feel natural at first. That's the point. The grooves were laid down a long time ago, and you're cutting new ones.

Now — both styles, and honestly everyone regardless of style — benefits enormously from what researchers call a corrective experience. That is, a relationship, with a partner or a therapist or both, in which the old pattern runs, and something different happens. The anxious person reaches, and instead of the other person pulling away, they stay and say I see you, you're okay, I'm here. The avoidant person pulls back, and instead of the other person collapsing or demanding, they give space calmly and are still there when the dust settles. These repeated experiences, over time, genuinely update the nervous system's sense of what's safe.

You can't manufacture those experiences entirely on your own. But you can choose relationships where they're more likely to happen. You can be honest with a partner about your patterns. You can ask for what you actually need rather than acting it out indirectly. You can do the inner work — therapy, journaling, honest reflection — that makes you a more self-aware participant in whatever relationship you're in.

One more thing. Secure attachment isn't a destination you arrive at and stay forever. Even people who start there can be shaken by significant loss or difficult relationships. Security is something you practise. A posture you return to, again and again, by choosing to reach out honestly, to stay present when things are uncomfortable, and to trust that connection is worth the risk.

You are already more than your early wiring. The fact that you know your pattern is the beginning of the whole thing.
