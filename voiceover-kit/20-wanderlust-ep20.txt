Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we're talking about the islands. Not the famous ones. Not the ones on the cover of the travel magazines, all turquoise and overwater bungalows and price tags that require a deep breath. I want to talk about the overlooked islands. The ones you can actually afford. The ones where you arrive and there are more fishing boats than sunbeds, where the guesthouses are run by the family whose grandmother built them, where the sea is every bit as clear and warm and extraordinary as anywhere on earth, and where tourism hasn't yet rewritten the entire local economy into a single transaction with you, the visitor.

Let me start in Greece. Most people who visit Greece go to Santorini or Mykonos. Both are beautiful. Both are extremely expensive and extremely crowded in high season, and I say this as someone who has been to both. The Greek island chain extends to over two hundred inhabited islands, and the further you get from the famous ones, the more Greece you find.

Ikaria is a mid-sized island in the eastern Aegean, and it is famous for a reason that has nothing to do with beaches or historic sites. It is one of only five places on earth classified as a Blue Zone, a region where people live measurably longer than almost anywhere else. The median Ikarian lives into their nineties, with dramatically lower rates of dementia, heart disease, and depression than the European average. Researchers who went to study why came back with a list that reads like common sense. They eat a Mediterranean diet heavy in legumes, olive oil, and greens. They walk everywhere, because the island is hilly. They nap in the afternoon. They socialise extensively. They have no particular interest in what time it is. The island famously has a phrase that translates roughly as we forgot to die. You spend a week on Ikaria and you start to feel why.

Samos is another eastern Aegean island, birthplace of Pythagoras and home to one of the finest ancient Greek archaeological sites outside of Athens, largely unvisited. It has mountain villages that produce extraordinary local wine, and a coastline alternating between pebble coves and long sandy bays that would be completely rammed if they existed in the western Mediterranean.

Now let me take you to the Philippines. The Philippine archipelago contains seven thousand six hundred and forty-one islands, of which only a few hundred are inhabited, and of which only a handful receive the majority of foreign tourists. Everyone goes to Palawan, to the island of Coron with its sunken Japanese warships from World War Two visible through ten metres of crystal water, or to El Nido with its dramatic limestone karst formations rising from the sea. These are genuinely extraordinary. Go if you can.

But Siargao, in the far south, is the Philippines surfing capital, a teardrop-shaped island with a laid-back energy, extraordinary reef breaks, and a population that has built a tourism infrastructure entirely around the idea of slowing down. It has coconut groves, still lagoons, and one of the best sunsets in the country. The island of Siquijor is known by Filipinos as an island of mystery and magic, a reputation rooted in its traditional healers and herbalists. It is small, quiet, and almost entirely off the package-tour circuit.

Let me cross to the Pacific and mention the Cook Islands, a fifteen-island nation in Polynesia. Rarotonga, the main island, is encircled by a reef that creates a warm shallow lagoon for swimming, and by a mountain interior that can be crossed on foot in a few hours. The population of the entire country is fifteen thousand people. There are no traffic lights. The local language is Maori. The welcome is genuine in a way that mass tourism destinations find very difficult to maintain.

And then there is Sao Tome and Principe, a tiny island nation in the Gulf of Guinea off the coast of central Africa, virtually unknown in mainstream travel. Portuguese-speaking, with colonial architecture dissolving into tropical forest, chocolate plantations where some of the world's finest cacao is grown, and empty beaches of volcanic black sand bordered by Atlantic surf and jungle. Getting there requires planning and a certain level of adventurousness. What you find is one of the last genuinely undiscovered island destinations on earth.

Here is the pattern across all of these places. The further you go from the famous version, the closer you get to the real thing. The logistics are slightly harder. The English menus are fewer. The timetables are less reliable. But the water is the same colour. The sunsets are the same length. And the cost is a fraction.
