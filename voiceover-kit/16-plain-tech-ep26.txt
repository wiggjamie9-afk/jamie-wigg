Welcome to Plain Tech. Today we are talking about upgrading your devices — when to do it, when to hold off, and how to make the switch without losing everything.

At some point, every phone, laptop, or tablet starts to feel slow. Apps take longer to open, the battery drains faster, and the camera does not quite keep up with everyone else's. It is one of the more frustrating parts of owning technology. Let's make it less painful.

The first question is: do you actually need to upgrade? Sometimes a slow device just needs a bit of maintenance. On a phone, clearing out old photos and apps you never use can free up storage space and speed things up noticeably. On a laptop, restarting it more often — rather than just closing the lid — clears out temporary files and can improve performance. If you have not run a software update in a while, doing that can also fix slowness caused by bugs.

If you have done all that and the device is still struggling, then yes, it might be time to upgrade. A good rule of thumb: if your phone or laptop is more than five or six years old, it is likely missing out on important security updates, and newer apps may not run well on it.

When it comes to choosing a new device, do not just buy the most expensive one. Think about what you actually use it for. If you mostly browse the internet, send emails, and video call with family, you do not need a high-end flagship phone or a premium laptop. A mid-range phone or a basic laptop will handle those tasks perfectly well and save you a significant amount of money.

For phones, you have two main ecosystems: Apple iPhone and Android. iPhones tend to be more consistent in terms of software support — Apple supports devices with updates for about five to six years after release. Android devices vary widely. A Samsung Galaxy or Google Pixel phone will generally get solid support for several years.

For laptops, the landscape has shifted recently. Apple's laptops with their own chips — the ones they call M-series — have become genuinely excellent in terms of battery life and speed. But a good Windows laptop from brands like Dell, Lenovo, or HP is perfectly capable for everyday use and often more affordable.

Now — the part that worries most people — what about all your stuff? Photos, contacts, messages, documents. The good news is that transferring from one device to another has gotten much easier in recent years.

For phones, both Apple and Android have official transfer tools. When you set up a new iPhone, it walks you through a process called Quick Start that moves almost everything from your old phone via a direct wireless connection. Android has a similar tool called Switch to Android or the Samsung Smart Switch, depending on the brand. If you have been using cloud storage — iCloud on Apple, Google Photos on Android — your photos are likely already backed up and will appear on your new device automatically.

For laptops, the most important things to back up are your documents folder and any work files. If you use a cloud service like Google Drive, Dropbox, or OneDrive, those files are already safe. Your email is almost certainly stored online and will sync to any new device.

One final tip: before you hand over or sell your old device, make sure you factory reset it. This wipes all your personal information. On an iPhone it is under Settings, then General, then Transfer or Reset iPhone. On Android it is under Settings, then General Management or System, then Reset.

Upgrading a device is a chance to start fresh, with something faster and better supported. It does not have to be stressful — especially when you know what to expect.
