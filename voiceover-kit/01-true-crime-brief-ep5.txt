This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a story that begins not with a body, but with a young man who walked into a police station and asked for help. He was polite. He was charming. He was articulate. He was, by every account, the kind of person you would want your daughter to bring home. And that, in the end, is the whole horror of him.

His name was Ted Bundy.

For years, across several American states in the nineteen seventies, young women disappeared. College students. Hitchhikers. Girls leaving night classes, walking to their cars, vanishing from beaches and ski resorts and quiet streets. The cases stretched from Washington and Oregon down through Utah and Colorado and, finally, to Florida. Different towns. Different jurisdictions. In an era before the kind of national databases we take for granted, no one immediately connected them. The pieces sat in separate filing cabinets in separate cities, and the pattern stayed hidden.

What made Bundy so effective was the very thing that should have made him harmless. He did not look like anyone's idea of a killer. He was good-looking, well-spoken, a law student, a young man active in politics, the sort of person who blended into a respectable life without a ripple. And he used that. He would approach women in public, in daylight, often wearing a fake cast on his arm or his leg, asking for help carrying books or unloading a sailboat. Who would refuse a friendly, injured young man a moment of kindness? That kindness was the trap.

He was first arrested in Utah in nineteen seventy-five, pulled over in the night, his car containing items that no innocent explanation could cover. A ski mask. Rope. Handcuffs. A crowbar. He was convicted of an attempted kidnapping, and slowly, as investigators in multiple states began to talk to one another, the larger shape of him came into view. He was charged with murder in Colorado.

And then Ted Bundy did something that turned him from a suspect into a legend of a very dark kind. He escaped. Twice.

The first time, during a court proceeding in Colorado, he jumped from a law library window and was recaptured after a few days in the mountains. The second time was worse. He starved himself thin enough to squeeze through a gap in the ceiling of his cell, climbed out, and got clean away. He made it all the way to Florida, where no one knew his face, and where he was free.

And in Florida, he killed again. He attacked young women in a sorority house at Florida State University, leaving several dead and others gravely injured in a single brutal night. Not long after, he abducted and murdered a twelve-year-old girl. The man who had slipped through the system's fingers had used his freedom to take more lives, and that fact has haunted American law enforcement ever since.

He was finally caught for good in nineteen seventy-eight, pulled over again in a stolen car. And his trial in Florida became one of the first true media spectacles of its kind, broadcast to a national audience. Bundy, ever the performer, chose to act as his own attorney. He cross-examined witnesses. He played to the cameras. He seemed to revel in the attention, in the women who showed up to court drawn to him, in the strange celebrity that grew around him even as the evidence of what he had done piled higher.

In the end, it was a small, physical, undeniable thing that helped seal his fate. A bite mark, left on one of his victims, matched to the impression of his own teeth. The charm could not argue its way past that.

Ted Bundy was sentenced to death. For years he fought it through appeals. And in the final days before his execution in nineteen eighty-nine, with no more cards to play, he began to confess, in detail, to killing he had spent a lifetime denying. He confessed to dozens of murders. The true number is something we will never fully know. He took some of it to the chair with him.

Here is what stays with me about Ted Bundy. We want our monsters to be obvious. We want them to look wrong, to feel wrong, to give themselves away. It would be so much safer if evil announced itself. Bundy proved the opposite. He was proof that a man can be warm and clever and likable, can be the friend who helps you change a tire, and still be hollow at the center where a conscience should be.

The women he killed were not careless. They were not naive. They simply did what decent people do. They saw someone who seemed to need help, and they offered it. He weaponized the best parts of us against us.

And that is the lesson that refuses to fade. Not to stop being kind. But to understand that the mask of an ordinary, charming man can be the most dangerous disguise of all.

This has been True Crime Brief.
