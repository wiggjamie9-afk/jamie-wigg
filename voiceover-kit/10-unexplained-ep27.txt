On the night of the second of February, nineteen fifty-nine, nine experienced hikers died on a slope in the northern Ural Mountains of the Soviet Union. They were students and graduates of the Ural Polytechnic Institute, led by a twenty-three-year-old named Igor Dyatlov. They were on a ski trek through difficult but not exceptional terrain, and they were well-equipped and experienced enough to handle it.

Their tent was found cut open from the inside on a slope called Kholat Syakhl, which in the local Mansi language means dead mountain. All nine had left the tent in the night wearing inadequate clothing for temperatures that had fallen below minus thirty degrees Celsius. Some were in socks. Some had no shoes at all. The tent was their shelter and their survival, and they had cut their way out of it and walked away from it.

The bodies were found in stages over two months. The first five were found within a kilometer and a half of the tent, in various states of undress. Two had died trying to build a fire. The remaining four were found in May, buried under four meters of snow in a ravine further down the slope. These four had more serious injuries. One had a fractured skull. Two others had severe chest trauma, broken ribs, the kind of internal damage that forensic investigators compared to the force of a car crash, yet without external bruising to indicate a blow. One woman was missing her tongue, eyes, lips, and part of her cheeks.

The Soviet investigation closed the case in May of nineteen fifty-nine with a ruling that the deaths were caused by a compelling natural force. That phrase, compelling natural force, has never been satisfactorily explained. The case files were classified. Decades passed.

After the Soviet Union collapsed, the files were partially released, and researchers began examining the case in earnest. The theories have accumulated steadily since. An avalanche was proposed, but the slope where the tent was pitched was not considered particularly avalanche-prone, no avalanche debris was found initially, and the pattern of the deaths does not fully match a group fleeing an avalanche. Infrasound from wind passing over the mountain has been proposed as a mechanism that could cause panic and disorientation at a physiological level. Some researchers have pointed to military testing in the area, to parachute mine experiments or rocket fuel testing that might explain the unusual injuries. Soviet investigators noted that some of the clothing recovered was found to contain radiation levels above background, a detail that has never been fully explained.

The local Mansi people, when interviewed by Soviet investigators, said the mountain was known to be bad. Not haunted in the way popular accounts often describe, but simply a place where people had died before and where the conditions were unpredictable in ways that defeated ordinary explanation.

What is verifiable is this: nine experienced people cut their way out of their only shelter in the middle of the night, in temperatures that would kill a person within minutes without protection, and walked away from the warmth and safety of their tent toward a slope that offered nothing. Whatever they saw or heard or felt inside that tent, it was worse than what was outside it.

In two thousand nineteen, Russian prosecutors reopened the investigation. Their conclusion was avalanche. Many researchers who have spent years on the case do not accept this. The discussion continues.

The mountain is still called Kholat Syakhl. The Mansi name predates the deaths. Whatever the mountain had a reputation for before February of nineteen fifty-nine, nine people added to it on a single night, and left behind a cut tent, footprints in the snow, and a cause of death that a Soviet prosecutor could only bring himself to call a compelling natural force.
