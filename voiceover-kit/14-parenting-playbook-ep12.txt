Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. Let me describe a kind of day you might know far too well. From the moment your feet hit the floor, you're herding. Get dressed. No, the other shoes. Have you brushed your teeth. We're going to be late. Where's your bag. And every single step is a negotiation, as though you've never done any of this before, as though mornings were invented fresh this very day. By the time you're out the door, you're frazzled and short-tempered, and the day has barely begun. Today, we're talking about building routines, and how the right rhythms can quietly take the daily fight out of family life.

Let me start with something I really want you to hear. If your days feel like a constant series of battles over the same things, the problem is almost never that your child is uniquely difficult. More often, it's that there's no rhythm doing the work for you, so every step has to be driven by you, in the moment, through sheer effort. A good routine is like a riverbank. It gives the day somewhere to flow, so you're not pushing the water along by hand.

Here's what's really going on underneath. Children feel safest when the world is predictable. A child who knows what comes next feels calmer and more in control, and a calm child cooperates far more easily than an anxious one. Uncertainty, on the other hand, breeds resistance and stalling. When every part of the day is up for debate, your child has to test each boundary fresh, every time. Routines remove that endless testing, because the answer is already settled. This is just what we do now.

So here are some principles that hold up.

One. Anchor the day with a few fixed points. You don't need to schedule every minute. You need a handful of reliable landmarks. Waking, mealtimes, and bedtime are the big three. When those stay steady, the whole day has a shape, and everything in between settles around them.

Two. Make the routine visible, not just spoken. Especially for younger children, a simple picture chart of the morning or the bedtime steps does wonders. Now the chart is the boss, not you. Instead of nagging, you ask, what's next on your chart. The child checks, and feels proud doing it themselves.

Three. Build routines with your child, not at them. Let them help decide the order where it doesn't matter. Do we do teeth then pyjamas, or pyjamas then teeth. A child who helped build the routine is far more invested in following it. Autonomy inside clear limits reduces the power struggles.

Four. Keep the steps few and the order the same. The same things, in the same sequence, most days. Predictability is the whole point. If the routine changes constantly, it stops being a routine and becomes just another thing to argue about.

Five. Expect the routine to wobble, and rebuild it gently. Holidays, illness, visitors, and big changes will all knock your rhythms off course. That's normal. You don't start again from nothing. You simply return to the familiar steps, and within a few days the rhythm usually re-establishes itself.

Let me give you some scenarios.

Scenario one. The chaotic morning. Move as much as you can to the night before. Clothes laid out, bags packed, breakfast decided. Then build a short, picture-led morning routine with no more than four or five steps, in the same order every day. You're not inventing the morning each time. You're following a path you've already laid.

Scenario two. The witching hour, that frayed stretch between coming home and dinner when everyone unravels. Give it a predictable shape. A snack, some quiet or some play, then the wind toward the meal. A child who knows what's coming in that wobbly window falls apart far less often.

Scenario three. The child who resists every routine you try to introduce. Start with just one. Don't overhaul the whole day at once. Pick the hardest moment, build a small reliable routine around only that, run it the same way for a week, and let the calm it brings convince everyone before you add the next.

Now, the bigger picture. Even the best routines won't make every day smooth, and they're not meant to. Children are not machines, and some days simply go sideways. A messy day, or a week where everything falls apart, doesn't mean your routines have failed. It means your child is human, and so are you.

And there's no single perfect routine. Families land in very different places depending on their schedules, their values, and their children's temperaments. What matters is not copying someone else's timetable, but finding a steady rhythm that fits your own home.

One quick note on wellbeing. If your child seems persistently unsettled no matter how steady you make their days, deeply anxious about small changes, or unusually distressed by ordinary transitions, that's worth a gentle conversation with your doctor or health visitor. Routines help enormously, but they're a tool, not a cure for everything.

So here's where I'd begin. Pick the single most stressful part of your day. Build one short, predictable routine around it, ideally with a picture chart your child helps make. Run it the same way, at the same time, for one week, and change nothing else. Let that one rhythm prove itself before you build the next.

You're not trying to control every minute. You're building riverbanks, so the day can flow without you pushing every drop of it along by hand.
