They were young women, most of them barely out of girlhood, and they had what looked like the best jobs in town. They sat at long tables in a clean, bright factory, painting numbers onto the faces of watches with a paint that glowed in the dark. It was delicate, well-paid, almost glamorous work. They were the envy of their friends. And the very thing that made them special, that beautiful luminous glow, was quietly killing every one of them.

Welcome to History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, the story of the Radium Girls, and the women who fought back from their deathbeds.

To understand it, you have to go back to the early years of the twentieth century, when a new element called radium had set the world on fire with excitement. Radium had been discovered by Marie Curie at the very end of the previous century, and it glowed with a faint, eerie light all on its own. To the public, it seemed almost magical. It was put into everything. There was radium toothpaste, radium face cream, radium water sold as a health tonic. People believed it was a source of energy and vitality. No one truly understood that this glowing miracle was intensely radioactive, and that its light was the light of something tearing matter apart.

During and after the First World War, factories sprang up to paint the dials of watches and instruments with radium paint, so that soldiers and ordinary people could read the time in the dark. The work was done almost entirely by young women, because it required small, steady hands and fine brushwork. In factories in New Jersey, in Illinois, and elsewhere, hundreds of them sat painting tiny numbers, day after day. And they were taught a particular technique to keep their brushes sharp. They were told to bring the bristles to a fine point with their lips. Dip the brush in the paint, draw it through their mouth to a point, paint the number, and repeat. Lip, dip, paint. Thousands of times a day. Swallowing a little radium with every stroke.

No one warned them. In fact, the company chemists and managers, who knew the dangers and handled the material with lead screens and tongs, told the girls there was nothing to fear. The young women were so unafraid that they treated the glowing paint as a toy. They painted their fingernails with it to surprise their boyfriends. They painted their teeth so their smiles would shine in the dark. They went home with the dust of it in their hair and on their clothes, glowing faintly as they walked, and people called them the ghost girls. They thought it was beautiful. They had no idea it was a death sentence written into their bones.

Because radium, once swallowed, behaves in the body almost exactly like calcium. And so it went where calcium goes. It settled into their bones and stayed there, glowing inside them, sending out its invisible radiation hour after hour, year after year, slowly destroying them from the inside. The first signs were in the mouth, where the most paint had passed. Teeth began to loosen and fall out. Then the jaw itself began to rot, an agonising condition the doctors came to call radium jaw, in which the bone died and crumbled and could sometimes be removed by hand. The women suffered terrible aches, anaemia, and fractures, their bones grown so brittle that they snapped under the simple weight of standing. Some grew tumours. Many of them, young women in their twenties, simply began to die.

And when they sought help, they were betrayed. The companies they had worked for denied everything. They claimed there was no link between the paint and the illness. In one of the cruellest acts in the whole story, a company arranged for medical examinations of the sick women and then, knowing the truth, concealed the results, and even spread the suggestion that the women were suffering from a venereal disease, blaming their own morals for their dying bodies. They calculated that the women were poor, that they were young, and that they would be dead before any case could ever come to court.

But the women fought. In New Jersey, a small group of dial painters, some of them already so ill they could barely walk, brought a lawsuit against their former employer. They became known as the Radium Girls. One of them, Grace Fryer, could no longer sit up without a back brace, and the radium in her body had ruined her jaw and her spine, and still she pressed on. When they finally appeared in court, several of the women were too weak to raise their arms to take the oath. The case drew the attention of the whole nation. The image of these dying young women, glowing from within, demanding justice from the corporations that had poisoned them, was impossible to ignore.

In the end, after long and bitter battles, the women won settlements. They did not win their lives back. Most of them died young, their bodies so saturated with radium that even decades later, their remains would still register on instruments, faintly radioactive in the grave. But their suffering and their courage changed the world. Their cases helped establish, for the first time in a serious way, that a company could be held responsible for the long-term health of its workers. Their fight led to new safety standards, to the right of workers to sue over occupational disease, and ultimately fed into the creation of the laws and agencies that protect workers' health today. The data gathered from their poisoned bodies also taught science crucial lessons about radiation that would later protect countless others, including those who worked on the atomic projects to come.

So what do the Radium Girls teach us? Perhaps that the comforts and wonders of any age can hide a cost that falls, almost always, on the people with the least power to refuse them. These were ordinary young women who simply wanted good work and a bright future, and they were lied to by the very people who should have protected them, because their lives were judged cheaper than the truth. And yet they refused to die quietly. From their sickbeds, with crumbling bones and failing bodies, they forced a powerful industry to answer for what it had done, and in doing so they bought safety for generations of workers who would never know their names. They glowed in the dark, once, and thought it was a gift. It is the least we can do to remember why.

This has been History Uncovered. Until next time, look a little more closely at the ordinary day around you. It is the only thing any of us are ever really given.
