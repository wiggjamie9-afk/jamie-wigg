Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

There is a line in the Meditations where Marcus Aurelius writes something almost startling in its simplicity. He says: Begin the morning by saying to yourself: I shall meet today with interference, ingratitude, insolence, disloyalty, ill-will, and selfishness.

It sounds like the most pessimistic alarm clock imaginable. But Marcus is doing something careful here. He is not cultivating bitterness. He is practicing what the Stoics called the premeditation of evils, the deliberate anticipation of difficulty so that difficulty, when it arrives, does not knock you off your feet.

The unprepared mind has a particular vulnerability. It constructs a version of the day in which everything will go smoothly, people will behave reasonably, and plans will hold their shape. And then the day comes. Someone cuts across you. A meeting turns frustrating. A person you counted on fails to show up in the way you needed. And because the mind had not accounted for this, it reads it as catastrophe. It feels personal. It feels unfair. The reaction is out of proportion to the event because the event was not expected at all.

Marcus continues the morning passage in a way people often omit. He says that he will encounter those difficulties, but that the people causing them are made of the same nature as him, share in the same reason, and are likely acting from ignorance rather than malice. So he will meet them without anger, without contempt, without resentment, because he was made for cooperation, not for reaction.

This is the full shape of the practice. You anticipate difficulty not to become hard and withdrawn, but so that you can meet real people, on a real day, without the shock of finding them imperfect. You lower the expectation not because people do not matter, but because the expectation was never a fair one to begin with.

Here is today's practice. Before your day properly starts, spend thirty seconds accounting honestly for what might go wrong or sideways. Someone may be short with you. A plan may shift. You may face a moment that asks more patience than you have on easy supply. Notice that you are accounting for this in advance, not dreading it. Then go into the day already equipped.

The mind that has rehearsed difficulty is steadier than the mind that expected none.
