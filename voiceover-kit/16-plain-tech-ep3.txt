First, let's get to know the usual suspects. Because once you can recognise a scam, you've already won half the battle.

The first one is the fake bank call or text. This is the one I just described. Someone contacts you pretending to be your bank, your card company, or even the tax office. They sound calm and official. They might say your account's at risk and you need to move your money to a, quote, safe account, or read out a code. Here's the truth. The second one is the your account is locked email. You get a message that looks like it's from a company you know. Your streaming service, your email provider, an online shop. It says there's a problem, your account is locked or your payment failed, and you need to click a link right now to fix it. That link takes you to a fake website designed to steal your login or your card details. The trick is the panic. They want you clicking before you're thinking.

The third one is the romance scam. This one's heartbreaking, and it's more common than people realise. Someone strikes up a warm, friendly conversation online, maybe on social media or a dating site. Over weeks or months they build a real connection, they seem genuinely caring, and then, eventually, comes a problem. They're stuck abroad, there's a medical emergency, a business deal gone wrong, and they need money. The relationship was the bait all along.

The fourth one is the tech-support pop-up. You're using your computer and suddenly a big warning fills the screen. Your computer is infected, it shouts, with a phone number to call immediately. If you call, a friendly voice offers to fix it, but they'll want to take control of your computer, or charge you for fake repairs. A real warning from your computer will never give you a phone number to call. That's the tell.

And the fifth one, the cruellest of all, is the grandchild in trouble call. The phone rings and it's a young voice, upset, crying. Now, here's the wonderful part. You don't have to memorise every single scam, because they all share the same fingerprints. Learn these red flags and you can spot a brand new scam you've never even heard of.

Red flag number one is urgency. Do this now, right now, or something terrible will happen. Scammers create panic on purpose, because a panicked brain doesn't stop to think. Red flag number two is secrecy. Don't tell anyone. Don't tell your family. Keep this between us. Anyone who tells you to keep a secret from the people who love you is up to no good. Real organisations are happy for you to check with others. Red flag number three is unusual payment, and the biggest giveaway of all is gift cards. If anyone, ever, asks you to pay with gift cards, the ones you buy in the supermarket and read out the numbers, it is one hundred percent a scam. No real company, no real bank, no real government office takes payment in gift cards. Ever. And red flag number four is pressure. They won't let you hang up, they keep you on the line, they won't let you stop and think. A genuine caller will always, always let you go away and check.

So if those are the warning signs, here's the single most powerful thing you can do. I call it the golden rule, and if you remember nothing else today, remember this. Hang up, and call back on the official number. Anytime someone contacts you out of the blue claiming to be your bank, or a company, or anyone asking for money or details, you simply say, thank you, I'm going to call you back. The real one. A genuine bank will completely understand. A scammer will try anything to stop you doing it. That one habit defeats almost every scam there is.

Now let me quickly cover two things that protect you behind the scenes. The first is strong passwords. Think of a password like the lock on your front door. A weak one, like your name or one-two-three-four, is a door anyone can open. A strong one is long and unique. My favourite trick? The second is two-factor authentication, which sounds technical but is beautifully simple. It just means that after you type your password, the company sends a short code to your phone that you also have to enter. It's a second lock on the door. So even if a scammer somehow got your password, they still can't get in without the code that's sitting on your phone. Most banks and email providers offer it, and switching it on is one of the kindest things you can do for yourself.
