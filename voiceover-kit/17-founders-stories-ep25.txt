Welcome to Founders' Stories.

There is a category of founder who makes everything harder for themselves on purpose.

Not in the self-destructive sense. In the sense that they have already been through the whole thing once and they know exactly which mistakes to make more carefully the second time.

Claudia Ferreira launched her first company at twenty-six, a social commerce platform for independent fashion sellers in Brazil. She raised two million dollars, grew to sixty-five employees, and watched the whole thing come apart over fourteen months when a regulatory change affected how payments could be processed on platforms like hers. She couldn't pivot fast enough. She wound the company down in two thousand and eighteen after four years.

She told me the shutdown was the most professionally humiliating experience of her life, and also the most clarifying.

She spent a year afterward doing two things. Working as a consultant to fund her life, and obsessively studying what had gone wrong. Not just the regulatory event, which was genuinely bad luck, but everything that had made the company fragile in the first place. The over-reliance on a single payment infrastructure. The team that had grown faster than the culture could absorb. The metrics that looked healthy on a slide but masked real weakness underneath. The decision to raise more than she needed because the money was available, which created pressure to grow faster than the business was ready for.

She wrote all of it down. She called it her failure document. She still has it.

When she started her second company in two thousand and twenty, she used the failure document as a design brief. It was not a list of things to avoid. It was a list of questions to answer before she moved to the next stage of growth.

The second company is a business-to-business software platform for small logistics operators in Latin America — route optimization, load management, driver communication. It is not a revolutionary product category. It is a well-understood problem with existing solutions, most of which are too expensive and too complex for the operators she is targeting. She is solving the same problem cheaper and simpler.

She has been deliberately slow about hiring. Fourteen months in, she had eleven employees. She was profitable at seven. She chose not to raise a large round when she had the option. She took a smaller check from fewer investors whose timelines matched hers.

I asked her what it felt like to do it differently the second time.

She said: "The first time, I was always running. Running toward the next thing, the next milestone, the next number. The second time, I move more like I am trying to build something that will still be here in ten years. Those are very different speeds."

She also said something about the cost of having failed once that I keep returning to. She said that the thing failure takes from you — your certainty, your confidence that you know how it goes — is also the thing it gives you. Because certainty is often wrong. Founders who have never lost anything often have a very high opinion of their own judgment. They haven't been tested yet.

Claudia has been tested. She knows exactly what she can survive. That knowledge doesn't make her reckless. It makes her steady.

The second company is harder to panic about when you already know what the bottom feels like.
