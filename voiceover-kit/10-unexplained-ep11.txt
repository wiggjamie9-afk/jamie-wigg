Hello, this is Austin, and you're listening to Unexplained.

Tonight there is no monster, no wreck, no vanished aircraft. Tonight there is only a sound. A low, dull, droning hum, like a distant diesel engine idling somewhere just out of sight, or a heavy truck left running in the next street over. Most people cannot hear it at all. But for those who can, it never stops. It follows them into their homes, into their beds, into the small hours of the night, and it is slowly driving some of them out of their minds. This is the Taos Hum.

The story centres on the town of Taos, in the high desert of New Mexico, in the early nineteen nineties. Residents there began to complain, independently of one another, that they could hear a persistent low-frequency hum. Not loud. Not constant in the way a machine is. But always there in the quiet, a throbbing, pulsing drone with no apparent source. In nineteen ninety-three, the complaints grew loud enough that researchers from several universities were brought in to investigate. They set up sensitive equipment around the town. They asked the people who could hear the hum to describe it. And here is the first strange thing. Only a small fraction of the population, perhaps two in every hundred, reported hearing it at all. To everyone else, the desert night was silent.

The hum is not only a story about Taos. Similar low droning sounds have been reported in many places around the world, in Bristol in England, in Windsor on the border between Canada and the United States, in Auckland in New Zealand. The phenomenon is real enough, and widespread enough, that it has a general name. People call these reports the Hum. And those who suffer from it describe something genuinely tormenting, a sound they cannot escape, that causes headaches, sleeplessness, dizziness, and a deep, grinding anxiety. So let us do what we do here. Let us set the legend aside and weigh the explanations by the evidence, not by which one frightens us the most.

The strongest explanations are the grounded ones, and they fall into two camps that may both be partly true. The first is that the hum is a real, external, very low frequency sound, and that only some people are sensitive enough to hear it. Our hearing varies enormously from person to person, especially at the bottom of the range, near the edge of what the human ear can detect. A faint, low-frequency tone might be completely inaudible to most of us and yet clearly, maddeningly present to a sensitive few. If that is the case, then the question becomes, what is making the sound? Possible sources are everywhere in the modern world, industrial machinery, gas pipelines, distant factories, electrical transformers, ventilation systems, even the deep rumble of ocean waves carried far inland.

The second grounded explanation is more unsettling, because it locates the hum not in the air, but inside the listener. Under this idea, at least some cases of the hum are generated by the body itself, a form of low-frequency tinnitus, a phantom sound produced by the auditory system when it has nothing else to listen to. This would explain why the hum is so often heard at night, in quiet rooms, by people lying still in the dark. It would explain why the equipment so often fails to record anything. And it would explain why two people standing in the same place can so completely disagree about whether there is any sound at all. The cruelty of this explanation is that it makes the suffering no less real. A sound that exists only in your own hearing is still a sound you cannot turn off.

Then comes the next tier, the natural but unusual. Perhaps the hum is the product of some rare interaction we do not fully understand, the resonance of certain geological formations, the movement of water deep underground, atmospheric pressure waves rolling across the land, or microseisms, the constant faint trembling of the Earth itself. These are real phenomena, and they can produce very low frequencies. Whether they can produce the specific, localised, persistent hum that torments a handful of people in a quiet town is far less certain.

Then there is a third tier, the further-out ideas, the ones the evidence supports least but the legend loves the most. Secret government experiments. Hidden underground installations. Communications systems broadcasting at frequencies meant to control or disturb the population. Signals from somewhere beyond the Earth. These stories thrive precisely because the official investigations have been so inconclusive. When the experts study a phenomenon for years and come away saying we are not entirely sure, the silence they leave behind fills quickly with darker imaginings.

So where does that leave us? Weigh it honestly, and the most likely truth is a mixture. Some of the hum is probably real external sound, low and faint, heard only by the sensitive. Some of it is probably generated within the listener, a phantom tone in a quiet room. And the deep frustration is that we may never be able to tell, for any one sufferer, which of these is happening to them. The Taos investigation never found a single, clear, external source. The hum was real to the people who heard it, and very nearly invisible to everything else.

And perhaps that is the heart of it. The Taos Hum is a mystery not of the spectacular, but of the intimate. It does not threaten the world. It threatens the quiet of a single bedroom, the peace of a single mind. It divides a town into those who hear it and those who never will, and it leaves no proof either way.

We are drawn to it because it lives exactly where the outer world and the inner world meet, in the act of hearing itself. We trust our senses. We assume that a sound is something out there, shared, recordable, real. The hum quietly takes that certainty away. It asks a question we cannot comfortably answer. When you lie awake in the dark and you are certain you can hear something, and the person beside you hears nothing at all, where exactly is the sound? And that question, the one we cannot quite reach, is where the hum will go on droning, low and patient, long after we have turned out the light.

This has been Unexplained. Sleep well, if you can.
