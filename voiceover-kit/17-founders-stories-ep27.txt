Welcome to Founders' Stories.

I want to tell you about a founder who had every reason to sell and chose not to.

Nadia Okonkwo started her company in her apartment in Lagos in two thousand and fifteen. It was a digital marketplace connecting Nigerian artisans — textile weavers, leather workers, ceramic artists, furniture makers — directly with buyers in Europe and North America. Not a drop-shipping operation. A platform where the craft itself was the story, where the buyer knew the maker's name, the village, the process, the time it took to produce the thing they were purchasing.

She bootstrapped for four years. She flew economy class to trade shows and craft fairs in London and Amsterdam to meet buyers in person, carrying samples in checked luggage. She did all of her own customer support in the early years, not because she couldn't afford to hire someone, but because she wanted to hear every complaint and every compliment directly.

By two thousand and nineteen she had four hundred active artisan partners, forty-five thousand registered buyers, and was generating just over two million dollars a year in gross merchandise value. The business was real and it was growing.

That year she received three acquisition approaches. One from a large lifestyle e-commerce company in the United Kingdom. One from a venture studio that wanted to invest and bring in a professional chief executive. One from a private equity group that framed its pitch around "unlocking the growth trajectory."

She took none of them.

I asked her why.

She said: "Every one of those conversations started from the same assumption. The assumption was that making this company bigger faster was the goal. That scale was the definition of success. But I had spent four years in very close relationship with the people who made things on this platform. I knew their names. I knew their children's names. I knew which weavers were putting their daughters through school with the income they earned here. And I knew that the thing that made this platform work was that relationship. The trust. The specificity. If I sold it to someone for whom this was a growth play, that would be the first thing to go."

Nadia is now in year ten. The company has grown carefully and deliberately. She has taken a small amount of investment from a fund specifically focused on African economic empowerment, whose values she spent two months evaluating before accepting. She has nine hundred artisan partners. Revenue has grown every year. She still reads every one-star review herself.

She told me that staying independent has cost her real money. The lifestyle platform that approached her in two thousand and nineteen was acquired itself two years later for a significant sum. Had she sold to them, she might have benefited from that. She knows this. She doesn't spend time on it.

What she spends time on is a program she launched three years ago inside the company. It funds business literacy training for artisan partners who want to expand. How to manage cash flow. How to negotiate. How to hire. Seventeen of the four hundred artisans she started with have now launched their own small export businesses, some of them on her platform, some of them not.

She considers those seventeen companies part of what she built.

Independence is not always the right choice. There are businesses that need the capital, the distribution, the infrastructure that a larger owner can provide. And there are businesses where the thing that makes them valuable is exactly what would be lost in that transaction.

Nadia knew which kind of business she was running. She protected it accordingly.

She told me near the end of our conversation: "I get asked sometimes whether I have an exit strategy. I say that my exit strategy is to still be here in fifteen years, and for the artisans on this platform to have built something of their own by then. That's the exit I'm working toward."
