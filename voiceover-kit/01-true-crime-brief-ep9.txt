This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a mystery that has no killer we can name, no courtroom, no verdict, and yet it remains one of the most enduring puzzles in the history of crime. It is a story of a hijacking in the sky, a parachute, a briefcase full of cash, and a man who stepped out of an airplane into the night and was never seen again. It is the story of the man the world came to know as D. B. Cooper.

The date was the twenty-fourth of November, nineteen seventy-one. The day before the American Thanksgiving holiday. A man calling himself Dan Cooper, a name later garbled in the press into the legend of D. B. Cooper, bought a ticket for a short flight from Portland, Oregon, to Seattle, Washington. He was described as a quiet, ordinary-looking man in a dark suit and tie, somewhere in his mid-forties. He sat near the back of the plane. He ordered a drink. He was, by every account, utterly unremarkable.

And then he handed a note to a flight attendant.

The note told her that he had a bomb. He opened his briefcase just enough to show her a glimpse of wires and red cylinders. And he made his demands, calm and precise. He wanted two hundred thousand dollars in cash. He wanted four parachutes. And when the plane landed in Seattle, he expected those things to be waiting for him.

What is remarkable about Cooper, in the accounts of those who were on that plane, is how composed he was. He was not raving. He was not frantic. He was polite. He seemed to know exactly what he was doing, exactly how an aircraft worked, exactly what he could ask for and reasonably expect to get. When the plane landed in Seattle, his demands were met. The money and the parachutes were brought aboard. In exchange, he allowed the passengers to leave. He kept a small crew.

Then he gave a new set of instructions. He ordered the plane back into the air, bound for Mexico, and he specified the conditions of the flight in precise detail. He wanted it flown low and slow, with the landing gear down and the wing flaps set a particular way, at an altitude and speed that, not by accident, happened to be exactly right for a man who intended to jump.

Somewhere over the dark forests of the Pacific Northwest, in cold rain and rough weather, Dan Cooper lowered the rear stairway of the aircraft, stepped out into the freezing night sky with the money strapped to his body, and parachuted into the wilderness below.

He was never seen again.

And that is where the mystery truly begins. The largest manhunt imaginable followed. The F B I searched the rugged terrain where he was believed to have landed. They interviewed countless suspects over the years. They built a profile, circulated a sketch, and chased lead after lead across decades. But they never found him. They never identified him with certainty. They never even established, beyond doubt, whether he survived the jump at all. He may have died on impact in the dark woods. He may have walked away a free and wealthy man. We simply do not know.

There was one tantalizing piece of physical evidence. Years later, in nineteen eighty, a young boy digging along the banks of a river found a rotting bundle of cash. Nearly six thousand dollars in worn twenty-dollar bills, and when investigators checked the serial numbers, they matched the ransom money handed to Cooper on that plane. It proved that the money, at least, had ended up in the wilderness. But it answered nothing. How it got to that riverbank, and what became of the man who carried it, remained as much a mystery as ever.

In twenty sixteen, after forty-five years, the F B I officially closed its active investigation. It remains the only unsolved act of air piracy in the history of American commercial aviation.

Here is what stays with me about D. B. Cooper. Unlike almost every other case we tell on this show, this one has no victim in the usual sense. No one was killed. The crew survived. The passengers walked away unharmed. What Cooper stole, more than the money, was an answer. He left behind a question mark that has hung in the air for more than fifty years.

And perhaps that is why he endures, why his name still sparks documentaries and amateur investigations and endless theories. He is the one who got away clean, or who vanished trying. He tapped into something we cannot quite let go of, the fantasy of a man who beat the system completely and stepped off into thin air, taking the truth with him.

Somewhere in those dark forests, or in a quiet life lived under another name, the answer existed. Maybe it still does. But for now, the file is closed, the parachute never found, and the man in the dark suit remains exactly what he made himself that November night. A ghost with a briefcase, falling silently through the rain, into a mystery that has never come back down to earth.

This has been True Crime Brief.
