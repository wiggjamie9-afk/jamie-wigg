Welcome to Longevity Brief. Today we are talking about biomarkers — the measurable indicators of biological aging and health trajectory that go well beyond what a standard annual check-up captures. Understanding which biomarkers matter, what they mean, and how to track them gives you a far more precise picture of where you stand — and what to do about it.

Most people are familiar with the basics: cholesterol, blood pressure, blood sugar. These remain valuable and should not be dismissed. But the landscape of clinically meaningful biomarkers has expanded considerably, and several of the most informative ones are still not routinely ordered by primary care physicians.

Let us start with metabolic markers.

Fasting insulin is one of the most underutilised and informative tests available. Your doctor may test fasting glucose, but that only tells part of the story. Fasting insulin reflects how hard your pancreas has to work to keep blood sugar controlled. Elevated fasting insulin — even with normal blood glucose — indicates insulin resistance, which is an early and modifiable driver of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and cognitive decline. Many experts consider a fasting insulin level above ten micro-units per millilitre a red flag, even if blood glucose is technically normal.

HbA1c, or glycated haemoglobin, provides a three-month average of blood sugar control and is more informative than a single fasting glucose reading. Levels below five point four percent are associated with lower risk of metabolic disease. Levels between five point seven and six point four percent indicate prediabetes — a condition affecting an estimated one in three adults, the vast majority of whom are unaware.

HOMA-IR is a calculated index derived from fasting glucose and fasting insulin that provides a direct estimate of insulin resistance. It can be calculated without expensive testing and gives a single actionable number.

Moving to inflammatory markers.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein, or hs-CRP, is a blood protein produced by the liver in response to systemic inflammation. Unlike standard CRP, the high-sensitivity version can detect low-level chronic inflammation — the kind that smoulders for years without producing obvious symptoms but silently damages blood vessels, neurons, and other tissues. Levels below one milligram per litre are considered optimal. Levels above three milligrams per litre indicate elevated cardiovascular and all-cause mortality risk. It is a relatively cheap test and it is highly actionable — the lifestyle levers that reduce hs-CRP are well-established.

Interleukin-six and tumour necrosis factor-alpha are deeper inflammatory markers sometimes included in functional medicine panels. Elevated levels are associated with accelerated biological aging and increased disease risk.

Cardiovascular-specific markers deserve attention.

ApoB, or apolipoprotein B, is increasingly recognised as superior to LDL cholesterol for estimating cardiovascular risk. Every LDL and VLDL particle contains exactly one ApoB molecule, so ApoB directly counts the number of atherogenic particles in circulation. Two people can have identical LDL-cholesterol readings but very different ApoB counts — and therefore very different risk profiles. Optimal ApoB for most adults is below eighty milligrams per decilitre. For high-risk individuals, below sixty is preferred.

Lp(a), or lipoprotein-a, is a genetically determined cardiovascular risk factor that is not modified by diet or standard lipid medications. Approximately twenty percent of the population carries elevated Lp(a), putting them at significantly higher risk for heart attack and stroke independent of other factors. It is tested once in a lifetime since it does not change. If you have not had it measured, it is worth doing.

Finally, hormonal markers.

DHEA-sulfate is an adrenal hormone that peaks in our mid-twenties and declines steadily with age. It correlates with biological age and is often used as a marker of adrenal reserve and overall hormonal health. It is not a longevity intervention in itself, but it provides context.

Testosterone, oestrogen, and sex hormone-binding globulin are relevant across genders as hormonal changes with age affect body composition, bone density, mood, libido, and cardiovascular risk in ways that are modifiable with appropriate support.

The practice of tracking biomarkers over time — not just as a snapshot but as a trend line — is where real insight comes from. A single reading tells you where you are. A series of readings tells you where you are heading. And knowing where you are heading is what makes intervention meaningful.
