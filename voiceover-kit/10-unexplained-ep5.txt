Hello, this is Austin, and you're listening to Unexplained.

Tonight we sit in a quiet observatory in the American state of Ohio, in the late summer of nineteen seventy-seven. A volunteer is reviewing a printout of radio signals from deep space, page after page of ordinary noise. And then his eye stops. A single burst of signal, far louder than anything around it, lasting just seventy-two seconds. He circles it in red pen and writes a single word in the margin. Wow. For more than forty years, no one has been able to explain it. This is the story of the Wow signal.

To understand why one man wrote that word, we have to understand what he was looking for. In the nineteen seventies, a radio telescope at Ohio State University, nicknamed the Big Ear, was quietly scanning the heavens as part of the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. The idea was simple and patient. If another civilisation out there wanted to be found, the cheapest way to call across the galaxy would be a narrow radio signal, a single clear tone standing out against the static of the universe. So the Big Ear listened, night after night, and a computer printed out what it heard as a long column of numbers and letters, each one a measure of how strong the signal was at a given moment.

On the fifteenth of August, nineteen seventy-seven, the telescope recorded something extraordinary. For seventy-two seconds, a signal rose up out of the background, climbed to a towering peak, and then faded away. On the printout it appeared as a short sequence of characters, six U, one. To the trained eye, this was close to perfect. The signal was narrow, confined to a single channel, exactly the kind of artificial-looking tone the search was hoping for. It sat very near a special frequency that astronomers had long thought a likely choice for interstellar contact, the natural frequency of hydrogen, the most common element in the universe. And the way it rose and fell matched precisely what you would expect from a fixed point in the sky drifting through the telescope's field of view as the Earth turned. A few days later, the astronomer Jerry Ehman looked at that printout, saw the towering spike, and wrote Wow in the margin. The name stuck.

And then came the part that has tormented scientists ever since. They looked again. The Big Ear actually had two feeds, so a genuine sky signal should have appeared twice, a few minutes apart. The Wow signal appeared only once. In the days, months, and years that followed, astronomers pointed telescopes back at that same patch of sky, in the direction of the constellation Sagittarius, again and again. They listened with more sensitive instruments. They never heard it again. One brilliant, deafening shout, and then eternal silence. So let us do what we always do. Let us set the wonder aside, and rank the explanations by the weight of the evidence.

The strongest explanations are the down-to-earth ones, and we should sit with them honestly before reaching for the stars. The most likely culprits are signals of human origin that bounced or leaked into the telescope, a stray transmission, a satellite, a reflection off a piece of space debris. But here the case is genuinely difficult. The frequency at which the signal appeared is a protected band, kept clear by international agreement precisely because it is sacred to radio astronomy. Transmitting there is restricted. And the way the signal rose and fell so smoothly fits a source out in space far better than a passing aircraft or a ground transmitter. So while a terrestrial accident remains possible, it is not the easy answer it first appears to be.

Then comes the next tier, the natural but unusual. The most discussed recent idea is that the signal came from a comet. Two comets, it turns out, happened to be near that region of sky, and comets are surrounded by clouds of hydrogen, which can give off radio energy near that very frequency. It is a tidy idea, and it had its moment in the headlines. But many astronomers pushed back hard. Comets are not known to produce such a strong, narrow, single-channel burst, and when those same comets were later observed, they did not blaze in the way the theory would require. The comet explanation is plausible enough to take seriously, and weak enough that it has not closed the case. Other natural ideas have been raised too, a chance flicker from a distant star, a rare burst from some energetic object, a freak alignment of cosmic noise. None has ever quite fit the shape of what the Big Ear heard.

Then there is the third tier, the further-out idea, the one the evidence supports least but which gives the story its name. That it was exactly what it looked like. A deliberate signal, a narrow tone broadcast across the dark by someone, or something, trying to be heard. This is the possibility that made a quiet astronomer write Wow in red ink. And it is not absurd on its face, the signal had nearly every feature the searchers had been told to look for. But it founders on one stubborn fact. It never came again. A beacon meant to be found would, you would think, keep calling. A single, unrepeatable shout is the one thing hardest to confirm and easiest to doubt.

So where does that leave us? Weigh it honestly, and the truth is that we simply do not know. The strongest position is caution, that this was most likely some natural or human source we have not correctly identified, because extraordinary claims demand evidence we have never been able to gather twice. And yet every ordinary explanation has a loose thread. The protected frequency. The perfect rise and fall. The silence ever after. It is the rarest kind of mystery, one that is neither solved nor quite dismissible, a question mark hanging in the sky toward Sagittarius.

And perhaps that is why it haunts us so. The Wow signal sits precisely on the line between the answer we long for and the disappointment we expect. If it was a comet, or a reflection, or a glitch, then the universe stayed silent, as it always has. But for seventy-two seconds, on a summer night in Ohio, the printout said something else. For seventy-two seconds, it looked exactly like the moment we have been waiting for since we first looked up. And then, before we could be sure, it was gone.

We are drawn to the mystery because it sits exactly where our knowledge ends and our hope begins. We can rule out a great deal. We can model comets and satellites and noise. But we cannot reach back into that one signal, hold it still, and ask it where it came from. That space, the one we cannot reach, is where the legend lives. And it is why, decades later, we are still pointing our telescopes at that empty patch of sky, still listening, still waiting for it to say Wow just one more time.

This has been Unexplained. Sleep well, if you can.
