Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

There is a question the Stoics returned to constantly, almost obsessively, across centuries and across very different lives. Epictetus asked it from a position of enslavement. Marcus Aurelius asked it from the most powerful seat in the known world. Seneca asked it facing exile and, eventually, a forced death. The question was this: what is actually mine?

Not in a legal sense. Not in the sense of property or status or reputation. In the deeper sense of: what cannot be taken from me no matter what happens?

Their answer was consistent. The only thing that is truly yours is your own faculty of choice. The Stoics called it the hegemonikon, the governing faculty, the inner citadel. Everything outside that citadel, your body, your possessions, your circumstances, your reputation, the opinions others hold of you, the length of your life, is subject to forces beyond your control. The inner citadel is not. What you choose to think, what values you hold, how you orient yourself toward whatever is happening, these remain yours even when everything else is stripped away.

Epictetus lived this idea in a way that was not theoretical. He had been enslaved. He had no control over where he went, what he did with his body, or how others treated him. And yet he taught, with remarkable conviction, that no one could enslave his judgement. The tyrant, he said, can put you in chains. But your freedom to choose how you respond to the chains, that he cannot touch.

This is not a comfortable idea if you are hoping philosophy will improve your circumstances. The Stoics were clear that it might not. Marcus went on campaigning in freezing conditions and ruling a difficult empire until he died. Seneca was eventually ordered to take his own life. Epictetus remained marked by the injuries of his enslavement. The philosophy did not rescue them from difficulty. It located freedom somewhere difficulty could not reach.

Most of us will never face those extremes. But the principle scales down into ordinary life with surprising precision. When the external world does not go as you hoped, when the job does not come through, when the relationship changes, when the recognition you expected does not arrive, the Stoic question is steady: what remains in your citadel? What is still yours?

Here is today's practice. Write down one thing in your life that feels genuinely uncertain right now, something whose outcome you cannot control. Now make two columns. In one column, list every aspect of that situation that is outside your influence. In the other, list what is actually yours: your attitude toward it, your effort within it, the care with which you show up for it. The second column is smaller. But it is the only column that is truly yours.

Freedom, in the Stoic sense, is not the absence of constraint. It is knowing exactly where constraint ends.
