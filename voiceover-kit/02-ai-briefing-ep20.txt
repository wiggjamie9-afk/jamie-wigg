Welcome to AI Briefing. Today we are covering AI and the law, specifically the collision between AI-generated content and copyright, which has moved from a speculative debate among legal scholars to active litigation with real defendants and real stakes. If you are building with AI, deploying AI-generated content at scale, or making product decisions that involve training data, this is the episode to stay with.

The core legal question is deceptively simple to state and enormously complicated to answer. When a large language model or image generator is trained on copyrighted work without a license, and that training produces a system capable of generating content, has the copyright of the original work been infringed? And if so, who is liable?

Multiple cases are now working through the courts in the United States and in Europe. A group of authors sued OpenAI, alleging that their books were used to train GPT-series models without consent or compensation. A coalition of visual artists brought similar claims against Stability AI and Midjourney over image generation models. The New York Times filed suit against OpenAI and Microsoft, alleging that Times journalism was used at enormous scale to train models that now compete directly with the Times as an information source.

The companies have mounted several lines of defense, and it is worth understanding each of them because the outcome of these defenses will shape the industry for years.

The first defense is fair use. In United States copyright law, fair use is a doctrine that allows certain uses of copyrighted material without permission, if the use is transformative enough, affects the market for the original in limited ways, and meets several other criteria. The AI labs argue that training is transformative: you are not reproducing the work, you are extracting statistical patterns from it. Critics counter that this is novel enough that existing fair use doctrine does not cleanly apply and that courts should not simply assume it does.

The second defense is more practical. When a model is used, it does not reproduce training data verbatim in most cases. It generates novel outputs. The question then becomes whether those outputs are substantially similar to protected works, which is the traditional copyright infringement test. If a model generates a paragraph that echoes a copyrighted author's style but does not copy their words, that is a different legal question than verbatim reproduction. Style is generally not protectable. Expression is.

The third, and for the industry perhaps the most significant, is the policy argument: that training on publicly accessible data is foundational to how AI works, and that making it legally hazardous would move AI development to jurisdictions with weaker copyright protections, without necessarily protecting the creators the law is meant to serve. This argument is being made in congressional hearings and in regulatory consultations, not just courtrooms.

None of this is settled. Several of the major cases are in early discovery. Courts are still determining what evidence they will require and what legal frameworks apply. The outcomes could range from findings that current training practices are entirely lawful, to requirements for retroactive licensing, to damages awards that change the economics of large-scale model training entirely.

For builders and product teams, the practical implications today are these. If you are generating content at scale for commercial publication, understand whether the model you are using has indemnification provisions, and what those provisions actually cover. Several of the leading model providers have added contractual protections for customers, but the scope varies. If you are training your own models or fine-tuning on data you have gathered, the provenance of that data now matters legally, not just technically. And if your product uses AI-generated content in ways that compete directly with identifiable original sources, that is a risk profile that legal teams are increasingly flagging.

The briefing on AI and copyright: the litigation is real, the legal questions are genuinely open, and the outcomes will materially shape what AI companies can build and how. The next twelve to twenty-four months will produce significant court decisions. Pay attention to them.
