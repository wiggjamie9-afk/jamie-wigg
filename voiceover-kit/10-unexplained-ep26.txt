Winchester Mystery House stands in San Jose, California, and it is one of the most architecturally strange buildings in the United States. It has stairs that lead into ceilings. Doors that open onto sheer drops to the floor below. Windows built into interior walls where no light could ever reach them. Hallways that dead-end in blank walls. A staircase with forty-four steps, each only two centimeters high, that rises just about three meters total. Rooms built inside other rooms. Chimneys that stop just short of the ceiling and serve no function.

Construction began in eighteen eighty-four and did not stop for thirty-eight years, ending only when the owner, Sarah Winchester, died in her sleep in September of nineteen twenty-two.

Sarah Winchester was the widow of William Winchester, whose company produced the Winchester repeating rifle. By the time of her husband's death in eighteen eighty-one, she had inherited around twenty million dollars and a fifty percent stake in the rifle company, making her one of the wealthiest women in America. She had also lost her only child, Annie, who died in infancy in eighteen sixty-six.

The legend says she consulted a medium after her husband's death and was told that her family was being haunted by the spirits of those killed by Winchester rifles. To appease them, she must build a house and never stop building. The moment construction ceased, she would die.

Whether or not Sarah Winchester believed this, construction did not stop. She employed workers around the clock, in shifts, for thirty-eight years. The house grew to one hundred and sixty rooms, covering more than four thousand five hundred square meters. When the San Francisco earthquake struck in nineteen oh six, sections of the house were damaged, and Sarah reportedly had certain sections boarded up and sealed, leaving them untouched inside the larger structure.

The spiritualist explanation is the one the house's history is usually told through. But historians who have studied Sarah Winchester more carefully offer a different reading. She was a woman of significant intelligence and organizational ability who managed a vast construction project for nearly four decades. She may have been genuinely bereaved and genuinely interested in spiritualism, which was enormously popular in the late nineteenth century and was not considered fringe belief among educated Americans of that era. She may have found the endless building project to be a form of purpose after devastating personal losses. She may have simply loved architecture and had the means to indulge it without constraint.

The strange design choices have rational explanations in some cases. Building codes did not exist in the same way they do today. The house grew organically over decades without a master plan. Rooms were added to other rooms. Staircases were begun and then surrounded by later construction. Some of the peculiarities attributed to madness may simply be the result of continuous improvised expansion over nearly forty years.

But not all of them are easy to explain away. The stairs that lead into ceilings serve no construction purpose. The doors that open onto drops are actively dangerous. The windows into interior walls block nothing and illuminate nothing.

Sarah Winchester left no diary. She gave no interviews. She communicated through lawyers and construction supervisors. What she believed, and why she built what she built, died with her in the room where she finally stopped.

The house is still there. The stairs still go nowhere. The doors still open onto empty air.
