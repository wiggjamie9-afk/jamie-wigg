Welcome back to the Parenting Playbook. This episode is about communication — specifically, how to talk to your children in ways that actually get through, and how to listen in ways that make them want to keep talking to you.

One of the most consistent findings in parenting research is this: children whose parents communicate with warmth, clarity, and genuine listening develop stronger language skills, higher self-esteem, and significantly more trusting relationships with their parents during adolescence. The way you talk to your child now is building the relationship you will have with them when they are sixteen.

Let's start with listening. True listening is rarer than we think. Most of us, when our child is talking, are also scanning the kitchen, thinking about the next task, or preparing our response. Children notice this. They notice it very clearly. And they learn quickly whether the effort of bringing you something is worth it.

Active listening with a child looks like this. Stop what you are doing, or at least significantly reduce your divided attention. Get on their physical level if they are small. Make eye contact. Reflect back what they said before you respond. Ask a follow-up question that shows you were actually following. These steps take practice but they are learnable.

Now let's talk about how we speak to children. Research by psychologist Carol Dweck and others has shown that the language we use shapes children's beliefs about themselves and their capabilities. When you say you're so smart, a child learns to protect that identity by avoiding challenges where they might fail. When you say you worked really hard on that and it shows, the same child learns that effort produces growth. The shift is subtle and the impact over years is significant.

Another communication trap worth naming is the lecture. When children are emotionally activated — when they've made a mistake, when they're defensive — the brain is not in a state to absorb long explanations. Short and direct is far more effective. Instead of a five-minute explanation of why what they did was wrong, try two sentences, a pause, and a question. What do you think you could do differently next time? That question does more cognitive work than any lecture could.

Questions in general are underused. Good open questions — what was the best part of your day, what felt hard this week, what are you looking forward to — invite children into conversation rather than interrogating them. Closed questions that demand a specific answer often produce resistance or shutdown, especially in teenagers.

Repair is also part of communication. When you say something you regret — and every parent does — coming back to your child and naming it is not a sign of weakness. I lost my temper earlier and I'm sorry for the way I spoke to you. That models exactly the kind of accountability and emotional maturity you are trying to build in them.

Communication is not just about transmitting information. It is the daily practice of relationship.
