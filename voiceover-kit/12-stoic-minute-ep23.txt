Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

Duty is not a fashionable word. It carries, for many people, a sense of obligation imposed from outside, a weight placed on you by someone else's expectations. But the Stoics meant something different by it, something that came entirely from within.

Marcus Aurelius wrote: Waste no more time arguing what a good man should be. Be one.

The Stoics believed that every human being has a role, not just one role, but several at once. You are a parent, or a child, or a colleague, or a citizen. You are a member of a household and a member of the larger human community. Each of these roles carries with it a set of natural responsibilities, not because someone handed you a rule book, but because the relationships themselves generate obligations. To be a friend is to show up for a friend. To hold a position of authority is to exercise it with care. To be alive in a community is to contribute to it.

Epictetus, who taught enslaved people alongside senators, made duty the centrepiece of his philosophy of freedom. He argued that the person who fulfils their responsibilities with full attention and genuine commitment is freer than the one who avoids them. Avoidance, he said, only creates a different kind of prison: the prison of a life half-lived, of knowing you did not show up.

This is not about self-sacrifice or the erasure of your own needs. The Stoics were not asking anyone to dissolve themselves into their obligations. They were making a more precise point: that the fulfilment of genuine responsibilities, entered into willingly and with care, is one of the primary ways a human being experiences meaning. Not as a burden, but as a form of participation in something larger than oneself.

Marcus, who governed an empire and found it exhausting, kept returning to this in his private writing. He was not performing duty for an audience. He was reminding himself, again and again, that the work in front of him was the work that belonged to him, and that doing it well was the thing that mattered.

Here is today's practice. Think of one responsibility you have been carrying reluctantly, one thing you do with a sense of burden rather than engagement. Now try a different orientation toward it. Not pretending it is easy, but asking: what would it look like to fulfil this particular responsibility as well as I possibly can, not because I have to, but because I have chosen to? The Stoics believed that choice is the whole difference between servitude and dignity.

The work does not change. What changes is who you are when you do it.
