Welcome to Founders' Stories.

Raising money is not a milestone. It's a tool. And like any tool, it can build something extraordinary or it can make a mess, depending entirely on who is holding it and why.

Today I want to talk about fundraising — not the highlight reel version where someone walks out of a partner meeting with a term sheet and a handshake — but the actual experience of raising capital, as told by founders who have been through it more than once.

Priya Nair closed her seed round of eight hundred thousand dollars on a Friday afternoon after forty-seven investor meetings over the course of eleven weeks.

Forty-seven meetings. Eleven weeks.

She tracked every meeting in a spreadsheet. She color-coded responses: green for warm interest, yellow for "following along," red for no. She kept detailed notes on every objection she heard so she could refine her pitch between meetings. She treated fundraising like a product sprint — structured, measurable, relentlessly iterated.

What did she learn?

First: the word "no" almost never comes in the form of "no." It comes as "not right now" or "too early for us" or "great team, wrong sector" or two weeks of email silence. Learning to read soft rejection quickly is one of the most important skills a fundraising founder can develop. Every week you spend chasing a cold lead is a week you're not in front of a warm one.

Second: your narrative matters more than your numbers at the seed stage. Investors are not buying a business at seed. They are buying a hypothesis and a founder. Priya's financials were projections — educated guesses dressed up in spreadsheet clothing. What she actually sold in those meetings was her understanding of the problem, her credibility as someone who could solve it, and her clarity about why now was the right moment.

Third: the fastest way to get a term sheet is to not need one. She told me that the two investors who moved fastest were the ones she talked to after she'd already been in conversations with three other firms. The social proof of being in demand — even if that demand was still soft — created urgency. Scarcity, real or perceived, is the engine of a competitive fundraise.

She also told me something I've heard from nearly every founder who has raised successfully: fundraising will take at least twice as long as you expect, and it will cost you more runway than you planned for. She had budgeted four weeks. It took eleven. During those eleven weeks, she was also trying to run her company. Her co-founder handled most of the product work while she was in pitch mode. They burned through sixty thousand dollars of their personal savings to stay afloat before the round closed.

The week after she closed, she didn't feel the elation she expected. She felt exhausted and mildly terrified. Because now the clock had really started. The capital came with expectations attached.

She said something I haven't been able to stop thinking about since our conversation. She said: "The round closing is not the end of the story. It's the end of the prologue."

If you're in the middle of a fundraise right now, or preparing for one, remember that. You're not raising to win. You're raising to keep building. The money is the fuel, not the destination.
