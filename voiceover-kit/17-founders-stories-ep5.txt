Welcome to Founders Stories. I'm Daniel. Quick note up front, same as always. What you're about to hear is assembled from widely shared public accounts. It's a story, told and retold, not insider reporting. The shape of it holds. Let's begin.

Today's story is about the pivot. About the moment a company throws away the thing it was built to be, and becomes something it never planned to become. And the famous version of this story belongs to a little company that started its life trying to make video games.

Late two thousand and nine. A team of developers set out to build an online game. Not a small one. An ambitious, sprawling, multiplayer world where people would explore and play together. They raised money. They hired a team. They poured years and a fortune into it.

And it failed. Not in a dramatic explosion. In the quiet, grinding way most things fail. The game was clever, but people just didn't play it the way the founders hoped. The numbers wouldn't move. The money was running low. By any honest measure, the company was finished. The thing they had built their lives around simply did not work.

Now here is where almost everyone gives up, and where this story turns. The founder, a man named Stewart Butterfield, had been here before, oddly enough. Years earlier, a different failed game of his had accidentally produced a photo-sharing tool that became Flickr. So he had learned something most founders never get to learn twice: that sometimes the real business is hiding inside the wreckage of the thing you meant to build.

So he asked a strange question. Not, how do we save this game? But, what did we actually build that works? And the answer was sitting right in front of them. To build the game, the team, scattered across different cities, had created an internal tool for talking to each other. A messaging system. A place where the whole team's conversation lived, organized into channels, searchable, with the funny side-conversations and the serious work all in one stream.

They loved it. They couldn't imagine working without it. And that was the clue. The thing they built to support the real work had quietly become more valuable than the real work.

So they made the terrifying decision. They killed the game. The thing they'd spent years and millions building. And they bet everything on the little internal chat tool. They cleaned it up, they gave it a name, Slack, and they pointed it at other teams.

I want to be honest about how big that decision was. They didn't pivot because the chat tool was already a roaring success. They pivoted because they had the courage to admit the original plan was dead, and the clarity to see what was actually alive in the rubble. Most founders cling to the original vision until the money is completely gone. These founders let go while there was still time to catch the new thing.

And the new thing took off in a way the game never did. Because they had felt the pain themselves, deeply, as a real team trying to get real work done, they understood exactly what other teams needed. The product spread from team to team, person telling person, because it solved a problem everyone secretly had and nobody had named. Within a few years, the company that failed at games became one of the fastest-growing business tools in the world, eventually selling for many billions of dollars.

So before I let you go, three lessons. And the thing I love about a good pivot is that the lesson is never about money. It's about attention.

Lesson one. Pay attention to what you build to get the real work done. The byproduct, the side tool, the thing you made just to make the main thing possible, that is often where the gold is. Watch what you can't live without, even when it wasn't the plan.

Lesson two. Letting go is a skill, and it's harder than starting. Anyone can fall in love with a vision. It takes real courage to look at years of your own work and say, this is dead, and the future is over there. Sunk cost is a tax on your judgment. Don't pay it.

And lesson three, the one I find most hopeful. Failure is data, not a verdict. The failed game wasn't wasted. It built the team, the skills, and accidentally, the product that would win. Your dead end might be a corridor. You won't know until you turn around and look at what you're actually holding.

A failed video game became a tool that changed how the world works together. Remember that the next time the plan falls apart, and the future feels like it died with it. It might just be moving rooms.

I'm so glad you spent this time with me. Take care of yourself, and I'll see you on the next one.
