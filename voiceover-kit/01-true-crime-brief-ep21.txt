This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a case that cracked open not because of a detective, not because of a witness, not because of a forensic lab — but because of a genealogy database and a retired investigator who refused to accept that a serial killer had simply gotten away with it. The case is known as the Golden State Killer, and it spans more than a decade of terror across California. What ended it represents a turning point in criminal investigation that is still reshaping the way cold cases are pursued.

Between approximately nineteen seventy-six and nineteen eighty-six, a man committed at least fifty sexual assaults and twelve murders across California. He operated in Sacramento, in the San Francisco Bay Area, and in Southern California. To each of the law enforcement jurisdictions chasing cases in those years, he appeared to be a different criminal. The rapes were handled by one set of investigators. The murders, in a different part of the state, by another. It was not until the nineteen nineties and two thousands that investigators, working across those files, recognized they were looking at the same man — the same DNA, the same behavioral signatures, the same chilling ability to surveil his targets before striking, to learn the layouts of their homes, to terrorize entire neighborhoods with the confidence of someone who believed, correctly, for a very long time, that he would never be caught.

He called himself the East Area Rapist in the Sacramento years. Southern California investigators called the murderer the Original Night Stalker. Paul Holes, a forensic investigator in Contra Costa County, spent years cross-referencing the cases and became one of the people most consumed by the question of who this man was. There was DNA. There had always been DNA. But the DNA matched no one in any criminal database. The man they were looking for had, apparently, never been arrested.

In the mid-two-thousand-and-tens, a new approach began to take shape. Consumer DNA services like Ancestry and twenty-three-and-Me had accumulated tens of millions of genetic profiles, and people were uploading raw DNA files to open genealogy databases like GEDmatch, hoping to find relatives. Law enforcement began to ask a question: could crime scene DNA be uploaded to such a database, not to find the suspect, but to find the suspect's relatives — cousins, second cousins, anyone who shared enough genetic material to map a family tree and narrow the search?

In two thousand and eighteen, Paul Holes and his colleagues tried exactly that. The Golden State Killer's DNA was uploaded to GEDmatch. The database returned matches — not to the killer, but to distant relatives. A genealogist named Barbara Rae-Venter built a family tree from those matches, tracing branches and generations, eliminating candidates based on age and geography, until a single name rose to the top. Joseph James DeAngelo, a former police officer living in a suburb of Sacramento. He was seventy-two years old.

Investigators surveilled him. They collected discarded DNA — from a tissue, from a car door handle. They compared it to the crime scene samples. It matched. In April two thousand and eighteen, DeAngelo was arrested in his driveway. In June two thousand and twenty, he pleaded guilty to thirteen counts of murder and a substantial number of sexual assaults to avoid the death penalty. He is serving multiple consecutive life sentences.

The method used to catch him — investigative genetic genealogy — has since been applied to hundreds of cold cases. It has convicted killers who committed their crimes decades before any forensic database existed. It has also raised significant questions about privacy, about consent, about whether the DNA people upload to find their ancestors can or should be used as a tool of criminal investigation. Some states have moved to regulate the practice. Others have embraced it fully. The debate is not settled.

But here is what is settled. A man who raped and murdered across California for a decade, who believed — with very good reason — that he had escaped justice permanently, was found living in a house not far from where he began. He was betrayed by the DNA of people who had never met him, relatives who uploaded their genetic material looking for family connections, inadvertently leaving a thread that an investigator pulled until it led straight to his door.

This has been True Crime Brief.
