First, let me take some pressure off you. Most people don't get 'hacked' by a genius in a hoodie typing furiously in a dark room. That's the movies. In real life, the bad guys almost always get in through one of two boring doors: a password you reused, or a message that tricked you into handing something over.

That's it. Those two doors. And the good news is, you can lock both of them.

Let's start with passwords.

Here's the problem. You probably have one or two passwords you use for everything, with little tweaks. We all did this. It feels safe because you can remember it. But here's why it's risky: when one website you use gets broken into — and websites get broken into all the time — the criminals get your email and that password. Then they go and try that same combination on your bank, your email, your shopping accounts. This is called 'credential stuffing,' which is a fancy term for 'they try your one password everywhere.' If you reuse passwords, one leak unlocks your whole life.

The fix is something called a password manager. Let me explain what that actually is, because the name sounds more technical than it is.

A password manager is basically a private, locked notebook for all your passwords — except the notebook lives on your phone and computer, it's protected by encryption, which just means the contents are scrambled so only you can read them, and it fills in your passwords for you automatically. You remember ONE strong password to open it. It remembers everything else. And it can create long, random passwords for each site that no human could ever guess.

So my recommendation, by category: get a password manager. You don't need a specific brand — what you want are these features. One: it should work across your phone and your computer. Two: it should offer to fill in passwords automatically so you'll actually use it. Three: it should let you turn on a second lock — I'll explain that in a second. Many phones and web browsers now have a decent one built right in, for free, and that is a perfectly good place to start. The best password manager is the one you'll actually use.

Now that second lock I mentioned. It's called two-factor authentication, and people freeze up when they hear it. So forget the name. Here's what it really is: it's a second little check after your password — usually a code sent to your phone, or a tap on an app — that proves it's really you.

Think of your password as the key to your front door. Two-factor is the deadbolt. Even if a criminal steals your key, they still can't get in without your phone in their hand.

Turn this on for the big three first: your email, your bank, and your main shopping account. Just those three. Your email is the most important one, because if someone controls your email, they can reset the password on everything else. Protect the email, and you've protected the master key.

Last door: the trick. These are scams, and they've gotten very good. A text says your parcel couldn't be delivered. An email says your account is locked. A call says it's your bank's fraud department. They all want one thing — to rush you into clicking a link, or reading out a code, or 'confirming' your details.

Here's the single rule that protects you from almost all of it: slow down, and go to the source yourself. If your bank 'calls,' hang up and call the number on the back of your card. If a text has a link, don't tap it — open your app or type the website in yourself. Real organizations are never hurt by you taking five minutes to check. Only scammers need you to hurry.

And remember: your bank will never ask for your full password or a one-time code. Ever. Anyone who does is a scammer. Full stop.

So that's it. Three things. A password manager so you're not reusing one password everywhere. The second lock — two-factor — on your email, bank, and main shopping account. And the golden rule for scams: when in doubt, slow down and go to the source yourself.

Do just the email two-factor today. That one takes five minutes and protects the most. The rest can wait for the weekend.
