This is Raven, and you're listening to the Longevity Brief. Twelve minutes, one topic, the science with the noise filtered out, and a couple of things you can actually do.

Reminder: general education, not medical advice. Diet is deeply individual. If you have kidney disease, diabetes, or other conditions, your needs differ, so talk to a professional.

Today, the question I get asked more than any other: what should I actually eat to live a long, healthy life? The internet has turned this into a religious war. The honest answer is calmer, and more useful, than any of the camps want it to be.

Let me start by clearing the table. Every few years a new diet is crowned as the answer. Very low carb. Very low fat. All meat. No meat. The marketing is loud and the certainty is total. But when you step back and look at what the long-term evidence actually supports for living longer, the screaming extremes mostly fall away, and what's left is surprisingly boring, and surprisingly consistent across cultures.

So what does that evidence point to? The single best-studied eating pattern for long-term health is the Mediterranean-style way of eating. And I want to be precise about what that means, because it's been flattened into a cliché. It means a base of vegetables, fruit, beans, lentils, whole grains, nuts, and olive oil. Fish and seafood regularly. Some dairy and poultry. Red and processed meat only occasionally. And not much ultra-processed food or added sugar. In one large controlled trial, people assigned to a Mediterranean pattern with extra olive oil or nuts had fewer major heart events than those told simply to eat low fat. That's a stronger kind of evidence than most diet claims can offer.

Now let me pull out the threads that seem to matter most, because you can hit these without obeying any single brand of diet.

First, eat mostly whole, minimally processed food. This may be the most robust finding in all of nutrition. Diets heavy in ultra-processed food, the packaged, engineered, hyper-tasty stuff, are linked again and again with worse health and earlier death. Shifting toward food that looks like food is a large part of the whole game.

Second, get enough protein, especially as you age. This connects straight back to our muscle episode. Many older adults eat too little protein to maintain muscle, and that accelerates the decline we worked so hard to prevent. Beans, fish, eggs, dairy, poultry, and lean meat all count.

Third, make plants the foundation. You don't have to be vegetarian. But the more your plate is built around vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and nuts, the better the long-term picture tends to look.

Let me clear up two myths.

The first myth is that one single nutrient is the enemy. For years it was fat. Then it was all carbs. The truth is that the quality and the overall pattern matter far more than demonizing one macronutrient. Olive oil is fat. Lentils are carbs. Both are part of the healthiest patterns we know.

The second myth is that you need to eat perfectly. You don't, and the pressure to be perfect makes people give up entirely. Longevity eating is about what you do most of the time, across years, not about a flawless week. Consistency beats purity.

So what do you actually do?

One. Crowd out, don't just cut. Instead of fighting to remove bad foods, add good ones, more vegetables, more beans, more fish, until they take up the room. The bad stuff shrinks by displacement.

Two. Cook more of your own food. It's the simplest way to eat fewer ultra-processed products without any rules at all. You control what goes in.

Three. Anchor each meal with protein and a plant. A protein source and a vegetable or legume on every plate quietly builds the whole pattern over time.

The brief on diet: ignore the extremes, lean toward a Mediterranean-style pattern of mostly whole foods, enough protein, and lots of plants, and aim for consistency, not perfection.

One thing this week: pick one regular meal and rebuild it around a protein and a plant, and make it your default.
