Tonight, the story of a trial that divided a nation, a case so saturated with media coverage that the phrase "trial of the century" has been used to describe it ever since, and a verdict that, more than thirty years later, still provokes argument wherever it is raised. On the twelfth of June, nineteen ninety-four, two people were stabbed to death outside a condominium in the Brentwood neighborhood of Los Angeles. Their names were Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman. Nicole Brown Simpson was the ex-wife of Orenthal James Simpson — O.J. Simpson — the former professional football player and sometime actor who had been one of the most celebrated athletes in America. What followed his arrest was something American jurisprudence had never quite seen before.

The physical evidence against Simpson was, by any conventional standard, substantial. Blood matching his was found at the crime scene. Blood matching the victims was found in his vehicle, on a glove recovered at his estate, and in his home. He had no credible alibi for the time of the murders. He had a documented history of domestic violence against Nicole, a record that prosecutors argued demonstrated a pattern of dangerous obsession. On the night of the murders, a limousine driver waiting outside his property could not reach him for an extended period, and witnesses later described seeing a vehicle matching his white Bronco near the crime scene.

And yet the prosecution lost. On the third of October, nineteen ninety-five, after one of the longest and most publicly consumed criminal trials in American history — close to nine months of testimony, three hundred and thirty-seven witnesses, forty-five thousand pieces of evidence — the jury deliberated for fewer than four hours. Simpson was acquitted on both counts of murder.

The verdict exposed a profound divide in American public opinion that followed, to a striking degree, racial lines. Polling conducted in the immediate aftermath found that a majority of white Americans believed Simpson was guilty and the verdict was wrong, while a majority of Black Americans believed the acquittal was just, or at minimum that the system had behaved exactly as it should when the evidence was tainted and the institution conducting the investigation was compromised.

That word — compromised — was at the center of the defense strategy. The defense team, which included lawyers Barry Scheck and Peter Neufeld, who had pioneered the use of DNA evidence to exonerate wrongly convicted people, turned their expertise around and attacked the DNA evidence against Simpson. They argued that the Los Angeles Police Department's forensic operation was incompetent, that the chain of custody for key samples was broken, and that blood had been planted or contaminated. Detective Mark Fuhrman, who had found the glove at Simpson's estate, was exposed on audiotape using a racial slur, and the defense leveraged his credibility crisis to maximum effect.

The infamous glove that did not appear to fit Simpson's hand during a courtroom demonstration became the image the trial is most remembered by. The prosecution had made a critical error in asking Simpson to try it on in front of the jury. The defense, with characteristic precision, built a closing argument around it. If it does not fit, attorney Johnnie Cochran told the jury, you must acquit.

It is important to understand that Simpson was later held liable for the deaths in a civil trial in nineteen ninety-seven, where the burden of proof is lower and where the jury found, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he had committed the killings. He was ordered to pay thirty-three point five million dollars in damages to the victims' families, an amount he largely never paid.

Simpson died in April two thousand and twenty-four. The criminal case remains closed. The murders of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ronald Goldman are, in law, officially unsolved.

What the Simpson trial gave America was not a clean answer. It gave the country a mirror, and it did not like what it saw — a legal system that operated differently depending on the resources of the defendant, a police force that had earned distrust through its own conduct, a media apparatus that had turned a double murder into a spectacle, and a public that could not agree on what justice looked like or whose version of events to believe. Those questions did not end when the verdict was read. In many ways, they are still being answered.
