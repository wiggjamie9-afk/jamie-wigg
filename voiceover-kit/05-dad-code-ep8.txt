# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 8
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. After last week, which was a heavy one, all that online safety stuff, today we get to do something I genuinely love. Today we're talking about building things together. Making something with your hands, alongside your kid. And I want to make the case that this quiet, simple, slightly old-fashioned thing might be one of the most powerful tools you've got.

Let me start with a picture. Me and my son, on the kitchen floor, surrounded by the wreckage of a flat-pack bookshelf. Tiny screws everywhere. The instructions, which appear to have been written by someone who hates joy. And him, four years old, handing me the wrong piece every single time with total confidence. And it took four times longer than if I'd just done it alone. And it was, hand on heart, one of the best afternoons we've had all year.

Here's what I figured out about why. When you build something with your kid, you take all the pressure off the talking. You ever notice how kids clam up the second you sit them down and go, so, how are you feeling. Direct attention makes them squirm. But put a kid next to you, both of you working on the same thing, hands busy, eyes on the task, not on each other, and they start to talk. The real stuff comes sideways. Shoulder to shoulder, not face to face. That's how a lot of us are built, dads especially. We open up when we're doing, not when we're being interrogated.

So a building project, and I mean this loosely, isn't really about the bookshelf, or the birdhouse, or the volcano made of baking soda. The thing you make is just the excuse. The real product is the time. The conversation that sneaks out while your hands are full. The bookshelf is the cover story. The kid is the project.

And there's a second thing going on, deeper than the chat. When a kid helps build something real, something that ends up actually existing in the house, they get a hit of something that no amount of praise can give them. They get competence. They point at that wobbly shelf for the next six months going, I made that. And they did. They learn, in their body, that they are a person who can make things happen in the real world. In a world that increasingly hands kids a glowing screen where everything is done for them, the experience of making a real, physical, slightly wonky thing with your own two hands is becoming rare. And rare things are valuable.

Now, the practical bit, because I know some of you are thinking, I'm not handy, I can barely hang a picture. Good news. It does not matter, at all. Three things to keep in mind.

One. Lower the bar to the floor. This is not about the finished object being good. If you're chasing a perfect result, you'll micromanage, you'll snatch the tools back, you'll ruin it. The goal is not a beautiful birdhouse. The goal is the kid feeling like a real part of making it. A terrible birdhouse built together beats a perfect one you did while they watched. Every time.

Two. Give them a real job, not a fake one. Kids smell a fake job a mile off. Holding a piece of wood that doesn't need holding, they know. So find them something that genuinely matters. You're in charge of the screws. Your job is to test if it's level. You hand me the next piece. Real responsibility, sized for them, makes them stand up taller.

Three. Let it be slow, and let it be imperfect, and let them mess it up. The wonky bit they did themselves will mean more to them than the neat bit you fixed. Resist the urge to come along behind them and make it tidy. That wonky corner is the proof that they were really there, really part of it. Leave it. Honor it.

And it doesn't have to be woodwork, I want to be clear. Build a fort out of sofa cushions. Cook a meal together, badly. Plant something and keep it alive together. Fix the squeaky gate. Make a den. The principle is the same. Two people, one shared task, hands busy, no pressure, side by side. That's the magic. The activity is almost irrelevant.

Here's what I want to leave you with. Your kid is not going to remember the perfect, store-bought, beautifully assembled life you tried to give them. They're going to remember the afternoon you both crawled around the floor swearing gently at a flat-pack, and laughed, and made a wobbly thing that's still standing. That's the stuff that lands. Not the polish. The presence. The shared mess.

Next week is our last in this little run, and I've saved something big. We're talking about the long game. The kind of man you're actually becoming through all of this, and the version of himself your kid is quietly learning to be by watching you.

This has been Dad Code. Now go and build something terrible with your kid this weekend. It'll be perfect.
