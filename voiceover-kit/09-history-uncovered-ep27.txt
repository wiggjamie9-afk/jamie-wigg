Every generation believes it is living through the most turbulent period in history. And every generation is, in its own way, correct.

But there is a particular kind of turbulence that is easy to miss while you are inside it — the slow, structural kind, where the ground shifts so gradually that you do not notice until the landscape looks nothing like it used to.

We are living through that kind now. And we have lived through it before.

In the fifteen hundreds and fifteen hundreds, Europe passed through what historians call the print revolution. Johannes Gutenberg's printing press, developed around fourteen forty, did not immediately transform society. For the first few decades, it mostly reproduced manuscripts that already existed — Bibles, classical texts, legal documents. The presses were expensive, the typesetters were few, and the literate population was small.

But over the following half century, something tipped. Prices for books fell by roughly eighty percent. Literacy began to spread beyond monks, merchants, and nobles for the first time in European history. Ideas that had previously been confined to universities began circulating through city streets in pamphlet form.

Martin Luther published his Ninety-Five Theses in fifteen seventeen. Within two months, copies had spread across Germany. Within two years, they had reached England. The Reformation was not caused by the printing press, but it would not have moved at that speed without it. Luther himself understood this — he used the press more deliberately and more effectively than almost any figure of his era.

What the print revolution actually caused was an explosion of the information supply without a corresponding expansion of the tools to evaluate it.

Suddenly there were hundreds of books. Then thousands. Educated Europeans found themselves confronted with more text than any person could read in a lifetime, much of it contradicting much else of it. The consensus reality that the Catholic Church had maintained for centuries — a single, authoritative interpretation of truth — began to fragment.

This produced extraordinary things. Scientific inquiry. Democratic theory. Religious diversity. The beginnings of the modern world as we recognize it.

It also produced extraordinary chaos. Religious wars that killed millions. Propaganda campaigns of breathtaking sophistication. A century in which it became genuinely unclear what was true and what was not, because anyone with access to a press could now make authoritative-looking claims about anything.

The parallels to our current moment are not subtle.

The internet has collapsed the cost of publishing to approximately zero. The global supply of information has increased faster in the past thirty years than in all of human history before it. The institutions that previously served as arbiters of truth — newspapers, universities, governments, churches — have lost much of their authority without being replaced by anything with equivalent reach.

And just as with the printing press, the chaos and the creativity are arriving together. The same technology that spreads misinformation at unprecedented scale also lets a historian in Lagos publish primary source research to readers in Oslo. It lets a grandmother in rural India learn to code. It lets suppressed communities find each other, build movements, and make demands that authoritative institutions can no longer simply ignore.

The people who lived through the print revolution did not know they were living through the print revolution. They knew there were a lot more books than there used to be. They knew some of those books were saying things the Church found dangerous. They knew something was shifting, but they did not have a name for it or a model for where it was going.

We are in the same position now. We are inside the shift. We cannot see its shape from here.

History suggests the outcome will not be simply good or simply bad. It will be complicated in ways we cannot yet anticipate, producing possibilities we cannot yet imagine, and destroying certainties we have not yet noticed we depend on.

This is not a warning. It is an invitation to pay attention.

The people who navigate great transitions most effectively are not the ones who resist change or the ones who embrace it blindly. They are the ones who look clearly at what is happening, learn from what has happened before, and choose their next step with open eyes.

That is the practice history makes possible. And it is why history still matters — perhaps now more than ever.
