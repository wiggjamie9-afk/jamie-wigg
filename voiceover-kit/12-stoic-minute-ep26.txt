Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

The Stoics are often misread as individualists. There is something in the language of the inner citadel, the focus on what is yours alone, that can sound like a philosophy of withdrawal, as if the goal were to become so self-contained that other people barely register. That reading misses something essential.

The Stoics were among the most emphatic communal thinkers in the ancient world. They coined the word cosmopolitan. Marcus Aurelius wrote: What injures the hive injures the bee. And: We are made for cooperation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of upper and lower teeth.

The Stoic picture of the human being is not a self-sufficient atom. It is a node in a network of obligations and relationships that extends outward in concentric circles: from the household to the neighbourhood to the city to the empire to all of humanity. The philosopher Hierocles described this as a set of rings, with the self at the centre and the rings expanding outward. The task of philosophical practice was, in his phrase, to draw those outer rings toward the centre, to feel as much concern for a distant stranger as you feel for yourself.

This sounds utopian. The Stoics did not pretend it was easy. Marcus spent decades ruling over millions of people he would never meet, trying to make decisions that treated their wellbeing as genuinely mattering. He often failed. He knew he often failed. But the aspiration held: that the life lived only for one's own interests is a life lived too small.

The connection between inner work and outward responsibility was, for the Stoics, inseparable. You could not become a good person in isolation, because being a good person was essentially relational. Virtue was demonstrated in how you treated others, what you gave to your community, whether you showed up when showing up was difficult. The Stoic who retreated from community, who used philosophical language to justify detachment from the needs of those around them, had, in the Stoic view, missed the point entirely.

Here is today's practice. Think of one person in your immediate circle who could use something from you this week. Not a grand gesture, just something real: an hour of genuine attention, an honest word of encouragement, the completion of something you have been delaying that affects them. The Stoic act of community is rarely spectacular. It is usually small, consistent, and chosen rather than compelled.

Marcus wrote: The fruit of this life is good character and acts for the common good. Community is not the price of Stoic life. It is the point of it.
