Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. I want to start today with a feeling, rather than a scene. It's that moment when your child's big emotion crashes into you, and you feel your own start to rise to meet it. They're furious, or terrified, or sobbing as though the world has ended over something small, and you can feel your own heart speeding up, your own jaw tightening, your own voice wanting to get louder. Today, we're talking about emotional regulation, and about something quietly powerful called co-regulation, which is the secret engine underneath it all.

Let me begin with the single most important idea in this whole episode, and I want you to really hold on to it. Children do not calm themselves down by being told to calm down. They learn to manage their feelings by borrowing ours first. A young child's brain simply isn't finished yet. The part that handles self-control, the part that can pause and soothe a big feeling, is still years away from being fully built. So when a storm hits, they cannot reach for calm on their own, because they don't yet have it inside. They reach for ours. That borrowing is what we mean by co-regulation, and it is how every human being learns to handle emotion in the first place.

So let's understand what's really happening in a big feeling. When a child is overwhelmed, the thinking part of their brain effectively goes offline. The alarm part takes over. In that state, reasoning, lectures, and consequences simply cannot land, because the part that would hear them isn't running. This is why talking sense to a child mid-meltdown so rarely works. They're not being defiant. They genuinely can't access logic in that moment. First they need to feel safe and calm. Only then can the thinking brain come back online.

Here are some principles that hold up.

One. Regulate yourself first. This is the whole foundation. If you bring panic or anger, you pour fuel on the fire. If you bring calm, you lend it to them. Before you do anything else, take your own slow breath, drop your shoulders, soften your voice. You are the thermostat, not the thermometer. Your calm is the medicine.

Two. Connect before you correct. When the feeling is at its peak, lead with warmth, not instruction. Get down to their level. Stay close. A steady hand, a quiet voice, a simple I'm here, I've got you. The lesson can come later, once the storm has passed. In the height of it, your job is to be the safe harbour.

Three. Name the feeling. Help put words to what's happening inside. You're really angry that we had to leave. You were so frightened. Naming a feeling helps tame it, because it turns a wild, wordless storm into something understandable and shared. Over time, the words you give them become the words they'll use on themselves.

Four. Allow the feeling, while holding the limit. Co-regulation is not giving in. You can completely accept the emotion and still keep the boundary. It's okay to be furious that we're not buying sweets. And we're still not buying them. The feeling is always allowed. The behaviour still has limits.

Five. Teach the calming tools when everyone is calm. You cannot teach a skill in the middle of a crisis. In the peaceful moments, practise the slow breaths, the cosy corner, the squeeze of a soft toy, together, as a game. Then, in the storm, you're reaching for something familiar rather than inventing it on the spot.

Let me give you a few scenarios.

Scenario one. The full meltdown in a public place. This is the hardest, because you feel watched. Lower your own system first. Then get close, lower your voice, and ride it out beside them rather than trying to argue them out of it. Your steady presence is doing the work, even when it looks like nothing is happening.

Scenario two. The child who is frightened, clinging, and can't be reasoned with. Don't try to talk them out of the fear. Lend them your calm body instead. Hold them, breathe slowly and a little loudly so they can match you, and let your steadiness become theirs.

Scenario three. The older child who slams off in a rage. They may not want to be held, and that's fine. Stay nearby. Let them know you're not going anywhere, and that you'll be ready to talk when the wave has passed. Presence without pressure is still co-regulation.

Now, the bigger picture, and it's an encouraging one. Every time you help your child through a big feeling with your own calm, you're not just surviving the moment. You're building the very wiring they'll one day use to soothe themselves. This is slow work, repeated thousands of times across childhood. Self-control grows gradually, year by year. A child who still falls apart easily is not behind. They're exactly where childhood is meant to be.

And do give yourself grace here. No parent stays calm every single time. You will lose your own composure sometimes, and that's part of being human. When it happens, you repair. You come back, you reconnect, and that mending teaches its own valuable lesson.

One note on wellbeing. If your child's emotional storms are extremely intense, very frequent, last a long time, or seem far beyond what you'd expect for their age, or if their distress is interfering with daily life, it's worth a gentle conversation with your doctor or health visitor. Sometimes a child needs a little extra support to learn these skills, and seeking it early is a strength.

So here's where I'd begin. The next time a big feeling hits, before you do anything else, regulate yourself. One slow breath, soft voice, get close. Connect before you correct, name the feeling, and save the lesson for later. Be the calm they get to borrow.

You're not raising a child who never falls apart. You're raising one who, piece by piece, is learning how to put themselves back together, using the calm you lent them first.
