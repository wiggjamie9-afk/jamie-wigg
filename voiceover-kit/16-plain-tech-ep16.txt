Welcome back to Plain Tech. Really glad you are here.

Today we are talking about Wi-Fi. Specifically, the thing that drives most people absolutely mad: the internet that works fine in the lounge but goes completely dead the moment you walk into the bedroom, the backyard, or the kitchen. You know the walk. The shuffle toward the router holding your phone up like you are trying to find a signal in a disaster movie.

Let us fix that. Or at least, let us understand why it happens, because once you understand it, the fix is pretty obvious.

Here is the plain truth about Wi-Fi. Your router — that box your internet provider gave you, probably tucked in a corner somewhere — is basically a little radio. It sends a signal outward in all directions, like ripples on a pond. The further you get from the middle, the weaker the ripple. And anything that gets in the way — a wall, a floor, a fridge, a fish tank, a mirror — weakens the signal even more.

So the first and most overlooked fix is simply where your router lives. If it is sitting on the floor in the corner of a room at one end of the house, you are making life hard for yourself. Ideally, a router should be in the middle of the space you want to cover, up off the floor, out in the open, not stuffed inside a cupboard or behind the television. Moving a router from a cupboard to an open shelf can sometimes feel like you got a completely new internet connection. That is not an exaggeration.

Now, walls vary a lot. A thin plasterboard wall barely slows Wi-Fi down. A thick brick wall or a concrete floor is a serious obstacle. This is why older houses with solid walls tend to have worse Wi-Fi coverage than newer ones. It is just physics. The signal cannot push through that much material.

So what do you do if moving the router helps but still does not reach the back bedroom or the garage?

There are two good solutions, and which one you choose depends on how far the problem area is and how much trouble you want to go to.

The first is something called a Wi-Fi extender or a repeater. This is a small plug-in device, often about the size of a large plug adapter, that you put roughly halfway between your router and the place with no signal. It picks up your router's signal and rebroadcasts it further. They are inexpensive, they plug into a normal wall socket, and setting them up usually involves pressing one button and following a quick app. The downside is that they can slow your connection down a little, and if you have a big house, one extender might not be enough.

The second solution, which is better if you have several problem areas or a larger home, is something called a mesh system. Mesh is just a fancy word for a set of two or three small boxes that all work together as one network. You put one near your router and the others around the house. They talk to each other and create one blanket of Wi-Fi that follows you from room to room without dropping. You never notice the handoff. Mesh systems cost more than a single extender but for a house with multiple dead zones, they genuinely transform the experience.

One more thing worth knowing. Your router broadcasts on what are called frequency bands. You may have seen them labeled as two point four gigahertz and five gigahertz in your Wi-Fi settings, or your router might just give them two different names. The quick version: the five gigahertz band is faster but shorter range. The two point four gigahertz band is slower but travels further and pushes through walls better. If you are far from the router or on a different floor, connecting to the two point four band may actually give you a more reliable connection even if it looks slower on paper. Reliable beats fast-but-dropping every time.

So the short version of today. Move your router somewhere central and out in the open. If you still have dead spots, a cheap extender for one problem area or a mesh system for a whole house will sort it. And if the back room is patchy, try the two point four band first.

One homework suggestion for today: just go and look at where your router is. If it is hidden in a cupboard or shoved against an outside wall, try moving it somewhere more central. It costs nothing, and it might solve the whole problem in five minutes.
