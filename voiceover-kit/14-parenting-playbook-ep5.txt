Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. Picture the dinner table. You've cooked something lovely, something balanced, something you were quietly proud of. And there sits your child, arms folded, lower lip out, announcing that they don't like it. They haven't tasted it. They're not going to taste it. And what was meant to be a warm family meal slowly turns into a standoff. Today, we're talking about picky eating, the fussy eater, and the mealtime battles that wear so many parents down.

Let me begin with the thing I most want you to hear. A child who refuses broccoli is not a child you are failing. Picky eating is one of the most common stages of early childhood, and for most children, it is exactly that, a stage. Understanding why it happens, and learning how not to make it worse, can take an enormous amount of stress out of your days.

So let's look at the why. First, around the ages of two and three, many children become naturally more cautious about new foods. There's even a name for it, food neophobia, a wariness of the unfamiliar. From an evolutionary point of view, this actually made sense. A small human just learning to roam the world being suspicious of strange new things in their mouth was a small human who stayed safe. So that flat refusal of the green thing on the plate is, in part, an ancient instinct doing its job.

Second, mealtimes are one of the very few places where a small child holds real power. They can't decide their bedtime, their clothes, or where the family goes. But they can absolutely decide whether to open their mouth. For a child hungry for some control over their world, the dinner plate becomes a stage. And the more we push, the bigger that stage becomes.

So here is the single most important idea in this whole episode, and it comes from a feeding expert named Ellyn Satter. It's called the division of responsibility. It goes like this. You, the parent, decide what food is offered, and when, and where. Your child decides whether to eat, and how much. That's the whole deal. You provide. They decide. When you try to control how much goes in, you cross into their half of the deal, and that's where the battles begin.

Let me turn that into principles you can actually use. One. Offer without pressure. Put the food on the table, including at least one thing you know they'll usually eat, and then step back. No bribing, no coaxing, no aeroplane spoon, no one more bite for mummy. Pressure is the surest way to make a food less appealing.

Two. Keep offering, even after a no. Here's a fact that surprises a lot of parents. A child may need to see a new food ten, fifteen, even twenty times before they'll try it. Each calm offering is a deposit. Every plate where the new food simply sits there, unremarked, is quiet progress. A no today is not a no forever.

Three. Don't become a short order cook. You don't need to make a separate meal for every refusal. Offer the family meal, with one safe food they like alongside it, and trust that a healthy child will not starve themselves at a table with food on it. Making a brand new dinner on demand teaches them that refusing is the way to get what they want.

Four. Drop the dessert as a reward. When we say, eat your vegetables and then you can have pudding, we accidentally tell the child that vegetables are the price and pudding is the prize. That makes the vegetable the enemy and the sweet thing the hero. Try to let all the foods just be foods.

Let me give you two quick scenes. Scene one. The child who gags or panics at new textures. Go gentle and go slow. Let them touch it, sniff it, lick it, with zero pressure to swallow. Tasting is a ladder with many rungs, and just looking at it on the plate without crying is rung one. Celebrate the small rungs.

Scene two. The mealtime that's become a nightly war. Call a ceasefire. For a week or two, simply serve, sit, eat your own meal, chat about the day, and pay no attention at all to what does or doesn't go into their mouth. Often, when the pressure leaves the table, so does the resistance.

Now, an important note. Most fussy eating is a passing phase. But if your child is losing weight, has very few foods they'll accept, gags or chokes often, or if you're genuinely worried about their growth or nutrition, please speak to your doctor or health visitor. There are wonderful specialists for exactly this, and reaching out is a wise and loving thing to do.

And here's my closing thought. The dinner table should be one of the warmest places in your child's day, not a battlefield. Your job is not to win the meal. Your job is to keep offering good food, with a calm heart, in a happy place, again and again. Trust the long game. Be gentle with them, and be just as gentle with yourself. You're doing better than you think. I'll see you next time on Parenting Playbook.
