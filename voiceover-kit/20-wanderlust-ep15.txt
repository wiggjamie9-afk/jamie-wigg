Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today, no single country, but the single best way I know to truly understand any country you ever visit. I want to talk to you about eating like a local. About food not as fuel, and not even as pleasure, though it is certainly that, but as the fastest, deepest, most joyful doorway into a culture that exists. Close your eyes and picture a tiny restaurant down a side street in a city you have never been to. There is no sign in English. There is no menu you can read. There are six tables, all full of local people, and a grandmother in the back stirring an enormous pot that has clearly been simmering since before you were born. The whole place smells of garlic and smoke and something you cannot name and already want. You hesitate in the doorway. And then you go in. That single decision, to walk through that door, is the difference between visiting a country and actually tasting it.

Here is the truth at the heart of today. You can read every guidebook, see every monument, photograph every famous view, and still come home not really knowing a place. But sit down and eat what the people eat, where they eat it, the way they eat it, and you understand them in a way no museum will ever teach you. Food carries the whole history of a place. The spices tell you who traded here and who conquered here. The ingredients tell you what the land gives and what the sea provides. The way people share a meal tells you everything about how they live together. To eat like a local is to read a country in its own language.

So let me give you the rules, hard-won, for finding the real food and avoiding the traps.

Number one. The golden rule. Walk away from the famous square. Restaurants in the most beautiful, most photographed, most central spots are almost always the worst and the most expensive, because they never need a single customer to return. They live on tourists passing through once. Walk five, ten minutes away from the postcard view, into the streets where ordinary people actually live, and the food gets better and cheaper with every step.

Number two. Follow the crowds, the local crowds. A restaurant full of local families on a weekday evening is telling you something true. An empty restaurant with a man outside waving a laminated menu and calling out to tourists in four languages is telling you something true as well. Trust the queue of grandmothers, never the man with the menu.

Number three. Eat where it is fresh and fast. The busiest food stall, the one with the longest line, is also the safest, because nothing sits, everything turns over, the ingredients arrive and leave the same day. Slow, empty kitchens are where food sits and trouble grows. Busy is clean.

Number four. The menu test. If the menu is translated into six languages and has glossy photographs of every dish, you are eating in a tourist factory. If the menu is short, handwritten, in the local language only, and changes with the day, you have found gold. A short menu means they make a few things and make them well.

Number five. Eat at the right time, the local time. In Spain, no local eats dinner before nine. In much of the world, lunch is the great meal of the day, not dinner. Eat when the locals eat, and you will find the kitchens at their freshest and their best, cooking for their own people rather than for the early tourists.

Number six. Go to the market. Before you eat anywhere, spend a morning in the local food market. It is the beating heart of every food culture on earth. See what is piled high and cheap, because that is what is in season and that is what you should be ordering. Talk to the vendors. Taste what they offer you. Buy a little fruit, a little cheese, a little bread, and make yourself a picnic. A market is a free education in a country's entire cuisine.

Number seven, and the bravest. Order the thing you cannot identify. Point at what the person at the next table is eating. Say yes to the dish the waiter recommends when you ask, what should I have. The single greatest meals of my travelling life have all been things I could not name and would never have ordered if I had understood the menu.

And here is the deeper thing, the reason this matters more than any other travel advice I could give you. When you eat someone's food, the food their mother made, the food their country is proud of, and you eat it with obvious joy, you are paying that culture the highest compliment there is. You are saying, your way of living is worth tasting. Doors open. Strangers smile. A meal becomes a friendship. More of my fondest memories of the world happened across a table than anywhere else.

So your homework this week. On your next trip, give yourself one rule. For one meal, eat something you cannot pronounce, in a place with no English menu, surrounded by local people. Walk through the door you would normally walk past. It is, I promise you, where the whole trip has been hiding all along.
