Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we're going somewhere I have wanted to take you for a long time. Close your eyes and picture a narrow lane in the old quarter of Kyoto, just after dawn. The cobblestones are still damp from the night, the shopfronts shuttered with dark wooden slats, and somewhere down the street a single bicycle bell rings once and then is gone. The air smells of cedar and of something sweeter, the first batch of red bean paste warming in a confectioner's window. This is Japan. And nowhere I have ever traveled rewards the patient, curious traveler quite like it.

Most people arrive in Japan with a list. The neon canyons of Tokyo, the temples of Kyoto, the deer of Nara who bow for crackers, a glimpse of a snow-capped mountain from a fast train. And those things are wonderful, every one of them. But Japan opens slowly, like a paper screen sliding back, and the real country lives in the quiet details just behind the postcard.

Let me give you the feeling first, and then the practical tips. Imagine slipping the curtain aside on a tiny restaurant down a side street, room enough for eight stools and one chef. No menu in any language you read. You point, you smile, you trust. And what arrives is the best meal of your trip, made by hands that have made that one dish for forty years. That is the heart of traveling in Japan. Mastery, humility, and an almost spiritual care for small things.

Now, here is how to do it well. First, get the rail pass before you arrive, if it still suits your route, and do the maths, because the rules have changed in recent years. The bullet train, the shinkansen, is a marvel. It glides out of the station to the second, silent and smooth, and the whole length of the country opens up to you. Reserve a seat on the right-hand side heading west out of Tokyo, and on a clear day Mount Fuji will appear in your window like a held breath.

Second, learn five words. Hello, thank you, excuse me, delicious, and please. That is all. The effort matters more than the accuracy, and you will watch faces soften every single time. Japan rewards courtesy more than confidence.

Third, carry a little cash. For all its technology, Japan still loves coins and notes, especially in the small places where you most want to eat. And get a transit card the moment you land. One tap, and the trains, the buses, even the vending machines belong to you.

Fourth, stay one night in a ryokan, a traditional inn. Sleep on a futon on woven tatami mats. Bathe in the hot spring waters of the onsen, the way the country has bathed for centuries. Wear the cotton robe to dinner and let someone bring you ten small dishes you cannot name. It is not cheap, but it is one night, and you will remember it for the rest of your life.

Fifth, go to a convenience store. I am completely serious. The humble Japanese convenience store is a small miracle. Fresh rice balls wrapped so the seaweed stays crisp, hot coffee, fried chicken, soft egg sandwiches at three in the morning. It is the secret backbone of every budget traveler's trip.

And here is my one quiet piece of advice. Leave a day with nothing in it. No temple, no train, no plan. Just walk a neighborhood, follow a canal, sit in a small park and watch the city be itself. Japan does not shout its beauty at you. It waits, politely, for you to slow down enough to notice.

So your homework this week. Find one Japanese phrase, just one, and learn to say it out loud until it feels natural in your mouth. Plant that seed, and let it grow into a journey.

Travel gently, travel curiously, and remember, the most beautiful place you'll ever discover might just be the quiet you find when you finally slow down. I'll be here next time. Until then, keep dreaming with your eyes open.
