There is a city in present-day Mali, deep in the Sahara, that was once the most important center of learning in the world.

Its name is Timbuktu. You have probably heard it used as shorthand for somewhere impossibly remote, impossibly irrelevant. The phrase "from here to Timbuktu" is used to mean nowhere in particular, a place beyond the edge of the map.

In the fifteenth century, Timbuktu held a university with twenty-five thousand students, libraries containing more than seven hundred thousand manuscripts, and a population of around one hundred thousand people — making it larger than most European cities of the same era.

The Mali Empire, which preceded it, and the Songhai Empire, which incorporated it, were among the wealthiest states in history. Mansa Musa, ruler of Mali from thirteen twelve to thirteen thirty-seven, is sometimes estimated to have been the wealthiest individual who has ever lived — his fortune, adjusted for modern terms, exceeding four hundred billion dollars. When he made his famous pilgrimage to Mecca in thirteen twenty-four, he traveled with a retinue of sixty thousand men and eighty camels carrying pure gold. He distributed so much gold along the route through Egypt that he collapsed the gold market in Cairo for more than a decade.

His pilgrimage was not just an act of faith. It was a deliberate diplomatic exhibition. Mansa Musa was announcing to the world that West Africa existed, that it was powerful, that it was educated, and that it was rich.

The announcement worked. Within years of his return, Timbuktu had attracted scholars from across the Islamic world. Libraries were built. Mosques were constructed. The Sankore Mosque became the center of an informal university where mathematics, astronomy, history, philosophy, and Islamic law were all taught at the highest level.

The manuscripts produced in Timbuktu during this period are staggering in their scope. They cover medicine, mathematics, astronomy, literature, and theology. Some contain marginal notes showing that generations of readers engaged critically with the texts. These were not passive copies. They were living documents in an active intellectual tradition.

Approximately three hundred thousand of these manuscripts are estimated to have survived into the twentieth century, held in private family libraries by descendants of the scholars who wrote them. In two thousand twelve, when extremist forces occupied Timbuktu and began destroying what they called un-Islamic artifacts, local librarians and families staged a remarkable rescue operation — smuggling hundreds of thousands of manuscripts out of the city by donkey, by boat, and by hand, storing them in secret locations until they could be secured.

The manuscripts survived. Many now sit in climate-controlled archives in Bamako, the capital of Mali, where they are being digitized by an international team of scholars.

The story of their survival is as remarkable as the story of their creation.

But here is the question worth sitting with: why did we need to rediscover Timbuktu at all?

The answer lies in how history was written during and after the era of European colonization. African scholarship, African architecture, African governance, and African intellectual traditions were systematically erased from the record — not because they did not exist, but because acknowledging them complicated the narrative used to justify conquest.

A continent of rich, sophisticated, literate civilizations was portrayed as a continent of primitive peoples requiring European civilization.

The manuscripts in Timbuktu's libraries existed the entire time. The scholars who wrote them existed. The empire that sustained them existed.

We simply stopped looking.
