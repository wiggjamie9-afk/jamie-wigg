This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a case that begins with a woman who simply went to work and never came home. It is the morning of the twenty-second of March, two thousand and six. Imette St. Guillen, a twenty-four-year-old graduate student studying forensic psychology at John Jay College in New York City, had spent the previous evening with a friend at a bar in Manhattan called The Falls. Her friend left early. Imette stayed. She was seen on surveillance footage inside the bar sometime after four in the morning. She is not seen leaving under her own power. She was reported missing the following day. Her body was found that same afternoon on a remote stretch of road in Brooklyn, wrapped in a floral bedsheet, her wrists bound with a plastic zip tie.

What investigators found at the scene was methodical and deliberate. Someone had taken time. Someone had tried to conceal what they had done, and done it with a kind of cold organization that pointed away from impulsive violence and toward premeditation. The case immediately drew attention in New York — a young woman, a student of forensic science of all things, gone from a bar in the middle of the night with no witness to what happened next.

The break came from an unlikely source. During the investigation, detectives pulled the phone records of everyone connected to The Falls, including the employees working that night. One of those employees was a man named Darryl Littlejohn, a bouncer. His phone had received a call in the early morning hours of the same night Imette disappeared, from a cell tower near the location where her body was later found. That alone was suggestive. What detectives found next sealed it.

On the zip tie used to bind her wrists, forensic analysts found a partial fingerprint. It matched Darryl Littlejohn. And on the floral bedsheet wrapped around her body, they found DNA. That, too, matched Littlejohn. The forensic evidence was overwhelming. The case that Imette St. Guillen spent her career studying would be solved in part by exactly the tools she had trained to understand.

But the story did not end with his arrest. Littlejohn was already on parole for a prior robbery conviction. He was not supposed to be working as a bouncer. The bar had employed him in violation of his parole conditions. In the aftermath of the murder, that fact prompted a city-wide reckoning about background checks for employees in licensed establishments, about the gaps between what the law requires and what bars actually practice. Imette's family pursued a civil case against the bar. The system, it turned out, had failed in multiple places long before that March night.

Darryl Littlejohn was convicted in two thousand and eight. He was also linked to the cold case abduction and assault of another young woman, and later convicted in that case as well. He is serving a sentence of life without the possibility of parole.

What remains with this case is not only the grief of it, but the irony that cannot be ignored. A young woman training to understand murder, to read evidence, to think about crime with scientific precision, became the subject of the very process she had devoted herself to learning. Her field solved her case. The DNA analysis, the fingerprint evidence, the patient work of forensic investigators — the tools of the discipline she loved — ultimately brought her killer to account.

That is not comfort. But it is something. It means the work she would have done had meaning, even when the worst possible thing took her away from it.
