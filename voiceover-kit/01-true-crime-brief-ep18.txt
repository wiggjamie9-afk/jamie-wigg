This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, one of the most audacious cold cases in American history — a man who hijacked a commercial airliner, extorted two hundred thousand dollars in ransom, and then leaped from the back of a Boeing seven-two-seven somewhere over the Pacific Northwest, parachuting into the wilderness and simply vanishing. The date was the twenty-fourth of November, nineteen seventy-one. The man used the name Dan Cooper. The press misread the alias and called him D.B. Cooper. That name has never left us.

The flight was Northwest Orient Airlines Flight Three-Oh-Five, traveling from Portland, Oregon to Seattle, Washington. Not long after takeoff, the man seated in seat eighteen-Charlie passed a note to a flight attendant. She assumed it was a phone number and tucked it in her pocket. He leaned over and told her she had better read it. The note said he had a bomb.

He was calm throughout. He wore a dark business suit and dark glasses. He ordered a bourbon and soda. He negotiated with ground personnel through the flight crew in a measured, unhurried manner. He wanted two hundred thousand dollars in negotiable twenty-dollar bills, four parachutes, and a fuel truck waiting in Seattle. When the plane landed, he released the passengers and most of the crew in exchange for the ransom and the parachutes. Then he directed the pilots to take off again, heading toward Mexico City at low speed and low altitude, with the landing gear down.

Somewhere over the Cascade Mountains, between Seattle and Reno, he lowered the rear air stairs of the seven-two-seven — a feature unique to that aircraft at the time — and he jumped. No one saw him go. The crew felt a bump and a change in cabin pressure. When the plane landed, he was gone, and two of the four parachutes were gone with him.

The investigation that followed was one of the largest in the FBI's history. Hundreds of suspects were investigated over the following decades. None were ever charged. The terrain into which he jumped — mountainous, heavily forested, frigid in late November — was considered by many investigators to be unsurvivable. Search parties found nothing. For years, the prevailing view was that D.B. Cooper had died in the jump, his body swallowed by the Cascade wilderness and never recovered.

Then, in February nineteen eighty, a young boy playing along the bank of the Columbia River near Vancouver, Washington found a rotting bundle of bills. The serial numbers matched a portion of the Cooper ransom money. It was real. Some of the money had made it out. But it had clearly been in the river for a long time, the bills partially decomposed and tangled in roots. The find confirmed that the money had traveled down the river — but it raised as many questions as it answered. Had Cooper landed in the water and drowned, the bills washing downriver over years? Had he buried the cash on the bank? Had someone else moved it?

The case has never been solved. The FBI formally suspended active investigation in two thousand and sixteen, forty-five years after the hijacking, citing a lack of viable leads. More than a thousand suspects had been investigated over the decades. The file was transferred to cold case status.

D.B. Cooper has become something close to a folk hero in American culture — a ghost in a dark suit, the man who beat the system, jumped into the storm, and was swallowed by the mountain dark. Bars in the Pacific Northwest host anniversaries. Novelists and filmmakers have returned to the story again and again. There are people who believe he survived, who believe he spent the ransom slowly and quietly, who believe he is buried in some small-town cemetery under a name nobody thought to connect to the most famous unsolved hijacking in American history.

We do not know. We may never know. He jumped and he was gone, and the wilderness kept its secret.
