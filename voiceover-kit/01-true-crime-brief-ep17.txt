Tonight, a poisoning case that puzzled doctors, baffled investigators, and ended the life of a man who had walked away from one of the most dangerous jobs in the world, only to be killed by a weapon so exotic it had never been used in an assassination before. It is November of two thousand and six. Alexander Litvinenko, a former officer of the Russian Federal Security Service — the FSB — is dying in a London hospital. The doctors attending him cannot explain what is destroying his body from the inside. His hair is falling out. His organs are failing. He is deteriorating at a rate that no conventional poison can account for.

Three weeks earlier, Litvinenko had been a healthy man. He had defected from Russia years before, had become a British citizen, and had spent the intervening years as a fierce public critic of the Kremlin and of Vladimir Putin personally. He had written books. He had made accusations. He had alleged, in public and in documented detail, that the FSB was involved in organized crime, in contract killings, in actions that the Russian government strenuously denied. He was not quiet. He was not careful. And on the first of November two thousand and six, he met with two men at a sushi restaurant and then at a hotel bar in London. Hours later, he became ill.

When Litvinenko died on the twenty-third of November, British scientists identified the cause. He had been poisoned with Polonium-two-ten — a radioactive isotope so rare and so expensive to produce that it can only be manufactured in a nuclear reactor. It is not something you obtain on the black market. It is not something a private individual makes in a laboratory. The quantity used to kill Litvinenko, a dose so small it was invisible to the naked eye, would have cost millions of dollars to produce and required state-level resources. This was not a crime of opportunity. It was a sophisticated state operation, carried out on the sovereign soil of a NATO ally.

The radioactive trail Litvinenko left across London told the story of the days before his death with horrifying precision. Polonium-two-ten registers on radiation detectors. Investigators were able to trace his movements — and the movements of his killers — through airports, hotel rooms, taxis, offices, the tea he drank, the chair he sat in. The two men he had met, Andrei Lugovoi and Dmitri Kovtun, were identified as suspects almost immediately. Both were former Russian intelligence officers. Both returned to Russia before British authorities could act. Russia refused, and continues to refuse, to extradite them.

A British public inquiry concluded in two thousand and sixteen, with high confidence, that Lugovoi and Kovtun had carried out the poisoning. And it concluded, with the same high confidence, that the operation had been approved by Vladimir Putin himself, and by Nikolai Patrushev, then director of the FSB.

Russia has never acknowledged any of this. Lugovoi, far from facing justice, was elected to the Russian parliament, where he enjoyed immunity from prosecution. Putin denied all involvement and dismissed the inquiry's findings as politically motivated.

The case remains one of the most extraordinary uses of a weapons-grade substance in peacetime, committed in one of the most surveilled cities on earth, and it was carried out with a kind of brazen confidence that suggested the perpetrators never expected to face consequences. And they were right.

What Alexander Litvinenko's death demonstrated was not the power of one state to silence a critic, though it demonstrated that too. What it demonstrated was the new shape of political violence in the twenty-first century — invisible, deniable, exotic enough to confound any system built to prevent it, and deployed with the certainty that the gap between what the world knows and what the world can prove is wide enough to walk a murderer through.
