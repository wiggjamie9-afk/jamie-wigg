# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 2
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Welcome back to Dad Code. Glad you found twelve minutes. If you're listening to this in the car park outside work, sitting in silence before you go in — I see you. Today's one for you.

We're talking about the impossible split. Being a present dad while also being the guy who pays for the roof the kids sleep under. Work and presence. The two things that never seem to fit in the same day.

Here's a number that wrecked me when I first heard a version of it. The average dad gets something like a few hundred Saturdays with his kid before they're grown and want nothing to do with hanging out with you. A few hundred. That's it. You can count them. You could fit them on a single page.

And most of us spend a big chunk of those few hundred Saturdays half-present. Body on the floor playing Lego, brain in a Slack thread. Eyes on the kid, mind on the email you didn't send. We're there but we're not there.

Now look — I'm not going to stand here and tell you to just put the phone down. That advice is useless and a bit insulting, honestly, because the bills are real. The job is real. The pressure to provide is wired into a lot of us deep, often handed down from our own dads who showed love by working themselves into the ground.

So let's be smarter than phone bad. Let's talk about presence as a skill, not a personality trait.

The truth is you cannot give your kids all of your time. That's a fantasy and chasing it just makes you feel like a failure at both jobs. What you can give them is undivided time. Quality over quantity isn't a cop-out — for working dads it's the entire game. Twenty fully-present minutes beats three hours of you scrolling next to them. They can tell the difference. Kids always can.

Here's where it clicked for me. I was doing the bedtime story thing, right, going through the motions, reading the book in the voice — but secretly checking my phone behind the book like an absolute amateur. Thought I was being slick.

And my daughter, four years old, stops me. Puts her little hand on my phone and pushes it down. Doesn't even say anything dramatic. Just goes, Daddy, read with your eyes.

Read with your eyes.

I have never been so cleanly destroyed by a four-year-old. Because she was right. I'd been reading with my mouth and living somewhere else. And I realized she'd clocked it. She knew. Kids always know when you've half-checked out.

That phrase is now law in our house. Read with your eyes. It means: be here. All of you. For this bit.

And listen, presence does not mean you have to be a magical Pinterest dad doing sensory crafts with kinetic sand. God, no. Presence can be deeply stupid and that's allowed. Some of the best parenting I've done has been the floor is lava for forty-five minutes, or being a horse until my knees filed a formal complaint. You don't have to be enriching. You have to be available. There's a difference and it takes the pressure right off.

Right, the actionable bit. Three moves.

One: protect one non-negotiable window. For me it's bedtime. Whatever happens at work, I'm there for the last forty minutes of their day. The phone goes in a drawer in another room — not on silent, not face-down, in a drawer. Because face-down still buzzes in your soul. Pick your window and defend it like your job depends on it. Because the bigger job kind of does.

Two: tell work where the line is. You don't have to be loud about it. I'm offline from six to seven-thirty, back after. Most reasonable workplaces respect a clear line. The problem is most of us never draw one and then resent everyone for crossing a line they couldn't see.

Three: when you're with them, narrate less and ask more. Don't perform Fun Dad. Just get curious. What was the best bit of today? Who'd you sit with at lunch? Presence is mostly just attention, and attention is mostly just good questions.

Here's what I want you to sit with. Your kids will not remember whether you answered every email. They will remember whether you looked up. They're not keeping score on your career. They're keeping score on your eyes — whether they ever met theirs.

You can be ambitious and present. But only if you decide, on purpose, where the ambition stops for the day. Draw the line. Read with your eyes.

Next week we go somewhere harder. Discipline. How to set a boundary with your kid without turning into the version of your own dad you swore you'd never become. I've been dreading and looking forward to that one in equal measure. See you then.

This has been Dad Code. Now go put your phone in a drawer. A real one.
