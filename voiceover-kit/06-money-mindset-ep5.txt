Welcome to Money Mindset. I'm Everet. Take a breath with me before we start. Twelve minutes, one idea, one step, zero judgment. Here we go.

Today we're going to talk about something almost nobody warns you about — the reason so many people earn more and more over the years, and somehow never feel any richer. It has a name. It's called lifestyle inflation, and once you can see it, you can stop it.

Here's the idea. Lifestyle inflation is the quiet habit of spending more every single time you earn more. You get a raise, and within a month or two, your spending has risen to match it. The bigger paycheck arrives, and so does the nicer apartment, the upgraded phone plan, the food delivery a few more nights a week. Nothing dramatic. Just a gentle, steady creep. And the cruel part is this: because each upgrade feels small and earned, you barely notice it. But added together, they explain why a person earning twice what they made five years ago can still feel like they're living paycheck to paycheck.

I want to be really clear about something, because this episode is not about guilt. Enjoying the fruits of your work is good. You're allowed to upgrade your life. The problem isn't spending more — it's spending all of it, automatically, without ever choosing. Lifestyle inflation isn't a moral failure. It's just what happens by default when nobody's steering. So today, we're going to grab the wheel.

Let me show you how powerful this is with a simple comparison. Imagine two people who both get a four-hundred-dollar-a-month raise. The first person lets their lifestyle inflate to absorb all of it — nicer everything, and a month later the raise has vanished into the background of their life. The second person decides, on purpose, to enjoy half and save half. They get two hundred dollars a month of new comfort — which still feels great — and they send two hundred a month toward their future. Same raise. Same job. One of them is quietly building wealth, and the other is running faster just to stay in the same place. The only difference was a single decision.

So here's the tool, and it's beautifully simple. It's called the fifty percent rule for raises. Whenever your income goes up — a raise, a new job, a side gig, even a tax refund — you commit, in advance, to keeping half of the new money for your future, and you let yourself genuinely enjoy the other half. Not all of it saved, which feels like punishment and never lasts. Not all of it spent, which is the trap. Half and half. You get to feel the reward and build the future at the same time.

Why does keeping only half work so well? Because the magic is in catching the money before it ever becomes a habit. Once you've lived with a bigger paycheck for a few months, giving any of it up feels like a loss, and we hate losses. But if you redirect half the moment it arrives — before your lifestyle ever adjusts to it — you never miss it. You can't miss a comfort you never got used to. It's the same trick as paying yourself first, just aimed at the new money specifically.

There's one more place lifestyle inflation hides, and it's worth a quick mention — the small recurring upgrades. The subscription you bumped to the premium tier. The plan with more data you don't use. These don't feel like lifestyle inflation because they happened once, quietly, and now they just renew forever. Once a year, it's worth scanning your recurring charges and asking of each one — is this still buying me something I actually feel? If not, it's just inflation on autopilot, and you're allowed to cancel it.

So here is your one step for today. Think about the next time your income might go up — a raise you're hoping for, a bonus, a side project, anything. And decide right now, before it arrives, what your split will be. Maybe it's fifty-fifty. Maybe, if money's tight, it's enjoy seventy and save thirty. The exact number matters less than deciding in advance, while it's easy, instead of in the moment, when it's hard. Write the number down somewhere you'll find it.

Let's recap. Lifestyle inflation is the quiet creep of spending rising to match every raise, and it's why earning more doesn't always feel like more. It's not a failure — it's just the default when nobody chooses. The fix is to decide your split before new money arrives, so you can enjoy part of it and build with the rest. And you'll never miss the half you redirect, because you redirect it before you ever get used to it.

Your one step today: decide your save-versus-enjoy split for the next time your income rises.
