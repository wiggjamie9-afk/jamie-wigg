# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 13
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. After last week, which was a tender one, all those big questions, today we stay warm but we go personal. Today we're talking about dads and daughters. About being a father to a girl, when you yourself grew up as a boy, in a boy's world, with absolutely no map for this.

Let me be honest right at the top. When I found out my first was going to be a girl, under all the joy, there was a little flicker of fear. A boy I thought I understood. Rough and tumble, kick a ball, I know that script. But a daughter? I genuinely thought, I don't know how to do this. I don't know what she'll need from me. And I'm here to tell you that fear was almost entirely nonsense, and where it wasn't nonsense, it was pointing at the wrong thing.

Here's the first thing that cracked it open for me. I thought my job was to figure out girl stuff. The hair, the dolls, the colors, whatever box my own dad's generation had put it in. And I had it completely backwards. My daughter doesn't need me to become an expert in her interests. She needs me to be genuinely, unfakeably interested in her. There's a difference, and it's everything. I cannot tell you the names of all the characters in her shows. But I can be the dad who sits down and goes, okay, tell me everything, who's this one, why's she the best. The currency isn't knowledge. It's attention.

Now let me say the thing that actually matters most, and it's bigger than tea parties. For a huge number of girls, their dad is the very first model they ever get of how a man should treat them. Let that land for a second. The way you speak to her. The way you speak to her mum. Whether you listen or talk over. Whether your love comes with conditions or just shows up. You are, like it or not, setting the baseline. Years from now, some part of her will measure how people treat her against the standard you set in the kitchen when she was six. That's a heavy thing to carry, but it's also a gift, because it means you've got real power to do good here.

So let me get practical. A few moves.

One: take her seriously. Not in a stiff way, in a real way. When she tells you something matters to her, however small it seems to your adult brain, treat it like it matters. Because the message underneath is, your inner world is worth my full attention. A girl who grows up being genuinely listened to by her dad is a girl who, later on, will not settle for being talked over. You're not just hearing her. You're calibrating what she'll expect from everyone after you.

Two: be careful what you praise, and praise wide. It's so easy to default to you look lovely, don't you look pretty. And there's nothing wrong with that on its own. But if pretty is the loudest thing she ever hears from Dad, she learns that's her main value. So go out of your way. You're so brave. You're so funny. That was such a clever idea. You worked really hard at that. Build her a whole picture of herself, where being smart and tough and kind and curious matter at least as much as how she looks. The wider you praise, the freer she gets to be.

Three: stay close, especially when it gets awkward. There's this thing that happens where, as daughters get older, some dads quietly back away. It gets less obvious how to connect, the easy little-kid stuff fades, and we get shy or uncertain and we drift. Don't drift. She needs you there just as much at thirteen as at three, probably more, even if she's pretending she doesn't. The form changes. You're not building Lego anymore, you're giving lifts and sitting in comfortable silence and being available. But the job is the same. Stay in the room. Keep showing up. Don't let the awkwardness win.

Here's what I want you to sit with. The fear I started with, that I wouldn't know how to be a dad to a girl, was based on a lie. The lie that fatherhood is about activities. It isn't. It's about presence, respect, and showing her, every ordinary day, that a man can be safe and warm and completely in her corner. You already know how to do that. It's the same code. It always was.

Next week we roll up our sleeves. Chores. Responsibility. The mighty, eternal, soul-testing battle of getting a small human to put one single sock in the basket. See you then.

This has been Dad Code. Now go ask your daughter to tell you everything, and actually mean it.
