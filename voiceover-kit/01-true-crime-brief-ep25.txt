Tonight, a case that looked, from the outside, like a wealthy family destroyed by grief. A prominent attorney, a respected name in a small South Carolina community, two deaths ruled inconclusive, and then a financial investigation that pulled the thread that unraveled everything. The name is Alex Murdaugh. The story is about the remarkable distance between a reputation and the truth behind it.

Alex Murdaugh came from legal royalty in the Low Country of South Carolina. His great-grandfather, his grandfather, and his father had each served as the solicitor for the Fourteenth Judicial Circuit, the top prosecutor in a region of rural counties south of Charleston. The Murdaugh name was synonymous with power, with legal authority, with a certain untouchable status. Alex became a personal injury attorney, making millions in settlements. He had a large house, a hunting property called Moselle, a wife named Maggie, and two sons, Buster and Paul.

On the night of the seventh of June, two thousand and twenty-one, Maggie Murdaugh and her twenty-two-year-old son Paul were shot and killed at the Moselle property. Alex Murdaugh called nine-one-one that night, reporting he had found their bodies when he came home. He said he had been visiting his ailing mother and was away when it happened. Investigators arrived at a scene that involved a rifle and a shotgun, two weapons, suggesting deliberate planning.

The investigation was complicated from the start by the very influence the Murdaugh name carried in that region. The local sheriff's office and state authorities circled the deaths without moving quickly toward any arrest. In the months that followed, a parallel story began to emerge.

Alex Murdaugh, it turned out, was not what he appeared to be. He had been stealing from clients for years — grieving families who came to him for settlement money, estate beneficiaries, vulnerable people who trusted him because of what his name represented. The total amount stolen was eventually calculated at more than eight point four million dollars. His law firm forced him out. His financial crimes were reported to the South Carolina Law Enforcement Division.

In September two thousand and twenty-one, in what investigators came to believe was an attempt to generate life insurance money so his surviving son could pay back some of what he had taken, Alex Murdaugh staged his own shooting on the side of a road, asking a man named Curtis Eddie Smith to shoot him in the head. Smith fired, but the wound was not fatal. Murdaugh survived. He later admitted to orchestrating it.

Investigators continued building the murder case while the financial crimes case expanded in parallel. The critical break came from cell phone data. Alex Murdaugh had told investigators he had not been at the kennel area of the Moselle property the night of the killings. But a video taken on Paul Murdaugh's phone — a Snapchat video sent minutes before the time of death — captured voices in the background. One of them was identified by multiple family members and acquaintances as Alex Murdaugh's voice.

He was arrested for the murders of Maggie and Paul Murdaugh in July two thousand and twenty-two. His trial began in January two thousand and twenty-three and became one of the most-watched criminal proceedings in the country. The prosecution walked the jury through the financial ruin, the motive, the physical evidence, and the critical inconsistency between his alibi and the phone video.

On the second of March, two thousand and twenty-three, Alex Murdaugh was convicted of two counts of murder. He was sentenced to life in prison without the possibility of parole. He was also subsequently convicted on dozens of counts of financial fraud and sentenced to an additional twenty-seven years.

The Murdaugh case is, at its core, a story about institutional protection. A family name that shielded misconduct for years, in a community where that name meant something, until the weight of what was hidden became too great to hold. The two people who died at Moselle were his wife and his son. And the man who stood to gain from their silence had been standing in plain sight all along, protected by a reputation built on other people's trust.
