Hello and welcome to the AI Briefing. Today we're following the money, because behind every dazzling AI demo sits a question that decides what actually gets built: who pays for this, and does it make sense? They call AI the future, but the future has a price tag, and it is an enormous one. So let's do the careful thing and explain, in plain language, the economics of AI. Why it costs so much, where the money comes from, and why some of the smartest people in the field quietly wonder whether the numbers add up.

Start with the single fact that shapes everything else. Building a frontier AI model is staggeringly expensive. Training one of the largest systems can cost tens or even hundreds of millions of dollars, and that's just one training run, before a single customer has used it. Why so much? We saw the reasons in earlier briefings. It takes vast quantities of those specialized chips, running for weeks, consuming electricity on the scale of a small town. The cost isn't a rounding error or a startup quirk. It is the central economic fact of the whole industry, and almost everything else follows from it.

That cost has a strange consequence. It means that at the very frontier, only a handful of players can even compete. Training the biggest models requires capital that very few organizations on earth possess. So a field that began with the open, scrappy spirit of research has, at its frontier, started to look like a contest among giants, because the price of entry is simply too high for anyone else. This is one reason that argument we covered, open versus closed AI, matters so much. The economics push toward concentration.

Now here's the part that surprises people, and it's the heart of today's briefing. Training the model is only half the cost. The other half is something called inference. Inference is just the technical word for using the model, the cost of every single answer it gives. And this matters enormously, because of scale. A model is trained once. But it might then answer billions of questions a day, for years. Every one of those answers takes real computation, real chips, real electricity. So even if you set aside the giant training bill, the act of simply running the service, day after day, costs a fortune. Unlike ordinary software, where serving one more user is nearly free, every AI answer has a real, physical cost attached to it.

That single difference quietly breaks the usual technology business model. For decades, software was a beautiful business precisely because it cost almost nothing to serve an extra customer. Build it once, sell it a million times, and the millionth copy is free. AI does not work like that. Every extra user, every extra question, burns a little more compute. The product gets more expensive to run the more popular it becomes. That is a genuinely unusual and uncomfortable shape for a business, and it's why so many AI products are offered cheap or free today while the companies behind them lose money on every use, betting that costs will fall fast enough to catch up later.

So where does the money come from to sustain all this? Mostly, for now, from investment. Enormous sums are being poured in by investors and by a few very large technology companies, on a bet, the bet that AI will become so useful, so woven into work and life, that the eventual returns will dwarf today's spending. That bet might well be right. But it's worth being clear-eyed: a great deal of today's AI runs not on profit, but on the expectation of future profit. The industry is, in large part, spending tomorrow's money today.

Now the honest part of the briefing, the open question nobody can answer yet. Will the value created exceed the staggering cost of creating it? On one side, optimists point out that the cost of computation has fallen relentlessly for decades, and that AI is following the same curve, getting cheaper to run at a remarkable pace. What costs a fortune today may be cheap in a few years, and then the economics flip from painful to wonderful. On the other side, skeptics ask a sharper question: are people and businesses actually willing to pay enough, for enough real value, to cover these bills, not someday, but before the patience and the money run out? Both can point to real evidence. Neither can prove it yet.

And that tension is the truest summary of where AI economics stands right now. Underneath the headlines about breakthroughs and miracles sits a simpler, harder business question: does this pay for itself? The technology is astonishing. Whether it is sustainable, as a business, at this cost, is genuinely not yet settled. The breakthroughs are real. So are the bills.

So, the briefing on the economics of AI. Building these models costs hundreds of millions, which concentrates the frontier in a few hands. But the deeper twist is inference, the cost of every answer, which means AI, unlike normal software, gets more expensive the more it's used. Today the industry runs largely on investment and the expectation of future profit. Whether the value will finally outrun the cost depends on a race between falling compute prices and real, paying demand, and that race is still being run.

As always, the goal of this show isn't to sell you the revolution or the panic. It's to help you understand what's really happening, so you can think clearly about it for yourself.

That's the briefing. I'm your host, and I'll see you next time.
