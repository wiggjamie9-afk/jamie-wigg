Welcome back to Focus Lab.

Let's talk about distraction — not just the obvious kind, but the internal kind. The one that comes from inside your own head.

Most productivity advice focuses on external distractions. Silence your phone. Use a website blocker. Go somewhere quiet. And yes, all of that helps. But there's a second category of distraction that doesn't get nearly enough attention, and that's internal distraction. The random thought that pulls you sideways. The sudden urge to check something. The creeping anxiety about an unrelated problem. The mental itch that demands to be scratched right now.

Internal distractions are not a sign of weakness or poor character. They are your brain doing what brains do — monitoring the environment, flagging unresolved loops, surfacing old concerns. The brain is wired for survival, and part of survival is never fully letting your guard down. The problem is that in a knowledge work context, this is actively working against you.

Here is a technique that tackles this head-on. It's called the capture list. Keep a small notepad or a single notes app open during your work sessions. The moment an intrusive thought arrives — a task you just remembered, something you need to check, an idea you don't want to lose — you write it down immediately and return to your work. That's it. Don't act on it. Don't follow the thread. Write it, close the loop in your brain, and get back to what you were doing.

This works because most internal distractions are just your brain's way of saying: "Don't forget this." Once it's written down, your brain can let it go. The loop is closed. The thought is held somewhere safe. And you can return to focus without the fear of losing the thing.

The second internal distraction pattern to watch is boredom or discomfort. When a task gets hard, the brain will try to escape. You'll suddenly feel like you need water, or you'll want to stretch, or you'll find yourself reading an article with no clear memory of how you got there. This is avoidance. The antidote isn't willpower. It's leaning into the discomfort for just a few more minutes. Sit with the difficulty. Often, breakthroughs happen just on the other side of the urge to quit.

External silence is easy to create. Internal silence is a practice. Start with the capture list. You'll be surprised how much space it frees up.
