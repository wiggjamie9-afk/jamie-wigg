Welcome to Sleep and Calm. Take a breath. Let your shoulders drop.

Tonight we are talking about the circadian rhythm — your body's internal clock — and what to do when that clock has drifted out of sync.

The circadian rhythm is a roughly twenty-four hour biological cycle that governs almost everything in your body. Sleep and wakefulness, of course, but also body temperature, hormone release, digestion, immune function, and mood. It is not a single clock but a whole system of clocks — organs, tissues, and cells all running on their own timing — coordinated by a master pacemaker nestled deep in the brain called the suprachiasmatic nucleus.

This master clock is set, primarily, by light. Specifically, by blue-spectrum light entering your eyes in the morning. When dawn arrives, your body uses that light signal to calibrate the entire system for the day ahead, beginning a cascade of biological events timed to the hours that follow.

When your rhythm drifts — through late nights, irregular schedules, shift work, travel, or simply the accumulated weight of a disorganised week — you may feel it as a kind of formless fatigue. Not quite tired enough to sleep easily, not quite alert enough to feel well. Your mood flattens. Your thinking slows. Food cravings increase. The body is operating out of rhythm, and it shows.

Resetting your circadian rhythm is possible, and the most powerful lever is consistent light exposure.

Start with morning light. Within thirty minutes of waking, get outside or sit near a bright window. Even on a cloudy day, outdoor light is many times brighter than indoor lighting. Ten to twenty minutes of morning light tells your master clock clearly and firmly what time it is. Do this consistently for a few days and you will begin to feel your rhythm consolidate.

Pair morning light with a consistent wake time. More than any other single habit, waking at the same time each day — yes, even on weekends — is the most reliable way to stabilise your sleep. Your body learns to expect wakefulness at that hour and prepares for it. It learns to expect sleep several hours later and prepares for that too.

In the evening, reduce light. Dim your home, lower the warmth of your lighting, and minimise screen time in the last hour before bed. This allows melatonin to rise naturally and your body to begin its transition toward sleep.

Eat at consistent times. Your digestive clock is one of the secondary clocks in your circadian system, and regular mealtimes help synchronise the whole network.

Resetting a drifted rhythm takes a few days of consistency. But the body responds quickly when you give it clear, repetitive signals. Morning light, consistent timing, evening darkness. These three things alone can restore a great deal.

You have an extraordinary internal clock. Help it find its time.

Sleep well.
