Sports Unfiltered. Today we go back to where it starts. Not the draft, not the professional combine, not the first contract. Way back. The courts, the pitches, the fields, the gymnasiums where kids are still figuring out what they are capable of and what this game means to them. Youth sport. The place where the entire pipeline begins, and the place where we are doing the most damage with the least awareness.

Because youth sport in two thousand and twenty-six is not what it was. It has been industrialised. Professionalised. Restructured around the logic of elite development from ages that used to belong entirely to childhood. And the consequences — for athlete development, for physical and emotional health, for the long-term relationship young people have with their own bodies and with competitive effort — are not what the parents writing the cheques for private coaching and travel teams and exposure tournaments think they are buying.

Let me start with the early specialisation question, because the research here is genuinely damning and genuinely ignored. The conventional wisdom in the travel and club sport ecosystem is that the earlier you specialise, the further you get. Focus on one sport, train intensively, and you build the runway you need to compete at the highest level. The sports scientists who have spent careers studying this say something completely different. Early specialisation increases overuse injury rates, increases burnout, and for the overwhelming majority of sports does not produce better professionals. The athletes who make it to elite levels tend to have played multiple sports as children, developed general motor competence, and specialised later. The athletes who specialise at eight years old are more likely to quit at fourteen than to make a roster at twenty.

But here is why the conventional wisdom persists anyway. The travel and club sport industry is a multi-billion dollar business. It is selling parents hope. The pitch is not hard to make to someone who loves their child: invest in their development early, give them every advantage, do not let them fall behind. The industry has commercialised parental anxiety and dressed it up as athlete development. And the kids are the ones absorbing the physical and emotional cost.

Now here is the cultural piece that does not get talked about enough. Youth sport is one of the primary places where children first encounter pressure, failure, team dynamics, resilience, and the relationship between effort and outcome. These are foundational life experiences. When the environment around youth sport becomes dominated by adult ambition — parental investment in outcomes, coaching cultures that treat children as pre-professionals, organisations optimised for rankings and exposure rather than development and enjoyment — the developmental value does not just diminish. It inverts. You can teach a child to hate competition. You can teach a ten-year-old that their value is conditional on their performance. And those lessons land, and they last.

The coaches and programs that actually produce the best athletes and the most psychologically healthy people are the ones doing something that looks almost boring from the outside. They are teaching fundamentals. They are keeping it fun. They are creating environments where kids want to come back the next day. They are treating development as a long arc measured in years, not a tournament bracket measured in weekends.

Sport at its best teaches children something about who they are that they cannot get anywhere else. That is worth protecting. And right now we are not protecting it nearly enough.
