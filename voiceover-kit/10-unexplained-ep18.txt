In the summer of nineteen forty-seven, a rancher named Mac Brazel found unusual debris scattered across his sheep pasture seventy-five miles north of Roswell, New Mexico. He described the material as unlike anything he had seen. Lightweight metal that sprang back to its original shape when crumpled. A foil-like substance that would not burn. Beams marked with symbols that resembled no alphabet. He reported it to the local sheriff, who contacted Roswell Army Air Field. What happened next became the most discussed and debated UFO incident in history.

The Army Air Force issued a press release stating that personnel had recovered a flying disc. The following day they retracted it and said it was a weather balloon. The international press had already run the flying disc story. The damage, or the cover-up, depending on your perspective, had already begun.

In the decades that followed, witnesses emerged. A mortician claimed to have received a call from the base requesting small child-sized coffins. A nurse named Glenn Dennis said she was escorted away from the base hospital and warned never to discuss what she had seen. Retired military officers said under oath that they had handled non-human material. Major Jesse Marcel, the intelligence officer who originally retrieved the debris, said to his dying day that the material was not of this Earth.

The official government response evolved over time. First a weather balloon. Then in nineteen ninety-four the Air Force released a report attributing the debris to Project Mogul, a classified program using high-altitude balloons to monitor Soviet nuclear tests. The balloons were large, used unusual radar reflectors, and their recovery would have been classified at the time. In nineteen ninety-seven a second report explained reports of recovered bodies as misremembered memories of crash test dummies used in high-altitude parachute experiments that occurred in the early nineteen fifties.

The critics of these explanations point out inconsistencies. The crash test dummies were dropped in programs that began in nineteen fifty-four, seven years after the Roswell incident. Memory cannot misplace an event by seven years, let alone conflate it with an unrelated one. The Project Mogul explanation accounts for the debris but not the witness testimony about unusual material properties, not the claimed coffin order, not the nurse's reported removal from the hospital.

What is certain is this. Something crashed near Roswell in July of nineteen forty-seven. The Army Air Force initially said it was a flying disc. They changed that statement the next day. The original documentation from the first press release was later found to have disappeared from archives. Multiple people who worked at the base subsequently made statements, some under oath, describing what they called non-human materials or non-human remains.

Project Mogul is a credible explanation for the debris. But Project Mogul does not cleanly account for every thread of the Roswell story, and the threads that remain unexplained are the ones that matter most to those who believe the official account was never the real one.

Roswell persists not because people are credulous but because the government's own response was inconsistent. A press release that said flying disc followed immediately by a correction has a particular shape. That shape is not the shape of an honest mistake about a weather balloon.
