# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 5
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. Today we're getting into something that makes a lot of dads sweat, and I think it's because almost none of us were taught it properly ourselves. Today we're talking about money. About how you raise a kid who has a healthy, calm relationship with money, when you might still be untangling your own.

Let me start with a confession. For years, money in my house growing up was a thing of whispers and slammed doors. It was tension you could feel in the air at the dinner table, but nobody ever explained. So I grew up thinking money was this scary, shameful adult secret. And I really do not want to hand that down. The silence is the thing that does the damage. The silence teaches a kid that money is dangerous and you must never look at it directly.

So the first move, before any clever system, is just this. Stop hiding it. Not the stressful, lie-awake-at-night stuff, you don't dump that on a six-year-old. But the everyday stuff. We have a certain amount. We chose this, so we said no to that. I'm saving up for the thing, that's why we're not buying it today. When you narrate the small choices out loud, money stops being a monster in the cupboard and starts being a normal tool. That's all money is. A tool. Kids handle tools just fine when you show them how it works.

Now here's the idea that genuinely changed how I think about teaching it. Kids do not learn money from a lecture. They learn it from holding it and losing it. A lecture about saving goes in one ear and straight out the other. But let a kid spend their own real money on a cheap plastic toy that breaks before bedtime, and they have just received a lesson that no speech could ever deliver. Let the small lessons happen now, while the stakes are tiny. Far better they learn the sting of a bad purchase on a few dollars at age seven than on a credit card at twenty seven.

So here's the simple system we use, and you can steal it. When our kid gets any money, pocket money, a birthday note from grandma, whatever, it gets split into three. Some to spend now. Some to save for something bigger they actually want. And some to give away, to someone or something they care about. Spend, save, give. Three jars, literally, where they can see the coins stacking up. The seeing matters. Abstract numbers mean nothing to a child. A jar filling up is a story they can watch.

And let me say a word about that third jar, the give one, because it's the one most of us skip. I think it might be the most important. Because it quietly teaches a kid that money isn't only about getting. It's also a thing you can use to be kind, to be part of something bigger than yourself. A kid who learns young that giving feels good is a kid who's going to have a much healthier relationship with money than one who only ever learns to grab.

One more thing, and this is the hard one. They are absorbing your money behavior whether you talk about it or not. If you stress-spend when you're sad, they clock it. If you can never say no to yourself, they learn there's no such thing as enough. If you fight about money behind a thin wall, they hear the tone even when they can't hear the words. We teach money mostly by accident. So it's worth getting a little more deliberate about the example.

Here's what I want to leave you with. You don't have to be rich to raise a kid who's good with money. You don't even have to be good with money yourself, not yet. You just have to be willing to stop whispering. Pull it into the daylight. Let them hold a little, lose a little, give a little. Make money a normal, ordinary, manageable thing. Because the goal was never to make them wealthy. The goal is to make them free. Free from the fear that ran through so many of our own houses.

Next week we're talking about resilience. About how you raise a kid who can fall down, and get back up, when every instinct in your body is screaming at you to catch them before they ever hit the ground.

This has been Dad Code. Now go and find three empty jars. You'll know what they're for.
