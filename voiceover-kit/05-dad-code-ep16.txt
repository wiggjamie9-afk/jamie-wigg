The Screen Time Wars. If you have kids, you know exactly what I am talking about. That daily battle where you say fifteen more minutes and somehow forty-five minutes later they are still glued to whatever glowing rectangle they chose that afternoon.

I used to think I had this figured out. I had timers. I had rules. I had a whole spreadsheet — yes, a spreadsheet — tracking how many minutes each kid had used per day. My wife looked at that spreadsheet and said, honey, you need a hobby. And I said, this IS my hobby. She did not find that funny.

Here is what I have actually learned after years of losing the screen time wars and eventually finding something that works most days.

The first thing is that fighting the screen itself is the wrong fight. Screens are not going anywhere. The internet is not going anywhere. What I actually want to teach my kids is not how to avoid screens but how to use them well. How to put them down when something more important is happening. That is a life skill. That is the goal.

So instead of fifteen more minutes I started saying things like dinner is in ten minutes, what is a good stopping point for what you are doing? That small shift — giving them some ownership of the transition — cut our meltdowns in half. Maybe more. Kids hate being yanked out of their world mid-moment. I hate that too. If someone interrupted me mid-podcast I would be pretty annoyed.

The second thing that helped was making sure there was something worth coming TO rather than just something being taken away. If a kid puts down a game and the alternative is homework and silence, well, that game is going to look pretty good. But if dinner smells great and someone has a funny story and there is a bit of chaos and warmth in the kitchen, suddenly the transition is easier.

Third — and this one surprised me — I got more flexible about WHAT they were watching or playing and more firm about WHEN. My old approach was the opposite. I was policing content I barely understood while being loose about time. That was backwards. Know what your kids are doing, know who they are playing with online, but once you trust the content, let the timer be the boundary, not a running commentary on every choice they make.

The hardest part of all this? Modelling it myself. My kids noticed — and they will call you out immediately — that I check my phone constantly. Dad, you said screens off at dinner. You are on your phone. They are right. Fair point. Completely valid. So now my phone goes face down at the table too.

We are all learning together. That is kind of the whole point.
