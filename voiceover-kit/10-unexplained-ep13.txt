Hello, this is Austin, and you're listening to Unexplained.

Tonight our mystery is not hidden in a distant ocean or a faded manuscript. It has been seen by sailors and farmers, by airline pilots and frightened families in their own kitchens, for hundreds of years. It appears during thunderstorms, a glowing sphere of light, often about the size of a grapefruit, that drifts through the air, sometimes through windows and down chimneys, sometimes along the aisle of an aircraft, before vanishing, sometimes silently, sometimes with a sharp bang. For centuries, serious scientists doubted it existed at all. This is ball lightning.

The accounts are old and remarkably consistent. A storm is raging. A bright ball of light, white or orange or blue, appears near the ground or floats down from the clouds. It moves slowly, almost deliberately, seeming to follow walls, drift through doorways, hover near metal objects. It lasts anywhere from a second or two to perhaps half a minute, far longer than an ordinary lightning flash. And then it disappears. Sometimes it simply fades. Sometimes it bursts with a loud report and the smell of sulphur or burning. People who have seen it describe it the same way across centuries and continents, which is one of the reasons it has been so hard to dismiss.

And yet, for a very long time, dismissed it was. The problem was that ball lightning was almost never captured by instruments. It struck without warning, it lasted only moments, and it appeared in the chaos of a storm, the worst possible conditions for careful measurement. So science was left with thousands of eyewitness reports and almost no hard data, and eyewitness reports, however sincere, are not proof. Many respected physicists suspected the whole thing was an illusion. So let us do what we do here. Let us set the legend aside and weigh the explanations by the evidence, not by which one is the most dramatic.

The first question is the most basic. Is it real at all? And here the ground has genuinely shifted. For most of history, the case rested on testimony. But in two thousand and twelve, scientists in China happened to have spectrographs and high-speed cameras pointed at a thunderstorm to study ordinary lightning, and by sheer chance they recorded a ball of light forming just after a lightning strike. They captured its glow, its movement, and crucially the light it gave off, broken down into its constituent colours. That recording, along with laboratory experiments that have produced small, short-lived glowing balls of plasma, has moved ball lightning a long way toward being accepted as a real physical phenomenon, not merely a trick of frightened minds. So the modern view leans toward this. Something real is happening. The harder question is what.

That brings us to the strongest current explanations, the grounded science of how a ball of light could exist and persist. The leading idea grew directly out of that two thousand and twelve recording. When lightning strikes the ground, it can vaporise minerals in the soil, silicon in particular. The theory is that this creates a small cloud of glowing, burning silicon particles, a ball of vaporised earth that floats in the air and slowly oxidises, giving off light as it burns. The colours captured in China were consistent with elements found in soil, which is striking support for this idea. Other scientists have proposed different mechanisms, microwave energy trapped inside a thin shell, or pockets of charged gas, or electrical effects in the air. These are competing explanations, but they share a hopeful quality. They are testable. They belong to physics, not to the supernatural.

Then comes the next tier, the natural but unusual cases that may not be true ball lightning at all. Some sightings are almost certainly something else, an ordinary lightning flash leaving a glowing afterimage burned onto the eye, or electrical discharges from power lines and equipment, or a phenomenon called Saint Elmo's fire, a faint glow that forms on tall pointed objects in storms. The difficulty is that all of these can be reported, honestly, as a glowing ball, which muddies the data. Part of the mystery of ball lightning is simply separating the real thing from its many convincing imitators.

Then there is a third tier, the further-out ideas, the ones the evidence supports least. That ball lightning is something exotic, a window into unknown physics, or connected to unexplained sightings in the sky, or even something not of this world. These ideas tend to flourish in the gap left by the missing data. When a thing is real, repeatedly seen, and not yet fully explained, the imagination is quick to fill the space. But the trend of the evidence is running the other way, away from the exotic and toward a difficult but ordinary corner of physics we are only now beginning to map.

So where does that leave us? Weigh it honestly, and the picture is one of a genuine mystery slowly yielding. Ball lightning is very probably real. It has, at last, been caught on instruments. And the most likely explanation is not a doorway to another world but burning vaporised earth, or trapped electrical energy, a brief, glowing object born in the violence of a storm. We do not yet have it pinned down. There may be more than one cause hiding under the single name. But this is a mystery that science is winning, slowly, one chance recording at a time.

And perhaps that is what makes ball lightning quietly remarkable. It is a reminder of how recently we were almost completely blind. For centuries, ordinary people swore they had seen a glowing sphere drift through their homes during storms, and for centuries the learned world told them, gently or otherwise, that they must be mistaken. They were not mistaken. The phenomenon was real all along. It was simply too fast, too rare, and too wild to be caught.

We are drawn to it because it sits at the edge of the believable, a thing so strange that science itself long refused to accept it, yet so persistently reported that it would not go away. It teaches a humbling lesson. Sometimes the mystery is not that people are imagining things. Sometimes the mystery is that the world contains something genuinely odd, hovering in the storm, glowing for a few seconds in a darkened room, waiting patiently for us to be ready to believe it.

This has been Unexplained. Sleep well, if you can.
