Welcome back to Plain Tech. Really glad you are here.

Today we are talking about video calls. Nearly everyone uses them now — for work, for keeping in touch with family, for doctor appointments, for all kinds of things — and yet most people have never had anyone explain how to actually look and sound good on one. Nobody teaches this. You just kind of show up and hope for the best.

So let us change that today. No expensive gear required. Everything I am going to tell you either costs nothing or costs very little.

Let us start with the most important thing, and it is not your camera or your microphone. It is your light.

Here is the simple principle. A camera works by capturing the light that bounces off your face. If the light in front of you is dim or coming from the wrong angle, you will look dark, shadowy, or like you are hiding something in a car park. The fix is easy: put your light source in front of you, not behind you.

The most common mistake people make is sitting with a window behind them. The camera looks at you, sees a big bright window, and tries to balance for it — which means your face goes dark. All the camera can see is a silhouette of you against a glowing rectangle. You could be anyone.

Turn around so the window is in front of you. Now that natural light is falling on your face, and you look a hundred times better without spending anything. If you are in a windowless room or calling at night, a simple desk lamp placed in front of and slightly above your eye level does the same job. You do not need a ring light or studio lighting. A lamp you already own, pointed at your face, makes a genuine difference.

Now let us talk about your position on screen. The most flattering angle for a video call is with the camera roughly at eye level or just slightly above. When your laptop is on a desk and the camera is below eye level, the person on the other end spends the whole call looking up your nose. This is not a great look for anyone. It is also oddly unflattering in a way that is hard to put your finger on.

The fix: raise your laptop or tablet. A few books, a shoebox, a laptop stand — anything that brings the camera up to eye level. This one change makes you look more confident and more present. Also: sit back slightly from the screen so the other person can see a little of your shoulders. A face filling the entire screen is slightly intense and feels closer than it needs to be.

Now for sound. You might be surprised to hear that sound is actually more important than video on a call. People will forgive a slightly blurry picture far more readily than they will forgive audio that cuts out, echoes, or sounds like you are in a drainpipe.

Most built-in laptop microphones are decent but pick up a lot of background noise — fans, traffic, other people, the hum of your refrigerator. The single biggest improvement you can make to your audio costs almost nothing: use headphones with a built-in microphone rather than the laptop's speakers and mic. The microphone in a basic pair of earphones is physically much closer to your mouth than the one in your laptop, so it picks up your voice more clearly and picks up background noise less. Your voice will sound cleaner, and you will eliminate the echo that happens when your laptop microphone picks up the sound coming out of its own speakers. Even the basic white earphones that came with a phone are a genuine upgrade.

If you want to go further, a small USB microphone — a dedicated mic you plug into your computer — is the single biggest audio upgrade available for the money. They start at very affordable prices and the difference in how your voice sounds is remarkable. But it is absolutely not necessary. Good earphones are enough.

A couple of smaller things worth knowing.

Close any apps you do not need before a video call, especially on older computers. Video calls use a lot of your computer's power. If your computer is also running twenty browser tabs and a software update in the background, the call can become choppy and slow for everyone. Close what you are not using.

Check your background, or at least be aware of it. You do not need a fancy virtual background. A plain wall or a tidy corner is completely fine. But glancing behind you before the call starts is a habit worth developing, just to make sure there is nothing in the frame you would not want someone to see.

And a practical tip that sounds obvious but gets forgotten: join a few minutes early, especially for important calls. This gives you time to check that your camera is showing the right image, your microphone is working, and that you are in a reasonably quiet spot. Scrambling to get connected at the last second is stressful and it shows.

To wrap up. Light from in front of you, not behind. Camera at eye level. Sit back a little from the screen. Use headphones with a built-in mic instead of the laptop's speakers. Close unnecessary apps. Glance at your background before you join. And arrive a little early.

Your homework for this week: next time you have a video call, try flipping your position so the window is in front of you instead of behind you, and notice the difference. It is quite dramatic and it costs absolutely nothing.
