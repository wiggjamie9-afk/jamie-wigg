The clever part is teaching a model to run that process in reverse. To take a slightly fuzzy image and make it slightly clearer. It does this millions and millions of times until it gets extraordinarily good at one specific skill: looking at a noisy mess and guessing what should be underneath it.

So when you ask it to generate a brand new image, here's what actually happens. The model starts with pure noise. A random field of static, like an untuned television. And then, guided by your words, it begins to refine. Step by step, it removes noise, nudging the static a little closer to something that matches your prompt. Then again. And again. With each pass, shapes emerge from the chaos. A horizon line. A figure. A face. After enough steps, that random noise has been sculpted into a coherent picture that matches the words you typed. It is, quite literally, finding an image hidden inside randomness, and your prompt is the map that tells it which image to find.

This is why your prompt matters so enormously. The model is trying to satisfy your description, and it can only work with what you give it. If you say, a dog, you'll get a perfectly generic dog. Now, where do they fail? And these failures are famous. The first, and most notorious, is hands. For years, AI image generators have struggled to draw human hands correctly. You'd get six fingers, or seven, or fingers bending in directions no finger should bend. Why? Because hands are geometrically complex, they appear in countless positions, and the model never learned the underlying rule that a hand has five fingers. It only learned what hands tend to look like, and hands look like a lot of different things. So it guesses, and it guesses badly.

The second classic failure is text. Ask one of these models to write a specific word on a sign, and you'll often get garbled, dreamlike gibberish that looks like letters but spells nothing. The model learned what text looks like as a visual texture, not what letters mean or how they spell words. Newer models have gotten dramatically better at this, but it remains a giveaway.

And the third is consistency. If you generate the same character twice, you'll likely get two different-looking people. The model has no memory, no persistent idea of who that character is. Each image is generated fresh from noise. Keeping a character looking the same across many images is one of the hardest problems in this whole field.
