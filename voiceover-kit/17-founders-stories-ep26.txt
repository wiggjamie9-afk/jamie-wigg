Welcome to Founders' Stories.

Most founders say they want to build something big enough to last. Very few talk honestly about what it actually means when that moment arrives — when the offer comes in and the number is real and the decision has to be made.

Today I want to talk about the exit. Not as an endpoint, but as a chapter. And more specifically, about the moment before it — because that moment reveals something about a founder that almost nothing else does.

Owen Takeda built a workforce scheduling platform for the hospitality industry over seven years. Hotels, restaurants, large venue operators. The product was deeply embedded — integration with payroll systems, compliance with labor law, shift-swapping tools built specifically for the way hospitality workers actually live and communicate. Replacing it was not easy. Customers who used it tended to stay.

He received his first acquisition approach in year four, when the company was at around three million dollars in annual recurring revenue. The offer was not large enough and the timing was not right. He said no.

The second approach came in year six. A large human resources software company wanted his product to complete a gap in their hospitality vertical. The offer was meaningful. He had a term sheet within three weeks.

Owen told me that the three weeks between receiving the term sheet and signing it were some of the strangest of his life. Not because the deal was complicated — though it was — but because he kept trying to figure out how he felt and couldn't quite land on an answer.

He had spent seven years thinking about the product every single day. Not because he was disciplined about it. Because he genuinely couldn't stop. In the shower, on runs, in the middle of conversations that had nothing to do with software — he was always somewhere inside the problem. The product had become the rhythm of his mental life.

Signing the deal meant that rhythm would change. He would have a new role inside the acquiring company for a transition period. After that, uncertain.

He signed it.

He told me the thing he thought about most in those three weeks wasn't the money. It was the team. He had thirty-one employees at the time of the acquisition. He had negotiated hard for their retention packages, for their roles, for a minimum employment guarantee from the acquirer. He told me: "The product was always going to be fine. Products survive in new hands. People are different. I needed to know they would be okay."

Two years after the acquisition, most of his original team is still with the company. A few have moved on. Two of them have started their own companies. He is proud of both facts equally.

He now advises early-stage founders on product strategy and does some angel investing. He told me that the most common mistake he sees in founders approaching an exit is treating the number as the whole story.

The number matters. But so does what you negotiated for your team. So does the acquirer's actual plan for the product. So does whether you will be able to live with the decision in three years, when the earnout has settled and the integration is complete and the company you built is now a product line in someone else's portfolio.

Owen said: "An exit is not the end of your relationship with what you built. It's the beginning of the part where you no longer control it. Get comfortable with that before you sign, or you will spend years in a grief you weren't expecting."

He doesn't regret it. He would do it again. He also says that the word exit is a strange one, because you don't really exit. You just change what the relationship looks like.
