# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 14
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. After last week, which got nice and warm, all that dads and daughters stuff, today we get grubby and practical. Today we're talking about chores. Responsibility. How you raise a kid who actually does their bit, without the house turning into a daily war over a single dirty sock on the stairs.

Let me set the scene, because you've lived it. You ask your kid to do one small thing. Put your plate in the sink. That's it. The whole quest. And what follows is a saga. The sighing. The slow-motion collapse onto the sofa as if you'd asked them to climb a mountain in their socks. The but whyyyy. And you stand there thinking, it would genuinely be faster, easier, and less painful if I just did it myself. And nine times out of ten, you do. And that, right there, is the trap I want to talk about.

Here's the thing I had to get through my own thick head. The chore was never about the plate. If it was just about the plate, sure, do it yourself, you're faster, fine. But it was never about getting the job done. It's about what the kid learns about being a person who lives with other people. A kid who never has to contribute grows up genuinely believing the world is a hotel, and someone else is staff. And that someone is usually their future partner, or their future flatmate, who is going to want a word with you.

So let me reframe it. Chores are not punishment. Chores are not you offloading work because you're lazy, even though some part of your brain feels guilty like it is. Chores are how a child learns the single most important truth about family. We all pitch in. This place runs because everybody does a bit. You belong here, and belonging comes with carrying some of the load. That's not cruelty. That's membership. You're not taking from them. You're giving them a place in the team.

Right, the practical bit, because this is a topic that lives or dies on the how. A few moves.

One: start younger and smaller than feels useful. We wait too long. We think they're too little to help, so we do everything, and then we're baffled at twelve when they won't lift a finger. A three-year-old can put toys in a box. A four-year-old can carry their cup to the sink. It'll be slower and messier than doing it yourself, infinitely, but you're not buying efficiency. You're buying a kid who, at three, learns that helping is just what we do here. Plant it early, before the eye-rolling starts.

Two: be careful with paying for everything. Now, this one's got nuance, and houses differ, so take what fits. I'm a fan of some jobs being paid, as a way to teach earning, fair enough. But I think some chores should just be unpaid, full stop, because you're a member of this family and that's the rent on belonging. If a kid gets cash for every single thing, you've quietly taught them that helping the people you love is a transaction. Clearing the table after dinner shouldn't come with an invoice. Some things you just do because we're in this together.

Three: let the natural consequence do the teaching, instead of you nagging forever. This is the one that saved my sanity. If the job is to bring the dirty washing down and they don't, then their favorite shirt isn't clean for Saturday. Not as a punishment you engineer with a smug face, but as the actual, real, gentle truth of how the world works. The washing doesn't wash itself. A kid learns ten times more from one missed shirt than from a hundred lectures from a frustrated dad on the stairs. Step back, let the lesson land, and resist the urge to rescue.

And a quick word on the nagging, because I was terrible at it. The endless reminding, the increasingly annoyed voice, the counting to three. It teaches a kid one thing only. That they don't have to act until Dad reaches a certain volume. So they wait for the volume. Try, instead, to say it once, calmly, with a clear consequence, and then hold the line. The calm is the whole game. The moment you're shouting about the chore, the chore has won and the lesson is lost.

Here's what I want you to sit with. Every time you do the easy thing and just clear up after them, you're saving five minutes today and spending it, with interest, on a future where they can't look after themselves. Teaching a kid to carry their bit is slow, annoying, deeply unglamorous work. But you're not raising a tidy child. You're raising a capable adult. Someone who walks into a room and notices what needs doing and just does it, without being asked, because somewhere back in childhood, a dad let them be useful.

That's the job. Not a clean house. A capable human.

Next week we close out this run on a lovely note. Traditions. The small, daft, sacred rituals that turn a family into a family. I cannot wait. See you then.

This has been Dad Code. Now go let your kid do a job badly, slowly, and entirely by themselves.
