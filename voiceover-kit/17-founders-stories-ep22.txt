Welcome to Founders' Stories.

Today I want to talk about what it means to build alone.

Not alone in the sense of lonely, though sometimes it is that too. Alone in the sense of choosing not to have a co-founder, not to take outside funding, not to hire a team of twenty until you genuinely need them. Alone in the sense of being a solopreneur — a word that still gets a strange reaction in startup circles, as if choosing to stay small is somehow less serious than choosing to scale.

Marcus Webb had spent nine years working in the marketing departments of other people's companies when he decided, at thirty-eight, to go out on his own. He didn't have a revolutionary product idea. He didn't have a platform ambition. He had a skill — writing long-form brand content — and a quiet belief that he could sell it directly to clients who needed it, without a boss deciding what his time was worth.

He gave himself three months and three clients as his definition of success. He got the first client in week two. He got the second in week five. The third took nine weeks. Three months in, he was making more money than he had in his last salaried role.

That was six years ago. Marcus now runs what he calls a lifestyle business. He has a roster of eight ongoing clients, a small team of two freelancers he brings in on larger projects, and a four-day working week he has maintained for the past three years. His business generates what he describes as enough — a word most startup culture doesn't know what to do with.

He told me the hardest thing about going solo wasn't the money uncertainty. He'd modeled that out carefully before he quit and had twelve months of runway saved. The hardest thing was the identity shift.

In a company, your status comes from your role. You have a title. You have a team beneath you. You go to industry events and people understand where you fit. When you go out on your own, all of that scaffolding disappears overnight. You're just a person with a laptop and an invoice template, trying to explain to someone at a networking event why you don't have a startup.

It took Marcus about eighteen months to fully stop apologizing for what he'd built. The turning point was a conversation with a client who told him, unprompted, that working with him was the best vendor relationship they'd had in a decade. Not the most innovative. Not the most scalable. The best. Reliable, responsive, deeply skilled, and actually present in a way that large agencies rarely are.

He realized that what he'd built — simple, uncomplicated, fully his — was itself a competitive advantage. Clients weren't hiring an agency with layers of account management. They were hiring him. And he was always there.

The solopreneur path is not for everyone. It trades the energy of a team for the freedom of autonomy. It trades rocket-ship upside for sustainable cash flow. It trades the complexity of managing people for the complexity of being entirely responsible for your own output.

But Marcus would tell you this: the day you stop needing permission is a day that changes how you work forever.

He still gets asked when he's planning to scale. He smiles and says he already has. He just scaled in a direction most people aren't watching.
