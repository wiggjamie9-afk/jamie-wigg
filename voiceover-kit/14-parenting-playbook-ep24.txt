Welcome back to the Parenting Playbook. Today we are tackling one of the most contested and anxiety-producing topics in modern parenting: technology. Screens. Devices. How much is too much, what actually matters, and how to have a sane relationship with all of it in your household.

Let's start with some grounding. The panic about children and screens is not new — every generation has had its version of it, from television to video games to social media. That does not mean the concerns are wrong. It means we should look at the evidence carefully rather than responding from fear.

Here is what the research actually supports. For children under eighteen months, screen use other than video chatting has no demonstrated benefit and some evidence of harm to language development, likely because screens replace interaction with real people. For children eighteen months to two years, high-quality educational content watched together with a parent can have some benefit — the key word is together. For children two to five, an hour or less per day of quality content remains the guideline from the American Academy of Pediatrics, with consistent co-viewing where possible.

For school-age children and teenagers, the picture becomes more nuanced. Not all screen time is equivalent. Watching a nature documentary, video calling a grandparent, creating something, playing a collaborative game with friends, and passively scrolling through algorithmically curated content are fundamentally different activities that produce different outcomes. The research on heavy social media use in adolescents — particularly girls — does show associations with higher rates of anxiety and depression. These associations are meaningful, even if causation is complex.

What seems to matter most is displacement. The question is not simply how many hours a day your child spends on screens. It is what those hours are displacing. Sleep is the most critical. When screen time — especially in the evening — cuts into sleep, there are clear downstream effects on mood, attention, behavior, and health. Physical activity, face-to-face social interaction, and unstructured play are also in competition with screen time in a finite day.

Practical strategies that the evidence supports include keeping devices out of bedrooms at night, establishing screen-free times such as during meals and the hour before bed, watching with your child when possible and making it a shared experience, and talking openly about what they are watching and playing and who they are talking to online.

Family media plans work better than blanket bans for most households. A plan involves the whole family in setting agreements and reflects the reality that screens are part of modern life, not a temporary threat to be outlawed.

The most important thing you can model is your own relationship with your device. Children who watch parents check phones constantly during conversations, meals, and outings are receiving a very clear message about where attention belongs. Your behavior will always be more persuasive than your rules.

Technology is not the enemy. Distraction, displacement, and disconnection are. Keep your eyes on those.
