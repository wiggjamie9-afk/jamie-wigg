Welcome to Sleep and Calm. Wherever you are in the world tonight, I am glad you found your way here.

Tonight we are talking about sleep after travel — and why crossing time zones can leave even the most well-rested person feeling strangely untethered from their own body.

Jet lag is not just tiredness. It is a mismatch between your internal clock and the external world. Your body keeps its own time — a roughly twenty-four hour rhythm tied to light, temperature, digestion, and a dozen other biological signals. When you move across multiple time zones quickly, your body does not know that the world has changed. It is still living on the clock of wherever you came from. And so you find yourself wide awake at two in the morning, or falling asleep at the dinner table, or feeling a low, strange fog at hours when you would normally be sharp.

The body does adjust. But it does so slowly — typically at a rate of about one time zone per day. Cross six time zones and you may need nearly a week before you feel fully yourself again.

There are things that help.

Light is the most powerful tool you have. In your new location, seek out natural daylight early in the morning if you have traveled east, or in the late afternoon if you have traveled west. Light at the right time acts as a reset signal for your internal clock, nudging it toward the new rhythm faster than it would shift on its own.

Melatonin, taken in a small amount at your new destination's bedtime, can also ease the transition — particularly when traveling east. It does not force sleep so much as it gently signals to the body that night has arrived.

Avoid alcohol on flights and in the first days after arrival. It may feel like it helps you sleep, but it fragments the deeper, more restorative stages of rest, and it amplifies the disorientation of jet lag rather than easing it.

Drink water. Airplane cabins are dry, and dehydration compounds the fatigue of travel. A well-hydrated body recovers more quickly.

And be gentle with yourself. In the first day or two after a long journey, do not demand peak performance. Rest when you can. Step outside into daylight. Eat at local meal times even if your appetite is not yet aligned.

Your body is doing something remarkable — crossing the world and slowly, steadily recalibrating. That takes time. Give it the time it needs.

You will find your rhythm again. You always do.

Sleep well, wherever you have landed.
