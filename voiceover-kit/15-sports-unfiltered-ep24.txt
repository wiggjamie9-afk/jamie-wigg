Sports Unfiltered. Today we are following the money. Not the way the league wants you to follow it — in the direction of inspiring revenue figures and record-breaking franchise valuations — but the actual flow. Where it comes from, where it goes, who benefits, and who is told to be grateful they get to be part of it at all.

Because the economics of professional sport are among the most fascinating and least examined subjects in public life. We talk about sport constantly. We analyse athletes, coaches, drafts, trades, tactics, schedules, rivalries. And then the conversation about the financial architecture that makes all of it possible — the structure that determines who gets wealthy and who gets the minimum — stays almost entirely inside boardrooms.

Let us start with the broadcast deal, because this is the engine of everything. The rights fees that television networks and streaming platforms pay for live sport are, in every major professional league, the single largest source of revenue. And they have exploded. Not incrementally — exponentially. The reason is not complicated. Live sport is one of the only categories of content that people insist on watching in real time. It is immune to the DVR problem. It is immune to the streaming fragmentation that has killed everything else. When you own the rights to live sport, you own something genuinely scarce in the attention economy, and scarcity commands price.

That money flows into the league, which distributes a share of it to franchises, which use it to fund operations, payroll, and — increasingly — to service the debt taken on to purchase the franchise in the first place. And here is the part that almost no broadcast coverage ever gets into. The majority of franchise ownership groups are not particularly liquid. They are leveraged. The teams are the asset. The broadcast revenue is the cash flow that services the debt on acquiring that asset. Which means that when owners cry poverty in collective bargaining negotiations, they are usually crying about paper wealth and cash flow simultaneously — a combination that is genuinely confusing but should not be taken at face value.

Now here is the question I want to sit with for a moment. Who is left out of this? The cities and communities that fund stadium construction through public subsidies are almost never the ones who capture the economic return when franchises appreciate. The local workers — the stadium staff, the vendors, the lower-tier service employees — operate almost entirely outside the revenue structure that their labour makes possible. The youth athletes who feed the talent pipeline from which leagues extract billions are compensated with exposure and opportunity, not with a share of the value they ultimately produce.

Sports economics is not a neutral technical subject. It is a set of choices about who captures value and who does not. And the choices that have been made — over decades, through negotiated agreements and legislative lobbying and public financing deals — have overwhelmingly favoured the ownership class and the leagues over athletes, workers, and communities.

I am not telling you sport is evil. I am telling you sport is an industry. And industries should be examined with the same critical attention we bring to any other sector that shapes public life and distributes wealth. The fact that we love watching it is not a reason to stop asking questions about how it works.
