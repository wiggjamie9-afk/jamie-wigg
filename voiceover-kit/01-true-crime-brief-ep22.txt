Tonight, a case that began on a residential street in coastal Georgia and ended in a federal courtroom, a case about what happens when ordinary citizens appoint themselves as enforcers, and a case that forced America to reckon with a legal concept that had, in practice, functioned as a license for harm.

On the twenty-third of February, two thousand and twenty, a twenty-five-year-old man named Ahmaud Arbery went for a run in a neighborhood called Satilla Shores, outside the small city of Brunswick, Georgia. He had done this before. He knew the area. It was a Sunday afternoon.

A retired police investigator named Gregory McMichael and his son Travis McMichael saw Arbery running and decided, based on nothing more than what they later described as a suspicion that he resembled a burglary suspect, to pursue him. They armed themselves. They contacted a neighbor, William Bryan, who joined the chase in his own truck. The three men followed Arbery through the neighborhood in their vehicles for approximately five minutes, cutting off his path, until Travis McMichael stepped out of his pickup truck with a shotgun.

There was a struggle. Three shots were fired. Ahmaud Arbery was killed on a residential street in broad daylight.

The local authorities who responded to the scene initially concluded that the McMichaels had acted within their rights under Georgia's citizen's arrest statute, a law dating to eighteen sixty-three. No arrests were made. The case was passed between two district attorneys, one of whom recused himself because Gregory McMichael had previously worked in his office. For two and a half months, nothing happened.

Then a cell phone video changed everything.

William Bryan had recorded the confrontation from his truck. That video was obtained by a local attorney and released to the public on the fifth of May, two thousand and twenty. Within seventy-two hours, Gregory McMichael, Travis McMichael, and William Bryan were arrested and charged with murder. The Georgia Bureau of Investigation took over. Federal prosecutors later added hate crime charges, citing evidence of racist social media posts by Travis McMichael and his father's history of making racially derogatory statements about Black residents of the neighborhood.

At trial, the defense leaned heavily on the citizen's arrest law and claimed self-defense — that Travis McMichael had fired only when Arbery grabbed for the shotgun. Prosecutors countered that the confrontation had been manufactured from its beginning, that Arbery had been unlawfully trapped and was trying to survive. They pointed out that no evidence ever connected Arbery to any crime in that neighborhood.

In November two thousand and twenty-one, all three men were convicted of murder by a jury in Glynn County. The following January, they were also convicted in federal court on hate crime charges and attempted kidnapping. Gregory McMichael, Travis McMichael, and William Bryan are each serving life sentences.

In the wake of the convictions, Georgia repealed its citizen's arrest law entirely. Legislators acknowledged that the old statute, while rarely invoked in its original form, had existed as a legal shelter that could be used to justify vigilante violence. More than a dozen other states reviewed similar provisions on their books.

The Arbery case sits at the intersection of race, law enforcement accountability, and the limits of self-appointed authority. What made it a national turning point was not just the killing — it was the two and a half months in which nothing was done, in which the family of a twenty-five-year-old man who had gone for a run could not get an arrest warrant. The video forced a system that had been content to look away to finally look directly at what had happened.

Ahmaud Arbery would have turned twenty-seven the day after his killers were convicted of murder.
