Welcome back to the Parenting Playbook. Today we're talking about school performance — not grades specifically, but the deeper question of how to support your child's learning in a way that builds genuine capability and a healthy relationship with education.

Here's where I want to start. Academic performance is strongly shaped by factors that have almost nothing to do with intelligence. Motivation, self-regulation, sense of belonging at school, sleep, stress levels at home, and a child's beliefs about their own ability — all of these matter enormously and all of them are within your sphere of influence as a parent.

Let's start with the homework question, because it's where most school-related tension lives. Research on homework at the elementary level is actually quite mixed — there is limited evidence that it improves learning for young children. What homework does accomplish, at any age, is practice in self-management and independent work. That's worth something. But the way you support homework matters more than whether it gets done perfectly.

Effective homework support looks like this: a consistent time and place, minimal distractions, your presence without over-involvement, and encouragement when things are hard without taking over. When you do the work for them — even when it's faster and less painful — you're removing the exact struggle that builds competence. Struggle is the mechanism of learning.

A concept from educational psychology called growth mindset, which we touched on in an earlier episode, is especially relevant here. Children who believe their intelligence and ability can grow with effort respond to academic difficulty with persistence. Children who believe ability is fixed — you either get it or you don't — tend to withdraw from challenge. The language you use around school performance shapes which of these beliefs takes hold. The difference between you're struggling because this is hard and you're struggling because you haven't got it yet is small in words and large in impact.

Reading deserves specific attention. The research is unambiguous that children who are read to, and who read independently for pleasure, develop cognitive and academic advantages that compound over time. This is not about drilling phonics at the dinner table. It's about keeping books available, reading together with genuine engagement, and letting your child see you read for pleasure.

What about struggling students? If your child is significantly behind grade level, or seems to be working extremely hard with little return, that warrants a conversation with the teacher sooner rather than later. Learning differences — including dyslexia, dyscalculia, and attention difficulties — are common and very well supported with the right identification and strategies. Waiting and hoping it improves rarely works as well as early intervention.

One more thing. Ask your child what they find interesting at school, not just how they did on the test. Children who feel that their curiosity is noticed and valued at home bring that engagement back to the classroom.

School is a long game. Support the process, not just the outcome.
