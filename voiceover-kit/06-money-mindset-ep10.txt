Welcome back to Money Mindset, the calm, no-jargon finance show. I'm your host. Twelve minutes, one idea, one step, zero judgment. Take a breath with me. Here we go.

Today we're going to talk about the quiet thief that drains more wealth and more peace than almost any bad habit with money — comparison. The exhausting, low-grade pressure of keeping up with everyone around you. The neighbour's new car. The friend's renovated kitchen. The stranger on your phone on a beach you can't afford. By the end of today, I want this to have less of a grip on you.

Let's start with something kind and true. The urge to compare yourself to others is not a character flaw. It's wiring. For most of human history, watching what the people around you had was genuinely useful — it told you whether you were safe, whether you were keeping up, whether the group would look after you. Your brain still runs that ancient program. The problem is the program was built for a village of maybe one hundred and fifty people, and now you're comparing yourself to the entire planet, every time you pick up your phone.

So the first thing to understand is this: you are not weak for feeling the pull. You're running very old software in a world it was never designed for.

Now here's the part that matters for your money. Comparison doesn't just make you feel bad. It actively makes you poorer. When you buy something to match what someone else has — a bigger car, a nicer phone, a holiday for the photo of it — you're not buying the thing. You're buying a feeling, and it's a feeling that fades in about three weeks. Then the comparison resets, the bar moves up, and you do it again. It's a treadmill, and the faster you run, the more it costs you.

There's a name researchers use for this. They call it the hedonic treadmill — the way we quickly adjust to anything new and return to roughly the same level of happiness we had before. The new thing becomes the normal thing astonishingly fast. Which means almost every purchase made to keep up gives you a tiny, temporary bump, and then nothing.

And here's the cruelest twist. You are almost always comparing your full, messy, behind-the-scenes life to someone else's carefully chosen highlight. You see the new car. You don't see the loan behind it. You see the kitchen. You don't see the credit card debt, or the stress, or the marriage strain. A lot of the lifestyles you're measuring yourself against are quietly funded by debt and held together with worry. You're losing a race against an illusion.

So let's gently take some of the power back. Three small shifts.

The first shift: notice the trigger, don't fight the feeling. The next time you feel that pang of not enough, just name it quietly. There it is. The comparison. You don't have to argue with it or feel ashamed. You just have to see it, because the moment you see it, it stops running you from the shadows. Most of comparison's power comes from being invisible.

The second shift: curate your inputs. You would not let someone walk into your home five times a day and tell you your life isn't good enough. But that is exactly what some accounts on your phone do. You're allowed to mute them. You're allowed to unfollow. Not out of bitterness — out of self-protection. Spend a few minutes this week unfollowing three accounts that consistently leave you feeling behind. Your finances will quietly thank you.

The third shift: build your own scoreboard. Comparison thrives when you have no measure of your own, so you borrow everyone else's. The cure is to decide, for yourself, what actually counts as a good financial year. Maybe it's reaching your emergency fund. Maybe it's no new debt. Maybe it's one small thing that's genuinely yours. When you have your own scoreboard, the neighbour's new car becomes just a car — their choice, not your verdict.

I want to say one more thing, because it's the heart of all this. The opposite of comparison isn't having more than other people. It's gratitude — quietly noticing what you already have before you go looking for what you lack. Gratitude and comparison genuinely cannot occupy the same moment. The second you truly take stock of what's good, the pull to keep up goes quiet on its own.

Here's your single step for today, and it takes about five minutes: open the app or the feed that most often leaves you feeling behind, and unfollow or mute three accounts. Just three. Then write down one thing you have right now that you'd have been thrilled about five years ago. That's your scoreboard starting.

Let's recap. Comparison is ancient wiring, not a flaw — but in a world of endless highlight reels, it quietly makes you both poorer and unhappier. You're usually racing against an illusion funded by someone else's debt. Notice the feeling without shame, curate what you let in, and build your own scoreboard. And remember, gratitude is the one thing comparison can't survive.

Your one step today: mute or unfollow three accounts that make you feel behind, and write down one thing you're grateful to already have.
