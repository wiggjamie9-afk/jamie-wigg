Welcome to Founders' Stories.

Ali Hassan arrived in the United States from Egypt in two thousand and one with a graduate scholarship, two suitcases, and what he describes as a very specific, very American education in how things work here.

He means that partly as a compliment and partly as something else.

He did his graduate work in computer science, spent four years at a large software company, and started his first business in two thousand and eight — a consulting firm that helped mid-size companies migrate their data infrastructure. It was, he says, a perfectly fine business. Steady clients, reasonable margins, a team of eight. He sold it in two thousand and twelve and made a modest return.

The second business was different. Not in what it did — it was still in data infrastructure — but in what he understood going into it.

He had spent his first decade in the United States learning to read a room. Not in a social sense, though that too. In a business sense. He had learned which doors you knock on and which ones you call ahead about. He had learned whose name to mention in an introduction email and whose to leave out. He had learned the rhythms of an American sales cycle, the language of investor updates, the unwritten rules of hiring and letting people go with dignity. He had learned all of this from outside, watching carefully, making the occasional expensive mistake and filing it away.

The second company, which he launched in two thousand and fourteen, raised a seed round within six months. He knew what the investors needed to hear — not because he was telling them what they wanted, but because he understood the frame they were using. He could meet them in their language.

He told me something about the immigrant founder experience that I don't hear discussed often enough. He said that being an outsider is an asset inside a business, even when it feels like a liability in the room. You notice things that the people inside the system can no longer see because the system has become invisible to them. You question assumptions that everyone else has stopped questioning. You ask why things are done a certain way and sometimes the honest answer, underneath the industry jargon, is that nobody really knows.

Ali's second company built a data observability product — software that monitors the health and reliability of business data pipelines. It was a problem he had seen up close in his consulting years, one that almost every enterprise had and almost none talked about publicly because admitting your data was unreliable felt like admitting your business was unreliable.

He grew the company to forty employees over four years, raised a Series B, and was acquired in two thousand and twenty-one by a larger infrastructure software company.

When I asked him what advice he would give an immigrant founder in their first years, he paused for a long time before answering.

He said: "Be patient with yourself on the cultural learning curve. It is real and it costs time. And then, when you have paid that cost, remember what you paid it for. You understand things about building under constraint, about operating without safety nets, about why things matter, that a lot of people in this ecosystem have never had to learn. That's not a disadvantage. It's the reason you'll outlast them in the hard moments."

He built two companies on ground that wasn't his by birth. The second one, he was ready for.
