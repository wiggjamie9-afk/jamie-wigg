This is AI Briefing. Today: AI agents. You've heard the phrase everywhere — agentic AI, AI agents, the agentic era. It's the buzzword of the moment, and like most buzzwords, it's used to mean five different things at once. So let's cut through it. What is an AI agent, really? What can one actually do? And where's the line between the genuine shift and the hype?

Start with what came before, because that's the contrast that matters. The AI assistant most people have used — the chatbot you type a question into — works in a simple loop. You ask. It answers. You ask again. It answers again. It's a conversation. Every step needs you. The assistant is a brilliant responder, but it waits. It does nothing on its own.

An AI agent breaks that loop. The simplest definition: an agent is an AI system that can take actions to accomplish a goal, not just produce text. You give it an objective, and it works toward that objective across multiple steps — deciding what to do, doing it, looking at the result, and deciding what to do next. It can use tools. It can browse. It can run code, send a request, check a calendar, edit a file. And crucially, it loops on its own, without you prompting every single move.

Here's the difference in one sentence. An assistant tells you how to do something. An agent goes and does it.

Let me make that concrete. Suppose you say: find me a flight to Tokyo next month under eight hundred dollars and book the cheapest one that has a window seat.

A chatbot would give you advice. It might tell you which sites to check, what days are usually cheaper, how to find deals. Useful — but you still do all the work.

An agent, in principle, takes the goal and runs. It searches the flight sites. It reads the results. It compares prices against your limit. It checks seat maps. If the first option doesn't have a window seat, it doesn't stop and ask — it moves to the next one. It chains those steps together until the goal is met. That loop — act, observe, adjust, repeat — is the whole idea. That's what makes it an agent rather than an assistant.

Underneath, most agents share the same basic anatomy. Four parts.

One: the model. The large language model at the core — the reasoning engine that decides what to do. This is the brain.

Two: tools. The things the agent is allowed to use. A web browser, a calculator, a code runner, a connection to your email or your files. Tools are how the agent reaches out and affects the world instead of just talking about it.

Three: memory. A way to keep track of what it's already done and what it's learned along the way, so that step five remembers what happened in step two.

Four: the loop. The logic that ties it together — take a step, look at the outcome, decide the next step, keep going until the goal is reached or it gets stuck.

That's it. A brain, some hands, a notepad, and a cycle. Everything else is detail.

Now — where is this genuinely useful, today? A few areas are real and working right now.

Coding is the standout. AI agents that write software, run it, see the errors, fix them, and run it again are already changing how developers work. The loop fits programming perfectly, because code gives instant feedback — it either runs or it doesn't, and the agent can read that result and try again.

Research and data gathering is another. An agent that searches many sources, reads them, pulls out the relevant pieces, and assembles a summary saves real hours.

Customer support, scheduling, and repetitive office tasks — anywhere the work is a series of well-defined steps — are landing zones too.

But here's the honest part of the briefing, because this is where the hype outruns reality.

Agents are not reliable the way a calculator is reliable. Every step in that loop is a chance for the model to make a mistake — and because the steps build on each other, a small error early can compound into a useless or wrong result by the end. More steps means more places to go wrong.

They can get stuck in loops, repeating the same failed action. They can misread a result and confidently head in the wrong direction. And when you give an agent real power — access to your email, your money, your files — a mistake stops being a bad sentence and starts being a real consequence. An agent that books the wrong flight, or sends the wrong message, or deletes the wrong file, is a different kind of problem than a chatbot that gives a clumsy answer.

That's why, for now, the sensible pattern is a human in the loop. The agent does the work; you approve the important, irreversible steps. It drafts; you send. It proposes the booking; you confirm the purchase. The further a task is from real-world consequences, the more freedom an agent can safely have. The closer it gets to your bank account, the more you want your hand on the final button.

So, the briefing on AI agents. An agent is an AI that takes actions toward a goal, in a loop, using tools — not just an assistant that answers questions. It's made of a model, tools, memory, and that act-observe-adjust cycle. It's genuinely powerful for coding, research, and multi-step tasks. And it's genuinely unreliable enough that, today, you keep a human watching the important steps.

The shift is real. The era of AI that does things, not just says things, has started. But we're early — and early is exactly when it pays to understand how the thing actually works.

That's the briefing. I'm your host, and I'll see you next time.
