This is The Longevity Brief. Twelve minutes, one topic, the science with the noise filtered out, and a couple of things you can actually do.

Reminder: general education, not medical advice. Supplements can interact with medications and medical conditions, so before starting anything, talk to your doctor or pharmacist, especially if you take prescription drugs.

Today, the episode you may have been waiting for, and maybe dreading: supplements. The longevity world is awash in pills and powders promising to slow aging. So let's do something the marketing won't, walk through what the evidence actually says, honestly, including where it says no.

Let me start with the single most important frame. For a healthy person eating a reasonable diet, the evidence that any supplement extends lifespan is weak to nonexistent. There is no pill shown in solid human trials to make you live longer. I want to say that clearly up front, because almost everything sold in this space implies otherwise. Supplements can be useful for treating specific deficiencies. That is very different from enhancing an already healthy body.

Now let's get specific, because nuance matters.

First, the multivitamin. For a person eating a varied diet, large trials have generally not shown that a daily multivitamin prevents heart disease, cancer, or early death. It's not poison, it's just mostly unnecessary insurance. The exception is people with genuine gaps, certain diets, conditions, or life stages.

Second, vitamin D. This one's genuinely mixed and worth understanding. Many people, especially in less sunny climates or those who spend little time outdoors, do run low. Correcting a real deficiency is sensible, and your doctor can test for it. But the bigger hope, that vitamin D supplements in people who aren't deficient prevent fractures, cancer, or extend life, has largely not held up in large trials. So, test if you might be low, treat if you are, but don't expect magic.

Third, omega-three fish oil. Strong evidence supports eating fish. The evidence for fish oil pills in healthy people is much weaker, and large trials have been underwhelming for preventing heart disease in the general population. For some specific patients, under medical guidance, there's a role. For most people, eating fish beats the capsule.

Fourth, the trendy longevity molecules. You'll hear names, things marketed around cellular aging and energy metabolism. I'll be careful here. Some have interesting early science, often in animals or short human studies. None has the kind of long-term human evidence that would justify the confident claims, or the prices, attached to them. Treat them as experiments you'd be paying to participate in, not proven tools.

Fifth, and this is the part the marketing hides: supplements carry real downsides. They're loosely regulated in many countries, meaning what's on the label isn't guaranteed to be in the bottle. Some interact dangerously with medications. And a few can actively harm, high-dose beta-carotene supplements, for instance, increased lung cancer risk in smokers in major trials. More is not safer. Fat-soluble vitamins can build up to toxic levels. This is not a harmless category.

So who genuinely benefits from supplements? Real situations exist. Vitamin B-twelve for many older adults and people on plant-based diets. Folate in pregnancy. Vitamin D for those who are deficient. Iron for diagnosed deficiency. Specific needs your doctor identifies. These are targeted corrections, backed by evidence, not lifestyle accessories.

Let me give you the honest practical takeaway.

One. Spend on food first. The nutrients that track with healthy aging come packaged in whole foods, with fiber and other compounds a pill can't replicate. A pill cannot fix a poor diet.

Two. Test, don't guess. If you're worried about a deficiency, ask your doctor for a blood test rather than blindly taking a stack of pills. Treat what's actually low.

Three. Be skeptical of any supplement promising to slow aging or extend life. That claim is, right now, ahead of the evidence, and sometimes it carries real risk.

The brief on supplements: they're valuable for correcting genuine deficiencies and close to useless, occasionally harmful, as longevity insurance for the already healthy. The diet does the work the bottle promises.

One thing this week: look at whatever supplements you currently take, and ask one honest question of each, is there real evidence I need this, or am I just buying hope? Bring the list to your next doctor's visit.
