Welcome to Calm Mind. Find a comfortable place to settle, and let yourself arrive.

Today we are going to talk about social anxiety. Not the mild nerves most people feel before a big presentation or a first date. The kind that can make a simple phone call feel like walking a tightrope. The kind that replays every conversation afterwards, looking for what you said wrong, how you were perceived, whether you came across the way you hoped.

If that sounds familiar, I want you to know something first. You are not strange. You are not broken. Social anxiety is one of the most common experiences humans have, and many of the people who appear the most effortlessly confident are managing very similar feelings underneath.

Social anxiety tends to grow in the gap between who we think we are and who we think we need to be around others. It thrives on self-monitoring — that internal camera that follows us around, recording everything, replaying it, editing it, judging it.

Social anxiety often has a core belief beneath it, something like: if people really knew me, they would not like what they saw. Or: I will say something wrong and be humiliated. Or simply: I am fundamentally different from everyone else in this room, and they will notice.

These beliefs feel like truth. But they are not facts. They are interpretations, shaped by experience, by self-protection, by a nervous system trying very hard to keep you safe in situations it once learned to treat as risky.

Here is what can help.

First, notice the self-focused attention. When anxiety rises in a social setting, almost everything we notice is about us. How we look, sound, are being received. Gently redirecting attention outward — to the other person, to the conversation, to genuine curiosity about them — reduces the self-monitoring loop. You cannot be fully interested in someone else and fully self-monitoring at the same time.

Second, allow the imperfection. Social anxiety feeds on the idea that there is a perfect version of you that should show up, and that anything less is a failure. Most real connection happens not through flawlessness, but through warmth. Through honesty. Through someone making a small mistake and laughing at themselves. Through the ordinary and imperfect texture of being a person.

Third, be gentle with the replay. The post-conversation review where you go over every word — that process is not giving you accurate information. Anxious memory is selective. It finds evidence for what it already fears. You can notice the replay happening, and choose not to treat it as a reliable narrator.

If social anxiety is significantly limiting your life, a therapist trained in cognitive behavioural therapy can offer tools that go much deeper than anything we can cover here. You do not have to manage this entirely alone.

You belong in rooms, in conversations, in connections. Not a perfect version of you. The actual you. That is the one people can actually meet, and actually know.
