In the forests of the Pacific Northwest of North America, something has been reported consistently for well over a century. Indigenous peoples of the region described a large, upright, hair-covered being long before European settlers arrived. The Salish and Lummi peoples had names for it. The accounts were part of oral traditions that predate written records of the continent by thousands of years. When settlers came and the forests were mapped and the logging began, the reports did not stop. If anything, as more people moved into the wilderness, more encounters were recorded.

Bigfoot, or Sasquatch, occupies a peculiar space in contemporary culture. It is simultaneously dismissed as obvious folklore and studied with genuine scientific rigor by a small but serious community of researchers. The dismissal is understandable. Decades of investigation have not produced a body, a bone, a tooth, or a confirmed genetic sample that belongs to a large unknown primate in North America. The evidence that exists consists largely of footprint casts, blurry photographs, contested video footage, and witness testimony. In the hierarchy of evidence, that is not a strong foundation.

The most significant piece of physical evidence remains the Patterson-Gimlin film, shot on the sixteenth of October, nineteen sixty-seven, at Bluff Creek in northern California. Roger Patterson and Bob Gimlin were searching for the creature when they filmed a large, upright, hair-covered figure walking away from them along a creek bed and glancing back at the camera. The film is forty-five years old and has never been definitively proven to be a hoax, and it has never been accepted as genuine evidence of an unknown species. Efforts to replicate it using a person in a costume have produced mixed results. Some biomechanics researchers who analyzed the gait of the filmed figure concluded it moved in ways that would be extremely difficult for a human to replicate. Others have disagreed.

The primate record itself is worth examining. Gigantopithecus blacki was a real genus of enormous ape that lived in Asia and went extinct somewhere between one hundred thousand and three hundred thousand years ago based on current fossil evidence. It was roughly the height described in Bigfoot accounts. Some researchers have proposed it as an ancestor, suggesting a population could have crossed the Bering land bridge during glacial periods. This is speculative and unsupported by fossil evidence in North America, but it is not biologically incoherent.

The deeper problem is the forest itself. The Pacific Northwest contains some of the most densely forested and difficult terrain in North America. Wolves were considered locally extinct in parts of the Rocky Mountain region and reappeared when they were reintroduced in nineteen ninety-five. Before reintroduction, surveys had not detected them in those habitats. Large animals can exist in significant numbers in difficult terrain without being caught on camera or leaving remains in accessible places.

That is not an argument for the existence of Bigfoot. It is an argument that absence of evidence in dense wilderness should be weighted carefully.

The consistent thread across all of this is the witnesses. People of all backgrounds, in all decades, in all weather conditions, continue to report encounters. Most describe the same thing: something large, bipedal, and covered in dark hair, moving through the trees with a speed and quietness that does not match a bear or an elk. Most of them were not looking for it. Most of them, when describing the encounter, describe the same feeling first.

Fear. Immediate, animal fear. The kind that does not wait for the mind to name what the eyes are seeing.

Whatever is producing that response in the forests of the Pacific Northwest, the reports have not stopped.
