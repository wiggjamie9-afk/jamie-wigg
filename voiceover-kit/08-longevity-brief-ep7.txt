This is Raven, and you're listening to the Longevity Brief. Twelve minutes, one topic, the science with the noise filtered out, and a couple of things you can actually do.

Reminder: general education, not medical advice. If you have an inflammatory condition or take related medication, work with your doctor, not a podcast.

Today, a process that sits quietly underneath almost every major disease of aging. Researchers have a slightly clumsy nickname for it: inflammaging. It's the slow, low-grade inflammation that tends to build up as we get older, and it may be one of the common roots that ties heart disease, diabetes, and cognitive decline together.

Start with what inflammation actually is, because the word gets misused. In its proper form, inflammation is one of the best things your body does. When you cut your finger or fight off an infection, your immune system floods the area, cleans up the threat, and then, critically, switches off. That's acute inflammation. It's sharp, useful, and temporary. It saves your life.

The problem is a different kind. Imagine that alarm never fully turning off. A low hum of immune activity, running in the background, year after year, not high enough to feel, but never resolving. That's chronic, low-grade inflammation. And as we age, for a mix of reasons, this background hum tends to creep upward. That's the inflammaging idea.

Why does this matter for how long you live? Because chronically elevated inflammatory markers are associated, across many large studies, with higher rates of heart disease, type two diabetes, certain cancers, and dementia. Now, I want to be careful here, because this is the honest part. Inflammation is often tangled up with these diseases as both a cause and a consequence, and untangling the direction of the arrow is hard. We should resist the temptation to call inflammation the single villain behind all aging. But as a shared thread that shows up again and again, it's a genuinely useful lens.

Let me clear up two myths, because this topic attracts a lot of selling.

The first myth is that there's an anti-inflammatory diet built from exotic superfoods and expensive supplements. The evidence does not point to a miracle ingredient. It points to a boring, familiar pattern: mostly whole foods, plenty of vegetables, fish, nuts, olive oil, and not much ultra-processed food, sugar, or excess alcohol. The same pattern that keeps showing up everywhere in this field. There is no special inflammation-fighting berry that beats simply eating well.

The second myth is that inflammation is purely about food. It isn't. Some of the strongest contributors to chronic inflammation are things we rarely connect to it: poor sleep, ongoing psychological stress, carrying excess body fat, smoking, and being sedentary. You cannot eat your way out of a life that's chronically under-slept and inactive. The lifestyle inputs work together.

So what actually lowers the background hum?

One. Move regularly. Exercise has a genuinely anti-inflammatory effect over time, which ties back to everything we've said about muscle and fitness. Movement is medicine here in a literal, measurable way.

Two. Protect your sleep. We devoted a whole episode to this for good reason. Short and poor sleep reliably nudges inflammatory markers up. Fixing your sleep is one of the most direct levers you have.

Three. Eat the unglamorous good stuff and cut the obvious bad stuff. More vegetables, fish, nuts, and olive oil. Less ultra-processed food, added sugar, and heavy alcohol. You don't need a special diet name. You need the pattern.

And a quieter fourth: manage chronic stress and tend your relationships, because loneliness and unrelenting stress show up in the inflammation data too.

The brief on inflammation: acute inflammation saves you, but a low chronic hum may underlie much of what goes wrong in aging, there's no magic anti-inflammatory food, and the real levers are movement, sleep, sane eating, and lower stress.

One thing this week: pick the single biggest source of chronic inflammation in your own life, whether that's poor sleep, sitting all day, or ultra-processed food, and improve just that one thing.
