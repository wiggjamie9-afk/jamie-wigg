Welcome to Plain Tech. I'm here to make technology simple, useful, and a little less stressful.

Today we are talking about email security. Specifically, how to know when an email is trying to trick you — and what to do about it.

Email is one of the oldest tools on the internet, and it is still one of the most common ways criminals try to get your information. The good news is that once you know what to look for, you get pretty good at spotting the bad ones quickly.

Let's start with what these fake emails are actually trying to do. Most of them want one of three things: your password, your credit card number, or access to your accounts. They do this by pretending to be someone you trust — your bank, a delivery company, even a friend.

The first thing to check is the sender's email address. Not the name that appears at the top of the email, but the actual address. You can usually see it by clicking or tapping on the sender's name. A legitimate email from your bank will come from the bank's real website address. If you see something that looks slightly off — extra letters, a random number, or a completely different website name — that is a red flag.

The second thing to watch for is urgency. Fake emails love urgency. They say things like "your account will be closed in twenty four hours" or "we noticed unusual activity, act now." Real companies almost never ask you to do something immediately through an email link. If you feel pressured, slow down.

Third, hover over any links before you click them. On a phone you can press and hold a link to see where it actually goes. If the web address looks strange or does not match the company they claim to be, do not click it.

Fourth, check the greeting. Fake emails often say "Dear Customer" or "Hello User" instead of using your actual name. Your real bank knows your name.

Fifth, look for spelling mistakes and awkward sentences. Not all scam emails are poorly written these days, but many still are. Odd phrasing or grammar that feels slightly off is worth paying attention to.

Now, what should you do if you get a suspicious email? Do not click anything inside it. Do not reply to it. If the email claims to be from your bank or a service you actually use, go directly to that company's website by typing the address yourself, or call their official number. Report the suspicious email using your email provider's "report spam" or "report phishing" button.

One more thing: if you use the same password on multiple websites, now is a great time to change that habit. A password manager can help you keep track of unique, strong passwords without having to memorise them all.

You do not need to be a tech expert to stay safe online. You just need to pause before you click.
