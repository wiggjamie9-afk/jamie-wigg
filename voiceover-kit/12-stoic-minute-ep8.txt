Welcome to Stoic Minute. I'm Harry.

Today's words come from Seneca, and they cut against almost everything our age tells us: It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor.

Think about how strange that is to modern ears. We measure wealth by what's in the account, the size of the house, the number on the salary. Seneca measures it by something else entirely. By the distance between what you have and what you feel you need. A man with a fortune who is forever reaching for the next thing is, by this measure, poorer than a man with little who has stopped reaching. Poverty, he says, lives in the craving, not in the bank.

This matters because the craving is a treadmill that never ends. You get the thing you wanted, and for a day or two the wanting goes quiet. And then it relocates. It attaches itself to the next thing, and the next, and you spend a whole life sprinting toward a horizon that moves exactly as fast as you do. The Stoics noticed that the goalposts always move, and concluded that the only way to win is to stop playing that particular game.

Their alternative was not to own nothing. It was to want what you already have. To turn your attention, even briefly, to the abundance that's already arrived and gone unnoticed because you were busy chasing what hadn't.

So today, try one small thing. Look at something you own, or some part of your life you wanted years ago and now barely see. And let yourself feel, for a moment, that you already have enough. Not forever. Just for today.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
