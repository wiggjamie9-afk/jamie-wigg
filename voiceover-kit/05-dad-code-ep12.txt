# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 12
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. After last week, which got a bit shouty, all that sideline stuff, today we go quiet. We go careful. Today we're talking about the hard conversations. The big questions. Death, fairness, why bad things happen, where did grandad go. The ones you cannot google your way out of, that arrive with no warning, usually in the bath, at half past seven.

Let me tell you about the moment that wrecked me. My daughter, five years old, in the bath, playing with a boat, and she looks up and goes, completely calm, Daddy, are you going to die one day? And just like that, the bathroom floor disappears. And every instinct in your body says deflect, deflect, change the subject, where's the boat going, look, bubbles. Because you don't want to scare her, but really, if we're honest, you don't want to feel it yourself.

And here's the first thing I learned, and it took me a few fumbled tries. The instinct to protect them from the hard truth is mostly an instinct to protect yourself. They're not actually that fragile. They handle the truth far better than they handle a dad who's clearly lying with a frozen smile on his face. Kids are tiny lie detectors. The moment you go weird and shifty, you've taught them that this subject is dangerous, that it scares even Dad, and now they've got the scary question and the knowledge that they can't bring it to you. That's the worst of both worlds.

So here's the reframe. Your job is not to have the perfect answer. Nobody has the perfect answer. Your job is to be the kind of dad these questions can safely land on. To be unshockable. To be the calm in the room when the big stuff shows up. The answer matters way less than your face while you give it.

Let me give you a few moves that actually held up.

One: it's always okay to say I don't know. In fact it might be the most honest, most useful thing you ever say to your kid. I don't know what happens after we die, sweetheart. Nobody really knows for sure. But I know I'm here right now, and I'm not planning on going anywhere for a very, very long time. You're allowed to not have it figured out. Pretending you do is how kids end up never trusting a word you say.

Two: answer the question they actually asked, and not the giant one in your head. When a four-year-old asks where babies come from, they often just want to know which hospital. We hear the question and our adult brain detonates into a full biology lecture. Slow down. Ask them, what made you think of that? Find out what they're really after. Usually it's smaller and simpler than the panic in your chest.

Three: don't fake a feeling you don't have, but don't dump all of yours either. If a pet dies, or a grandparent, it's good for them to see that you're sad. That's how they learn sadness is survivable, that big feelings don't break a person. But there's a line. They shouldn't have to comfort you. They need to see a dad who feels it and is still standing. Sad, and steady. Both at once. That's the thing they're learning to do for the rest of their lives, and they're learning it from your face.

Now, a gentle flag, because this is a sensitive one. Some of these conversations touch your own family's grief, your own beliefs, things that are genuinely heavy and personal. You know your kid and you know your house. You decide how much, and when. There's no shame in saying, that's a really big question, let's talk about it properly tomorrow when we're not so tired. Buying yourself a night to think is allowed.

Here's what I want you to sit with. Your kid is going to live in a world full of things that aren't fair, and people who leave, and questions with no clean answer. You cannot fix that. You cannot armor them against it. What you can do is be the first safe place those questions ever landed. So that for the whole rest of their life, when something huge and frightening shows up, some quiet part of them remembers: I can take the hard stuff to someone, and they won't flinch, and they won't lie, and they'll still be there in the morning.

That's the gift. Not the answer. The steadiness underneath it.

Next week we lighten right up, because I think we've earned it. Dads and daughters. The thing I was the most scared of, and the most wrong about. See you then.

This has been Dad Code. Now go let yourself be asked something you can't answer.
