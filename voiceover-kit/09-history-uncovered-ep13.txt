Imagine a man so rich that when he travelled, he changed the economy of every country he passed through. Imagine a man whose generosity was so vast that he accidentally crashed the price of gold across an entire region, and it took more than a decade to recover. He may have been the wealthiest human being who ever lived, richer than any king or emperor or modern billionaire, and he ruled an empire that much of the world has never even heard of.

Welcome to History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, the story of Mansa Musa, the richest man in history.

His full title was Mansa Musa, and the word Mansa meant king, or emperor, or sultan. He ruled the empire of Mali, in West Africa, in the early part of the fourteenth century, around the year thirteen twelve. Today, when many people picture medieval Africa, they imagine something small and poor and isolated. The truth could not be more different. The Mali Empire was enormous, one of the largest states on earth at the time, stretching across a great swathe of West Africa and taking in lands that today belong to a dozen modern nations. And it sat on top of something the whole world wanted. It controlled the source of an astonishing share of the gold then circulating across Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa.

In the world of the fourteenth century, gold was wealth itself, and salt was nearly as precious, and Mali had abundant access to both. The trade routes that crossed the Sahara Desert, with their great caravans of camels, ran straight through Mansa Musa's territory, and he taxed them. The riches that flowed into his treasury were almost beyond counting. Modern attempts to estimate his wealth tend to give up, because it was simply too vast and too different in kind to compare with the fortunes we measure today. It is enough to say that he commanded more gold than perhaps any single person before or since.

For most of his reign, the wider world barely knew he existed. And then, in the year thirteen twenty-four, Mansa Musa decided to make the pilgrimage to Mecca, the holy journey that every devout Muslim hopes to make at least once. He was a devout man, and the pilgrimage was a sacred duty. But Mansa Musa did not travel as a humble pilgrim. He travelled as the emperor of one of the wealthiest realms on earth, and the procession he assembled has echoed down through history as one of the most spectacular displays of wealth ever recorded.

The chroniclers tell us that his caravan stretched for miles. He was said to be accompanied by tens of thousands of people, soldiers, officials, attendants, and enslaved servants. Hundreds of camels followed him, each one, it was said, laden with hundreds of pounds of gold. Ahead of the emperor walked heralds dressed in silk, carrying golden staffs. The whole procession glittered as it crossed the desert, a moving river of wealth on its way across a continent. And as it went, it gave that wealth away.

This is where the story becomes almost legendary. When Mansa Musa and his enormous caravan arrived in Cairo, in Egypt, the effect was extraordinary. He was so generous, and he distributed so much gold, to officials, to the poor, to anyone he met, and he and his followers spent so freely in the markets, that he flooded the entire city with gold. And here a strange thing happened, a lesson in economics played out in real time. Because so much gold suddenly poured into Cairo, the value of gold there collapsed. There was simply too much of it. Prices were thrown into chaos. By the accounts of Arab historians who wrote about it afterward, the price of gold in the region was depressed for years, perhaps as long as a decade, before it recovered. A single man, through nothing but his passage and his generosity, had disrupted the economy of one of the great cities of the world.

He completed his pilgrimage to Mecca, and he returned home. But he came back changed, and he changed his empire in turn. Along his journey he had encountered scholars, architects, and poets, and he brought some of them back with him. He poured his wealth into building. He is credited with commissioning great mosques and centres of learning, and his reign is closely associated with the rise of the city of Timbuktu as a jewel of the medieval world. Under Mali's patronage, Timbuktu became one of the most important centres of trade, culture, and scholarship anywhere on the planet. Its libraries held hundreds of thousands of manuscripts. Scholars came from across the Islamic world to study there. For a time, this West African city was a beacon of knowledge, mathematics, astronomy, and law.

And there was one more consequence of that fabulous pilgrimage. Word of it spread far and wide, carried by every traveller and merchant who had witnessed it. The story of the African emperor and his rivers of gold reached the courts and map-makers of Europe. Decades later, a famous map of the known world was drawn, and on it, in the heart of West Africa, sat a regal figure on a throne, crowned in gold, holding a golden orb. It was Mansa Musa, depicted for European eyes as the lord of a land of unimaginable riches. For the first time, much of the outside world became aware of the wealth and the kingdoms of West Africa, and it was Mansa Musa's golden journey that had put them on the map.

So what does the story of Mansa Musa teach us? Perhaps it is that our picture of the past is far smaller and far poorer than the past itself truly was. We are taught a history that often shrinks whole continents and whole civilisations down to a footnote, when in fact there were empires of breathtaking wealth, cities of learning, and rulers whose fortunes dwarfed anything we can imagine. Mansa Musa was real. The libraries of Timbuktu were real. The rivers of gold that crossed the desert were real. And the only reason so many have never heard his name is that for a long time, the story was simply not told. He was here, the richest man who ever lived, and his empire shone with gold and with knowledge, and it is well past time we remembered it.
