Tonight, a body on a beach, a name that was not known for forty years, and a resolution that came not from a witness, not from a confession, but from a genealogical database and the DNA of a distant relative who had no idea they were connected to one of the longest-running unidentified homicide cases in Massachusetts history. The victim became known as the Lady of the Dunes. What took four decades to learn is a story about what forensic science can now do with the evidence that has been waiting, in cold storage, all along.

On the tenth of July, nineteen seventy-four, a young girl walking her dog near the dunes at Race Point Beach in Provincetown, Massachusetts, made a discovery that would become one of the Cape's most enduring mysteries. A woman's body lay in a hollow in the sand, partially covered by pine boughs that someone had placed deliberately over her. She had been dead for some time. She had been murdered — her skull showed significant blunt force trauma and evidence of a sharp implement. Her hands had been severed and were not found at the scene. She was nude. She wore only a blue bandana over her hair.

Investigators initially estimated she was between twenty-five and forty years old, well-nourished, with reddish-brown hair and a distinctive set of dental reconstructions suggesting prior access to quality dental care. She had no identification. No one reported her missing. No match came through missing person systems. The local medical examiner and state police worked the case, but without a name to start from, the investigation stalled almost immediately.

Provincetown in the summer of nineteen seventy-four was a particular kind of place — an artists' colony, a gathering point, a community where people passed through without leaving records. The victim could have come from anywhere.

For forty years, she remained nameless. The case attracted periodic attention, including a theory — never confirmed — that the woman's face bore a resemblance to an extra appearing in a crowd scene in the nineteen seventy-five film Jaws, which had been partially filmed on Cape Cod the previous year. That theory circulated widely but produced nothing definitive.

Then came the genealogical technology that has become, in the twenty-first century, one of the most powerful tools in cold case investigation. In two thousand and twenty-three, the Massachusetts State Police Cold Case Unit, working with forensic genealogists, submitted DNA from the victim's remains to investigative genealogy databases. The process was the same one that had identified the Golden State Killer five years earlier — searching for partial genetic matches, building family trees from those matches, and narrowing the branches until a candidate emerged.

The DNA returned a match to a relative. Investigators built outward from there, charting family lines, eliminating candidates by age and geography and other factors, until they arrived at a name. In August two thousand and twenty-three, the Massachusetts State Police announced that the Lady of the Dunes had been identified as Ruth Marie Terry, a woman born in nineteen forty-six who had grown up in the South. She had disappeared without explanation in the early nineteen seventies. No one had connected her absence to the unidentified woman found in Provincetown because no one had known where to look or what to compare.

The identification of Ruth Marie Terry closed one question and left another entirely open. Investigators still do not know who killed her, or why, or precisely how she came to be in Provincetown in the summer of nineteen seventy-four. A name is not a prosecution. The case is classified as an active homicide investigation.

What the Lady of the Dunes represents is both the remarkable progress forensic science has made and its limits. A woman who lay unidentified for forty-nine years now has a name. Her family, or what remains of it, knows what happened to her in the broadest sense. But the person who placed pine boughs over her body on that beach in nineteen seventy-four has not been named. And in the absence of a living suspect, in the absence of a confession, in the absence of anything that can now be matched against that cold, preserved crime scene, the question of justice remains unanswered.

Her name was Ruth Marie Terry. It took the world forty-nine years to learn it.
