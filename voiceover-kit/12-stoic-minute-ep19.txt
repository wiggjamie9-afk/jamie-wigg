Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

Seneca wrote, near the end of a long life, a line that carries the whole of his philosophy in one sentence: We suffer more in imagination than in reality.

He was not dismissing pain. Seneca knew grief personally and wrote about it with honesty. He lost friends, faced exile, navigated decades of danger under volatile emperors. He was not the kind of man offering comfort from a safe distance. What he identified was a specific and very human habit: the tendency to suffer every difficulty twice, or twenty times, in the mind, long before, or long after, it arrives in fact.

Think about the last time you lay awake anticipating something difficult. A hard conversation. A result you were waiting on. A confrontation you could not avoid. The mind does not hold the scene once and put it down. It returns to it, adds detail, imagines the worst outcome, rehearses the disaster in vivid colour, and then rehearses it again. The actual event, when it arrives, is often considerably less terrible than the previews. But the suffering in the interval was entirely real.

The Stoics were precise about where this suffering comes from. It does not come from the future, because the future does not yet exist. It comes from an idea of the future, held in the mind, fed by imagination, and taken to be more real than it is. Marcus Aurelius put a related point sharply: Stop letting your mind be a slave, stop being jerked by selfish impulses, stop being either dissatisfied with the present or in dread of the future.

The remedy is not to stop imagining altogether. It is to notice when imagination has crossed from useful preparation into unnecessary suffering. Useful preparation is brief and purposeful: here is what might happen, here is how I would respond. The rest, the rumination, the catastrophising, the loop that will not stop, is suffering you are administering to yourself.

Here is today's practice. If you find your mind returning to a feared future event today, ask yourself one question: is there anything useful I can do about this right now? If yes, do that thing. If no, gently set the thought down. Not by forcing it away, but by returning to the actual present moment, which is usually, on inspection, manageable.

The present is where you actually live. The catastrophe is usually still in your imagination.
