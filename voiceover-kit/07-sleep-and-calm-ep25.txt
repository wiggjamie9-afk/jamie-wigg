Welcome to Sleep and Calm. Let the day step back now. This time is yours.

Tonight we are going somewhere unusual — into the world of dreams. Specifically, into the stage of sleep where most dreaming happens: REM sleep.

REM stands for rapid eye movement, named for the way your eyes move beneath closed lids during this phase. It is one of the more mysterious and fascinating aspects of human sleep, and scientists have been studying it for decades with a sense of genuine wonder.

REM sleep typically arrives about ninety minutes after you first fall asleep, and it comes in cycles throughout the night. The first REM period might last only ten or fifteen minutes. But as the night progresses, each REM cycle grows longer. By the time you are in the last hour or two of sleep, REM can stretch to forty-five minutes or more. This is why the dreams you remember most vividly are often the ones you were having just before waking.

During REM sleep, your brain is remarkably active — almost as active as when you are awake. Your breathing becomes irregular. Your heart rate varies. And your muscles are temporarily paralysed, which researchers believe is a protective mechanism: your body is prevented from acting out the movements of your dreams.

What is REM doing for you while you dream? The science suggests a great deal.

REM sleep appears to play an important role in emotional processing. While you sleep and dream, your brain revisits experiences from the day — particularly emotionally charged ones — and processes them in a way that reduces their intensity. You wake having metabolised something that might otherwise stay sharp and unresolved. There is a reason people say that things often look better in the morning. In many cases, they literally do.

REM sleep also supports memory consolidation, creativity, and the brain's ability to make unexpected connections between ideas. Many people report waking from vivid dreams with a new perspective on a problem, or an idea that had not occurred to them before.

To protect your REM sleep, protect the second half of your night. REM is concentrated in the later hours, so cutting your sleep short — even by one hour — can significantly reduce the amount of REM you receive. Alcohol also suppresses REM, as does cannabis, even if those substances seem to help you fall asleep initially.

If you want to dream more, the most reliable path is simply to sleep enough and to sleep consistently. A body that trusts its sleep routine sinks into REM with ease.

Dream well tonight. Your sleeping mind is doing important work.

Sleep well.
