# PODCAST #01: TRUE CRIME BRIEF — Episode 2

This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a man who hid in plain sight for thirty years. A church president. A Cub Scout leader. A married father of two, with a steady job and a quiet house on a quiet street in Wichita, Kansas. The kind of man you'd ask to keep an eye on your house while you were away.

His neighbors knew him as Dennis. The city of Wichita knew him by another name. A name he gave himself, in letters he mailed to the police and the press. Three letters. B. T. K.

Bind. Torture. Kill.

That was his signature. That was his promise. And for three decades, he kept it.

It began in January nineteen seventy-four. The Otero family — a father, a mother, and their children — were killed in their own home in the middle of the morning. There was no forced robbery that made sense, no clear motive. The scene was deliberate. Methodical. The work of someone who had planned it, who had taken his time. Wichita had never seen anything like it.

And then the killer did something that would define him. He wanted credit. Later that year, he placed a letter in a library book and directed police to find it. In it, he described the murders in detail only the killer could know. And he named himself. He asked, almost casually, what he should be called. He offered some options. One of them stuck. BTK.

This is the thing that made BTK different. He was not a man who killed in the shadows and vanished. He was a man who needed an audience. He taunted. He wrote letters. He sent poems. He mailed packages with drawings, with chilling little chapters describing what he had done and what he planned to do. He played a game with the police and with the whole terrified city — and for a very long time, he was winning.

He killed through the nineteen-seventies. He killed into the early eighties. Women, in their homes, the same signature each time. And then — he went quiet. The letters stopped. The killings, as far as anyone knew, stopped. The seventies became the eighties, the eighties became the nineties. A generation of Wichita children grew up hearing the name BTK the way you'd hear about a ghost. A story to frighten you. A case that had gone cold and stayed cold.

Most people assumed he was dead. Or in prison for something else. Or simply gone.

He wasn't. He was still there. Still in Wichita. Living his quiet life, going to church, walking his dog. Waiting.

And then, in two thousand and four, after thirty years of silence, BTK did the one thing no one expected. He started writing again.

Why? The best answer anyone has is the simplest and the most unsettling. He was being forgotten. A local journalist had written about the thirtieth anniversary of the murders. There was talk of a book. And the man who had always needed an audience could not stand to be a footnote. His ego pulled him out of hiding. He wanted the spotlight back.

So the letters came again. And the city's blood ran cold, because everyone understood what it meant. He was alive. He was here. And he was talking.

But here is where the story turns. Because the world of two thousand and four was not the world of nineteen seventy-four. And BTK — careful, methodical, patient BTK — did not fully understand that.

In his new letters, he began asking the police a question. He wanted to know: if he sent them a computer floppy disk, could it be traced back to him? Could they find him through it?

The police, through the media, told him no. It was a calculated answer. It was, to put it plainly, a lie.

And he believed it.

In February two thousand and five, BTK mailed a purple floppy disk to a local television station. He thought it was safe. He thought he had asked the right question and gotten an honest answer.

Investigators put that disk into a computer. And on it, in the deleted data that every computer quietly keeps, they found something he never meant to send. A file. And attached to that file was information that pointed to a name — Dennis — and a place. A local church.

It took them very little time after that. They looked at the church. They found a man named Dennis Rader, the president of the congregation. They pulled a DNA sample connected to his family, compared it to evidence the killer had left decades earlier, and it matched.

The man who had outwitted the police for thirty years was undone by a single floppy disk and his own need to be heard.

When they arrested him, Dennis Rader did not put up a fight. And in court, he did something almost more disturbing than the silence of all those years. He calmly, flatly, described what he had done. No emotion. No remorse you could find. He talked about his victims as projects. He explained his methods like a man explaining a hobby. The mask was gone, and there was nothing behind it but cold machinery.

He was sentenced to ten consecutive life terms. He will die in prison.

Here is what stays with me about BTK. We tell ourselves that monsters look like monsters. That evil announces itself. That we would know. Dennis Rader was a husband. A father. A churchgoer. A man with a city job who enforced code violations — who, in a grim irony, spent his days knocking on neighbors' doors about their dogs and their lawns. He coached. He volunteered. He was, by every outward measure, ordinary.

And the very thing that made him feel invincible — his need to be seen, to be named, to be remembered — was the thread that, when finally pulled, unraveled everything.

He wanted an audience. In the end, the audience is how they caught him.

This has been True Crime Brief.
