Hello, this is Glinda, and you're listening to Sports Unfiltered. Today we're picking a fight with the future. Or maybe defending it. Technology in officiating. Replay reviews, goal-line sensors, automated line calls, the whole machine. Some of you think it saved the game. Some of you think it ruined it. And I want to walk right down the middle of that argument and make both sides uncomfortable.

Let's start with the obvious. The whole point of technology in officiating is fairness. For a hundred years, sport ran on the human eye. One person, often standing in the wrong spot, making a split-second call on something that happened in a fraction of a second, sometimes the most important call of the entire season. And they got it wrong. Of course they got it wrong. They were human. There were games decided, championships handed out, careers altered, all on a mistake that everyone watching at home could see in slow motion three seconds later. That was maddening. So we built machines to fix it. And on that narrow goal, it works. The right team scores. The ball was in or it was out. We finally know.

But here's where it gets complicated, and this is the part the fairness crowd doesn't like to hear. We didn't just remove the errors. We removed something else too. We removed the flow. We removed the certainty of the moment. Think about what a goal used to be. The ball hits the net and the entire stadium erupts, a pure, instant, total explosion of joy. Now? The ball hits the net and forty thousand people pause. They half-celebrate. They look at the screen. They wait. They hold their breath for ninety seconds while someone in a booth draws lines on a freeze frame. And the goal might get taken away by a toenail. We made the game more correct, but we drained some of the magic right out of it.

And there's a deeper problem nobody saw coming. We thought technology would give us certainty. Black and white. In or out. Goal or no goal. And sometimes it does. But often it just moves the argument to a new place. Now we argue about where exactly a player's body parts were. We argue about whether a hand was in a natural position. We argue about whether the camera angle was good enough. We were promised the end of debate, and instead we got a new, more bitter, more technical kind of debate. The machine didn't kill the argument. It just gave the argument a magnifying glass.

So let me lay out the real tension here, because it's not actually about cameras. It's about what we want from sport in the first place.

If you want sport to be purely fair, a clean test where the better team always gets the result they earned, then technology is your best friend. Use all of it. Every angle, every sensor, every review. Get it right no matter how long it takes.

But if you believe sport is theatre, a living, breathing, emotional drama, then you have to admit that part of the magic was always the human mess. The bad call you raged about for twenty years. The injustice that became part of the legend. The fact that it wasn't perfect was part of why it felt so real.

Now here's my take. I think technology in officiating is right, but we've used it wrong. We reached for it to settle absolutely everything, and in doing so we forgot that some calls are meant to be close, and some moments are meant to be felt before they're verified. Use the machine for the clear, season-defining mistakes. The ball that was obviously over the line. The clear and obvious error. But stop using it to slice toenails and freeze every celebration. We chased perfection so hard we forgot we were supposed to be having fun.

Because here's the thing we can never get back. A sport that is one hundred percent correct and zero percent alive is not the sport we fell in love with. The goal was never to build a perfect machine. The goal was to play a beautiful game. That's it from me. This has been Sports Unfiltered. Now go argue.
