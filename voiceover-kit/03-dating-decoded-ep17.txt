Welcome back to Dating Decoded, the show where we take the messy, confusing world of modern love and look at what's actually going on underneath. I'm your host. Today we're talking about first dates. That strange, exciting, slightly nerve-wracking window of time when you're trying to figure out if a person is who you think they might be — while they're doing exactly the same thing to you.

First dates get a lot of overthinking. The outfit, the venue, the opener, the questions, what to order, whether to split the bill, when to leave. And then underneath all of that, the bigger question that nobody says out loud but everyone is feeling: do they like me? Which often quietly becomes: am I likeable enough?

Let's start by dismantling something. The first date is not an audition. I know it can feel like one. But the audition frame is actually the thing that makes first dates so stiff and so exhausting. When you walk in thinking I have to impress this person, you are already putting yourself in the worst possible position, because you're performing rather than connecting. And people can feel the difference. A performance might be interesting for a moment. A genuine person is magnetic.

The mindset shift that makes first dates easier is this: go in curious, not impressive. Your job isn't to convince them. Your job is to find out if you actually like them. These are wildly different modes, and the second one is so much more useful. When you're curious about a person rather than anxious about their verdict of you, you ask better questions, you listen more, and paradoxically you come across as far more appealing. Because genuine curiosity is one of the most attractive things a person can offer someone.

So — what should you actually talk about? The answer is: go deeper than the surface sooner than you think. Most people stick to safe small talk on first dates. What do you do, where are you from, have you been here before. These things are fine as warm-up, but they don't tell you much about a person, and they don't create the kind of connection that makes you think about someone afterwards. The conversations people remember are the ones where they said something honest or unexpected, where the other person surprised them, where both of them laughed about something real.

You can get there faster than you think without it feeling forced. Instead of what do you do for work, try what does a really good week look like for you? Instead of where are you from, try what's something about where you grew up that still shows up in who you are? These aren't interview questions — they're invitations. And a person who responds well to them, who leans in rather than deflecting, is telling you something important about who they are.

Now let's talk about nerves, because they're going to be there. The trick is not to try to eliminate them — you can't. The trick is to remember that the other person is almost certainly feeling them too. Most people, no matter how cool they seem, are at least a little nervous on a first date. They want to be liked as much as you do. Remembering that you're both just two humans trying to connect across a table can take a lot of the pressure off. It's not a test. It's a shared attempt.

Presence matters more than polish. Being on your phone, even once, even just for a second to check something you could easily check later, sends a message you probably don't mean to send. Eye contact, putting your whole attention on the person in front of you, remembering what they said five minutes ago and coming back to it — these are the small things that make a person feel like you are actually there with them. And in a world of constant distraction, that feeling is rarer than you'd think.

What about the things people actually worry about? The awkward silence. The bill. The end of the date. Here's my honest take on each.

Awkward silences on a first date are almost always survivable. A pause doesn't mean the date is going badly. Sometimes it just means you've both arrived at a natural resting point, and the next thought hasn't formed yet. You don't need to fill every second. A comfortable silence shared by two people who are relaxed with each other is actually a good sign. Let it breathe.

The bill. There's no universal rule here anymore, and anyone who tells you there is, is telling you their preference, not a law. What matters is that you've thought about it beforehand so you're not fumbling awkwardly when it arrives. Whatever approach you take, clarity and confidence in how you handle it is far more attractive than the amount paid.

The goodbye. The worst thing you can do is oversell plans you're not certain about. Don't say we should definitely do this again if you're genuinely not sure. Don't manufacture urgency. If you liked them, say so simply: I had a really good time. If you want to see them again, reach out. Not in three days to prove some kind of point. Just when you actually feel like it.

One more thing. First dates occasionally go badly. The person is nothing like what you hoped, or the chemistry is completely flat, or they say something that makes you think no thank you very much. This is not a personal failure. It's information. Being able to say that wasn't it, and leaving without making either person wrong, is its own skill and a very useful one.

The right person for you exists. A bad first date isn't evidence against that — it's just part of the process. Show up, be present, be genuinely curious, and trust that the people worth knowing will recognise you without you having to perform.
