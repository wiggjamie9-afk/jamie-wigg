Welcome back to Sleep and Calm. I am glad you are here.

Tonight we are talking about sleep hygiene — not in a clinical or complicated way, but in the gentlest sense of the word. The small rituals and quiet choices that tell your body and your mind that the day is done, and that rest is allowed.

Sleep hygiene is simply the collection of habits that surround your sleep. And when those habits are aligned, falling asleep feels natural rather than forced.

Let us begin with light.

Your brain reads light as a signal. Bright light says it is daytime — be alert, be ready, stay awake. Dim, warm light says something different. It says the sun is going down. It says it is safe to slow down. So in the hour or two before bed, try to soften the lighting around you. Lower the lamps. Move away from screens where you can, or at the very least, reduce their brightness.

This one change — shifting from bright white light to warm, low light — begins to ease the release of melatonin, the hormone that draws you toward sleep. Your body already knows how to do this. You are simply giving it permission.

Next, consider temperature.

Your body temperature naturally drops as you fall asleep. A cooler room — somewhere between sixteen and nineteen degrees celsius — supports this process. If your room feels warm, try opening a window slightly, or sleeping with lighter bedding. A cool pillow, cool feet, a light sheet rather than a heavy duvet. Small adjustments that your nervous system will thank you for.

Then there is the question of consistency.

Your brain loves rhythm. When you go to sleep and wake at roughly the same time each day — even on weekends — your body begins to anticipate rest. It starts preparing for sleep before you even climb into bed. Your heart rate begins to slow. Your muscles begin to soften. You feel the pull of tiredness not as a struggle but as a natural tide.

If your schedule varies a lot right now, that is all right. You do not need to change everything tonight. Just notice. Just observe where your current patterns sit, without judgment.

One more thing before we close: the hour before bed.

This is precious time. Not for tasks, not for problem-solving, not for catching up on messages. This hour belongs to your body. Use it for something that asks nothing of you — gentle reading, a warm drink, soft music, a slow walk through your home turning off lights one by one.

Your nervous system will recognise this as a winding down. And it will begin to meet you there.

You are allowed to rest. You have earned this quiet.

Let your breath slow now. Let your shoulders drop. Let the evening hold you gently as you move toward sleep.

Sleep well.
