In the summer of the year fifteen eighteen, in the city of Strasbourg, a woman stepped into the street and began to dance. There was no music. There was no festival. There was no reason that anyone could see. She simply danced, alone, in the heat of the day, her arms moving, her feet turning, her face wet with sweat and fixed in something that did not look like joy. She danced through the afternoon. She danced into the evening. And when at last she fell, exhausted, she rose again and kept dancing. Within a week, thirty more people had joined her. Within a month, there were hundreds. And some of them would dance until they died.

Welcome to History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, the strange and terrible story of the dancing plague.

To believe this story, you have to know that it is not a legend. It is one of the best documented events of its kind in all of history. We have the records of the Strasbourg city council. We have the notes of physicians who were there. We have sermons, and chronicles, and the accounts of men who watched it happen with their own eyes and wrote down what they saw in growing alarm. The woman who began it was named, in the records, as Frau Troffea. She danced outside her house, and her neighbours gathered to watch, and at first they may have laughed, the way people laugh at something they do not understand. But the laughter did not last.

Because she would not stop. Hour after hour, her body kept moving, as though it no longer belonged to her. By the fourth day she was still going, her shoes worn through, her feet bruised and bleeding on the cobblestones. And then, one by one, others began to join her. A second dancer. A third. By the end of the first week, more than thirty people in Strasbourg were dancing in the streets, helplessly, ceaselessly, with no music and no will of their own.

The authorities did not know what to do. And here is where the story becomes almost unbelievable. The town's physicians were consulted, and they concluded that the dancing was caused by what they called hot blood, an overheating of the body, and that the only cure was for the afflicted to dance it out of their system. So the council, acting on this medical advice, decided to help them dance. They cleared two guild halls. They set up a wooden stage in the open air. They even hired musicians, pipers and drummers, to play for the dancers, and they brought in strong, healthy men to dance alongside them and keep them moving.

It was the worst possible decision. Instead of curing the dancers, it gave them an arena, and it drew in more victims. The numbers swelled. By some accounts, at the height of it, as many as four hundred people were dancing in the streets and halls of Strasbourg. And they were not dancing for joy. Witnesses described faces twisted in fear and exhaustion. People cried out for help even as their legs kept moving. They danced until they collapsed from heat, from stroke, from sheer physical ruin. The chroniclers wrote that some of them died, dropping dead in the street from heart attacks and exhaustion, dancing themselves into the grave.

The authorities, finally realising their mistake, reversed course completely. They banned music in the city. They forbade public dancing of any kind. And at last, in a kind of desperation, they turned to faith. The remaining dancers were loaded onto wagons and carried out of the city to a shrine in the mountains, dedicated to Saint Vitus, a saint long associated with such afflictions. There, before the altar, the survivors were given small wooden crosses and red shoes, and prayers were said over them. And slowly, over the following weeks, the dancing faded. The plague that had gripped Strasbourg simply let go.

So what was it? That is the question that has haunted this story for five hundred years, and the honest answer is that no one is certain. For a long time the favourite explanation was poison. A fungus called ergot, which grows on damp rye, can contaminate bread, and it produces compounds related to the drug L S D. Ergot poisoning can cause hallucinations and violent convulsions. But there is a problem with that theory. Ergot also cuts off blood flow to the limbs, often causing such agony that victims cannot walk at all, let alone dance for days on end. It is hard to see how poisoned people could have danced as these people danced.

The explanation that many historians now favour is stranger, and in some ways more unsettling. They believe it was a case of what is called mass psychogenic illness, a kind of contagious affliction that spreads not through the body but through the mind. Strasbourg in fifteen eighteen was a city under enormous strain. There had been years of failed harvests and crushing famine. Disease was everywhere. The poor were starving in the streets. And the people lived in a world saturated with religious fear, where they genuinely believed that Saint Vitus could strike a sinner down with an uncontrollable, cursed dance. Under that weight of hunger, dread, and belief, the human mind can do extraordinary things. One person broke, and began to dance. And in a community primed to believe such a thing could happen, it spread from mind to mind like a fever, until hundreds were caught in it.

What makes the dancing plague so haunting is that it was real, and it was witnessed, and it cannot be entirely explained away. These were not actors. They did not want to dance. Many of them begged for it to stop. And yet their bodies carried them on, in front of crowds, in the open street, until some of them died. It is a window into how fragile the line can be between the order of a society and its sudden, total unravelling.

So what does the dancing plague teach us? Perhaps that the human mind is far stranger and far more powerful than we like to admit, and that it does not act alone. We are not islands. We catch things from one another, not only diseases of the body but moods, fears, and beliefs, and under enough pressure a whole community can be swept into something none of its members chose. We like to think we are rational, separate, in control. But the dancers of Strasbourg remind us that we are far more open to one another than that, and that the things which move a crowd can move our own feet before we even know it. They danced, five hundred years ago, until they could dance no more. And we still do not fully know why.
