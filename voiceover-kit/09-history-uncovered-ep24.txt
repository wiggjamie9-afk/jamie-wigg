In the year twelve sixty-eight, the Mongol Empire was the largest contiguous land empire in human history. It stretched from the Pacific coast of China to the doorstep of Poland. Its postal relay system, called the Yam, could carry a message from one end of that empire to the other in two weeks — a distance of more than five thousand miles.

And almost no one alive today could name its capital city.

Karakorum. That is where it was. A city of silk, gold, and scholars built in the middle of the Mongolian steppe by Ogedei Khan, son of Genghis, in twelve thirty-five.

Karakorum was not a myth. It was a real, functioning city with embassies from China, Persia, Russia, and Europe all operating simultaneously. A French goldsmith named Guillaume Boucher built a mechanical silver tree in its great hall that dispensed wine, koumiss, and other drinks from different spouts. A Flemish friar named William of Rubruck visited in twelve fifty-four and wrote a detailed account that survives to this day. He described a city with a Nestorian Christian church, a mosque, a Buddhist temple, and a neighborhood of Chinese artisans, all within walking distance of each other.

This is the forgotten story of the Mongol Empire: it was not only one of the most brutal military forces in history. It was also, for a brief window, one of the most sophisticated multicultural administrative systems the world had ever produced.

The Pax Mongolica — the Mongol Peace — lasted from roughly twelve forty to thirteen fifty. During this period, the trade routes connecting China to Europe, now commonly called the Silk Road, were safer and more well-maintained than at almost any point before or since. Merchants, diplomats, scholars, and pilgrims could travel thousands of miles with relative security under the protection of Mongol law.

Marco Polo made his famous journey during this period. So did Ibn Battuta. So did the Franciscan friar Giovanni da Pian del Carpine, who was sent by Pope Innocent the Fourth to negotiate with the Mongols in twelve forty-five.

The goods that moved along these routes were not just silk and spice. Ideas traveled too. Mathematical knowledge from the Islamic world flowed into China. Chinese printing techniques moved westward. Persian astronomers worked alongside Chinese astronomers in Mongol observatories. The Black Death, tragically, also moved along these routes — arguably the most consequential single event of the fourteenth century, carried from Central Asia to Europe by the very infrastructure the Mongols had built.

Here is the haunting detail: the Mongol Empire did not fall slowly. It did not gradually decline over centuries the way Rome did. It collapsed quickly, torn apart by civil war among Genghis Khan's descendants, by the Black Death that devastated its populations, and by the inability of any single ruler to hold together a territory that defied administration by its sheer scale.

By fourteen hundred, the great unified empire was gone, fractured into successor khanates that spent the next two centuries fighting each other.

Karakorum was abandoned and eventually dismantled. The stones from its temples and palaces were used to build a Buddhist monastery called Erdene Zuu in the sixteenth century, which still stands near the site today. Walk its grounds and you are walking on the ruins of a city that once held the throne of the world.

The Mongol Empire's legacy is genuinely complicated. Its conquests killed an estimated forty million people — a figure so large it measurably affected global carbon dioxide levels by returning farmland to forest. It also created the conditions that brought the Renaissance's ideas east and China's technologies west.

History rarely offers clean moral verdicts. The Mongol Empire offers perhaps the most complicated one of all.
