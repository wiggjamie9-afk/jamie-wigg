Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we're talking about something every experienced traveller knows and almost nobody talks about openly: the hidden cost of doing too much.

I call it the itinerary trap. You've got two weeks in Southeast Asia, so you make a list. Bangkok, Chiang Mai, Luang Prabang, Hanoi, Halong Bay, Hoi An, Ho Chi Minh City. Seven destinations, two weeks. It sounds reasonable until you're on day nine, eating bad airport food at midnight for the third time, half-asleep on another night bus, and you realise you haven't actually stopped moving long enough to feel the place you're in.

The itinerary trap is one of the most common mistakes first-time travellers make, and it comes from a completely understandable place. You've worked hard for this trip. You've waited. You want value. You want to see everything. The problem is that moving fast is the very thing that prevents you from seeing anything real.

Here's a number that will change how you travel. Three. Three nights minimum in any single place before you start to feel it rather than just look at it. On night one, you are figuring out where you are. On night two, you are starting to navigate. By night three, you have a coffee place, a favourite street, a bar where the owner recognises you. You have begun to exist in the place rather than simply pass through it.

Five nights is where real discovery happens. Five nights means you've had a boring afternoon and found something extraordinary because of it. You've had dinner with someone you met on day two. You've gone back to the market you liked and they remember your face. You've walked a street you missed on the first walk through. Five nights is where travel becomes experience rather than consumption.

So how do you choose where to spend that time? Not always the most famous place. Often the second-tier cities and towns, the ones that don't appear on every itinerary, are where you will have the most extraordinary time. Let me give you some examples.

In Mexico, everyone goes to Mexico City and Oaxaca. Both are wonderful. But the town of San Cristobal de las Casas in Chiapas, at two thousand metres elevation, with its cobblestone streets, indigenous Tzotzil culture, cathedral-lit evenings, and remarkable food scene, is one of the most alive and distinctive cities in the country. Most tourists never make it there.

In Morocco, everyone goes to Marrakech. The blue city of Chefchaouen gets some traffic now, but the ancient Roman ruins of Volubilis and the town of Meknes beside them are barely on the tourist radar despite being extraordinary. Walk those columns at golden hour and you will have them nearly to yourself.

In Portugal, Lisbon and Porto dominate the conversation. Entirely justifiably. But the Alentejo region, a few hours inland, offers cork forests, white hilltop villages, and some of the best wine in Europe at a fraction of the coastal prices. The town of Evora has a Roman temple standing in its centre. It is a UNESCO World Heritage city that most people couldn't point to on a map.

These second-tier places share a few qualities. Lower prices. Fewer tourists. A local population that hasn't been worn thin by the industry of hospitality. Accommodation that hasn't been bought up by international chains. And a slightly slower, more authentic rhythm that reminds you why you came here in the first place.

The practical guide to slowing down. Before your next trip, halve the number of cities on your list and double your time in each one. Choose one place that isn't on the standard itinerary for that country, and give it your longest stay. On at least two days of your trip, make no plans whatsoever before ten in the morning, and see what happens.

Here is the thing about travel that nobody tells you in the brochures. The best stories never happen on schedule. They happen in the gaps. In the free afternoon you didn't plan. On the walk where you took the wrong turn. In the conversation that started because you sat at the same table for three mornings in a row. You cannot schedule these things. You can only create the conditions for them by being in one place long enough for the world to find you.

Slow down. You will see more.
