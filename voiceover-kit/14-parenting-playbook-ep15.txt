Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. Today's episode is a little different, because it's not really about your child. It's about you. I want you to picture the moment. It's been a long day. You've asked nicely three times. The milk is now on the floor, the shoes still aren't on, and something inside you just snaps. Your voice goes loud and sharp in a way you don't like. And a few minutes later, once the heat has passed, comes the wave you know so well. The guilt. The quiet thought, I'm meant to be the grown-up here. Today, we're talking about managing your own anger, because the calm we keep asking our children to find has to start somewhere, and that somewhere is us.

Let me say the most important thing first, and please take it in fully. Feeling angry at your child does not make you a bad parent. Anger is a normal human emotion, and parenting is one of the most provoking, exhausting, relentless jobs there is. Every loving parent loses their temper sometimes. The goal of today is not to become a parent who never feels anger, because that parent does not exist. The goal is to handle the anger in a way you can be at peace with, more of the time.

So let's understand what's really going on. When your child pushes you to the edge, the very same thing happens in your brain that happens in theirs during a meltdown. The alarm part takes over, and the thinking part goes quiet. That hot, automatic surge, the loud voice, the words you regret, that's your own brain flipping into survival mode. Knowing this changes everything, because it means your job in that moment is the same job you do for your child. First, calm the alarm. Only then can the wise, patient parent come back online.

Here are some principles that hold up.

One. Learn your own early warning signs. Anger rarely comes from nowhere. There's usually a build-up, the tight chest, the clenched jaw, the rising voice, the gritted teeth. The earlier you notice the climb, the more choices you have. By the time you're shouting, the moment has already won. Catch it lower down the hill.

Two. Pause before you act. The oldest advice in the book, and still the best. When you feel the surge, buy yourself a few seconds. One slow breath. A step back. Even just closing your mouth for a moment. That tiny gap is where your thinking brain gets a chance to come back and take the wheel.

Three. It's okay to step away. If you're close to losing it, and your child is safe, it is completely fine to say, I need a minute, and step into another room to breathe. This isn't abandoning them. You're showing them, in real time, what a person does with a big feeling. That's a powerful lesson, taught without a single word.

Four. Lower your inputs where you can. So much parental anger is really exhaustion, hunger, noise, and being stretched too thin, all wearing the costume of anger. You will have a far longer fuse when you're a little less depleted. Looking after your own basic needs is not selfish. It's part of the job. You cannot pour calm from an empty cup.

Five. Repair afterwards. This one matters enormously, so please don't skip it. When you do lose your temper, and you will, go back and mend it. A simple, honest, I'm sorry I shouted. I was frustrated, but it wasn't okay to raise my voice like that. This does not undermine you. It teaches your child that everyone makes mistakes, that relationships can be repaired, and that big feelings don't have to break the bond. The repair can matter as much as the rupture.

Let me give you a few scenarios.

Scenario one. The slow-burn day, where small things pile up until you finally erupt over something tiny. Here, the work happens before the explosion. Notice your simmer earlier. When you feel yourself thinning out, that's the signal to lower the inputs, take a breather if you can, and tend to your own tank before it runs dry.

Scenario two. The sudden flash, where something happens and you've shouted almost before you knew it. Be gentle with yourself, that surge is fast and very human. The work here is the repair. Go back, name what happened simply, apologise for the manner not the limit, and reconnect.

Scenario three. The shame spiral afterwards, where you replay it and decide you're failing. This is where so many loving parents get stuck. Try to treat yourself the way you'd treat a good friend. You had a hard moment. You're not a bad parent. You're a tired human being doing a very hard job, and you came back to put it right.

Now, the bigger picture. Your calm is not just for your own peace of mind. Remember, children learn to manage their feelings by borrowing ours. Every time you handle your own anger with a pause, a breath, a step away, and a repair, you're showing your child exactly how it's done. You are the most important lesson on emotion they will ever watch. That's a lot of pressure, I know, so let me soften it. You don't have to do it perfectly. You just have to keep coming back and trying, and letting them see the trying.

And here's something I really want to leave you with. Be as patient and forgiving with yourself as you're learning to be with your child. The same grace, the same understanding, the same belief that a hard moment is not the whole story. You deserve that too.

One important note on wellbeing. If your anger feels frequent, frightening, hard to control, or if you're ever worried about how you might act in those moments, please don't carry that alone. Talk to your doctor or health visitor. Reaching out for support is not a sign of failure. It is one of the most responsible and loving things a parent can do, for themselves and for their child.

So here's where I'd begin. This week, just start noticing your own early warning signs, the moment the heat begins to climb. You don't have to fix anything yet. Simply catch it sooner. And when you do lose your temper, because you will, practise the repair. Come back, apologise for the how, and reconnect.

You're not aiming to become a parent who never gets angry. You're becoming a parent who knows what to do with the anger when it comes, and who models, in the most ordinary moments, the very calm you're hoping to grow in your child.
