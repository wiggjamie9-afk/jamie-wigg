This is History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, the story of a woman who taught the world to see colour — and whose name almost no one knows.

Her name was Eunice Newton Foote. She was born in eighteen nineteen in Connecticut, in the United States. She raised children, managed a household, and she did science. Not professionally. There was no professional path for a woman scientist in America in the eighteen fifties. She did it the way that curious people throughout history have always done it when the formal world has no place for them. She did it anyway.

In the summer of eighteen fifty-six she conducted a series of experiments using glass cylinders, air pumps, and sunlight. She filled different cylinders with different gases and placed them in the sun, and she measured how hot each cylinder became, and how long it held that heat. And in one of those cylinders, filled with carbon dioxide — which she called carbonic acid gas — she noticed something that would eventually shake the foundations of our understanding of the planet we live on. The cylinder filled with carbon dioxide got hotter than all the others. Much hotter. And it cooled down far more slowly when taken out of the sun.

She wrote it up. She submitted a paper. The paper was read at the American Association for the Advancement of Science in August of eighteen fifty-six. She was one of the first women ever to have research presented before that body. But she could not present it herself, because women were not permitted to present their own work. A male scientist — a professor named Joseph Henry — read her paper aloud while she sat and listened to her own words in someone else's voice.

Her conclusion, written plainly in that paper, was this. An atmosphere of carbonic acid gas would give our earth a high temperature. In plain English: if there is more carbon dioxide in the air, the planet gets warmer.

She wrote those words in eighteen fifty-six.

Three years later, in eighteen fifty-nine, an Irish physicist named John Tyndall conducted a more elaborate set of experiments and reached essentially the same conclusion. Tyndall published extensively, corresponded with the great scientists of the age, and is widely credited in the history of science as the person who discovered the greenhouse effect. His work is cited in almost every account of how we came to understand climate science.

Foote's paper — published three years before his, in a scientific journal, with the core idea intact — was forgotten almost immediately. It was not rediscovered until twenty fifteen, by a retired petroleum geologist who came across it while researching something else entirely. One hundred and fifty-nine years after she wrote it.

Why was it forgotten? The reasons are not hard to find. She was a woman at a time when women were not taken seriously as scientists. She had no university position, no professional network, no chain of colleagues who would cite her and keep her name alive. She was a footnote to her own discovery because the world of science in her era had decided, before she ever sat down to conduct an experiment, that her kind of person could not produce knowledge worth remembering.

What she actually produced was the first recorded identification of the greenhouse effect of carbon dioxide. It took the world more than a century and a half to notice.

We are still, in every meaningful sense, living with the consequences of what she described. The planet is warmer. The carbon dioxide is higher. And the woman who first wrote it down died in eighteen eighty-eight, largely unremembered, her paper sitting undisturbed in an archive for a century and a half, waiting for someone to find it.

The history of science is full of Eunice Newton Footes. People who looked carefully, thought clearly, wrote it down — and then were quietly set aside. Knowing her name is, in a small way, a form of repair.
