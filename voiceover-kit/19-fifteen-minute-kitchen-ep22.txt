Welcome to fifteen minute kitchen. Today we are going international — and I mean properly international, not just adding a packet of taco seasoning. We are making a classic Japanese dish called oyakodon, which translates to parent and child bowl, because it uses both chicken and egg together. It is one of the most comforting, deeply flavourful meals you can make in under fifteen minutes, and once you understand it, the whole category of Japanese rice bowls opens up to you.

Here is your grab list. Two chicken thighs — boneless, skinless, and thighs over breast here because the flavour is so much better and they stay juicy. Three eggs. One medium onion. Cooked Japanese-style short-grain rice, or any white rice you have ready — this is the one part that needs to be done ahead, so get that on first. For the sauce: two tablespoons of soy sauce, two tablespoons of mirin, one tablespoon of sake or dry white wine, and one tablespoon of sugar. Two hundred millilitres of dashi stock if you have it, or light chicken stock. That is the whole list.

Start by mixing your sauce. Into a small bowl: the soy sauce, mirin, sake, and sugar. Stir until the sugar dissolves. Set it aside.

Slice the onion thinly — half-moon slices — and cut your chicken into bite-size pieces, roughly two centimetres. Beat your eggs just lightly in a bowl, not until completely uniform — you want some threads of white and yolk because that is what gives oyakodon its characteristic look and texture when it sets in the pan.

Into a wide shallow pan over medium heat, pour in your stock and then add your sauce mixture. When it comes to a simmer, add the onion. Cook for about two minutes until the onion starts to soften. Then add your chicken in a single layer. Do not stir it around — let it simmer gently in that flavourful broth for about three minutes until the chicken is just cooked through.

Now the most important moment. Raise the heat slightly to get the broth bubbling steadily, then pour your beaten eggs over everything in a slow, even spiral from the outside in. Do not stir. Put a lid on the pan and cook for just sixty to ninety seconds. You want the egg to be just set on the outside but still glossy and soft in the centre — almost custardy. This is the texture you are aiming for.

Slide the whole thing directly over a bowl of steamed rice. Every last drop of that broth goes on too, because it soaks into the rice and becomes part of the meal. Scatter over some thinly sliced spring onion if you have it.

The technique here is all about gentle heat and barely set egg. Overcooked egg in oyakodon goes rubbery and the whole dish falls apart. You want to pull it off the heat while it still looks slightly underdone — the residual heat and the hot broth finish the job.

This is the kind of dish that sounds like it should take hours, but once you know the sauce ratio and the egg trick, you can make it on a Tuesday night in the time it takes for the rice to steam. The full recipe with the sauce ratios and tips on finding mirin and dashi are in the show notes.
