It begins, as these stories so often do, on a quiet road in the dark. A young couple, parked near a lover's lane just outside Vallejo, California. It is late December, nineteen sixty-eight. They are barely out of their teens. And in the space of a few minutes, a man none of them ever saw clearly steps out of the night, fires without warning, and walks away. No motive. No connection to the victims. No reason that anyone could ever find. By morning, two young people are dead, and a shadow has fallen across Northern California that will not lift for more than half a century. Because this killer would not stay quiet. He wanted us to know he was out there. He wanted us to be afraid. And he gave himself a name. The Zodiac.

Welcome back to the True Crime Brief. I'm your host. In our first two episodes, we traced the long shadow of the Golden State Killer, and we sat with the chilling, methodical evil of Dennis Rader, the man who called himself B T K. Both of those men were eventually unmasked. Both finally had a name, a face, a confession. Today's story has none of that. Today we enter the one case that has defied everything: police work, technology, and almost sixty years of obsession. The Zodiac was never caught. The Zodiac was never identified. And to this day, we do not know who he was.

Let me take you back to the landscape of it. The late nineteen sixties in the San Francisco Bay Area. A time of enormous cultural change, of music and protest and a sense that the old rules were dissolving. And into that moment steps something old and dark and patient. A predator who chose victims almost at random. Couples in cars. People who simply had the misfortune to be in a lonely place at the wrong hour.

That first confirmed attack near Vallejo took the lives of two teenagers. Then, about six months later, in July of nineteen sixty-nine, another couple was attacked in a parking area not far away. One of them survived. He survived to describe the man, and to describe something that would become the killer's signature: the cold, almost businesslike manner in which he approached the car, raised a flashlight and a gun, and opened fire. The man who lived would carry that night for the rest of his life.

But it was what came after that attack that transformed the Zodiac from a local horror into a national legend. Because shortly afterward, three Bay Area newspapers each received a letter. Inside each envelope was a portion of a coded message. A cipher. Three parts that, the writer claimed, when assembled and solved, would reveal his identity. He demanded the newspapers print them on the front page, or he would go on a killing spree. The papers, working with police, printed them.

And then something remarkable happened. The cipher was not solved by the F B I. It was not cracked by a master codebreaker in a government laboratory. It was solved, within about a week, by a high school teacher and his wife working at their kitchen table. A schoolteacher named Donald Harden and his wife Bettye. They broke the code. And the message it contained was not a name. It was a manifesto of cruelty. The writer described how he killed people for pleasure, and how he believed his victims would become his slaves in some afterlife. He had promised to reveal who he was. He had lied.

It was in a later letter that the killer signed himself with the name that would define the case. He called himself the Zodiac, and he drew a symbol: a circle crossed by lines, like the crosshairs of a gun sight. That symbol became one of the most recognizable images in the history of American crime.

What made the Zodiac so uniquely terrifying was not just the murders, awful as they were. It was the performance. He wrote letter after letter to the newspapers, taunting the police, mocking their failures, claiming credit for killings, threatening to blow up school buses full of children. He sent pieces of a victim's bloodstained shirt to prove he was who he said he was. He created a public spectacle of fear, and he fed on the attention. In one letter he wrote that he liked killing because it was so much fun. He seemed to want, more than anything, to be watched.

The confirmed attacks that police firmly attribute to him left five people dead and at least two survivors. But in his letters, the Zodiac boasted of far more victims, claiming numbers that climbed into the dozens. Investigators have never been able to confirm those larger claims, and many believe they were lies, more theater designed to amplify the terror. We may never know the true number. That uncertainty is part of the wound this case left behind.

One of the most haunting episodes came in the fall of nineteen sixty-nine, when a cab driver named Paul Stine was shot in San Francisco. It was a brazen killing in a residential neighborhood, and witnesses, including some teenagers across the street, actually caught a glimpse of the man. From that came the famous police sketch: a heavyset man in glasses, with short hair. That face, drawn from memory, has stared out from newspapers and documentaries for decades. And yet it has never been matched, with certainty, to any living person.

Then the attacks appeared to stop. The letters continued for a while into the early nineteen seventies, then grew sparse, and then they simply ended. The Zodiac, whoever he was, fell silent. Whether he died, was imprisoned for some other crime, moved away, or simply chose to stop, no one knows.

Over the years, investigators chased many suspects. One name in particular was pursued for decades by a man who became personally consumed by the case. But the physical evidence never delivered the certainty everyone craved. Fingerprints recovered from some scenes have never been definitively tied to a named individual. And the cruelest twist of all may be the ciphers themselves. The Zodiac sent four coded messages in total. The first was solved by that schoolteacher and his wife. But three more remained unbroken for decades. It was only in twenty twenty, more than fifty years later, that an international team of amateur codebreakers finally cracked one of the longest remaining ciphers. And even then, the message contained no name. Just more taunting. Just more of his voice, reaching out across the years, still refusing to tell us who he was.

That is the strange, enduring power of this case. We have his words. We have his symbol. We have the testimony of those who survived him and the memories of those who loved the people he took. We have decades of dedicated detectives and obsessed amateurs who have given enormous parts of their lives to the search. And still, the central question hangs open: who was he?

I want to be careful here, because at the heart of all this fascination are real people. Real young men and women whose lives were stolen on quiet roads and city streets. Their families have lived not only with grief, but with the particular agony of a case that has never closed, never delivered an answer, never allowed them the small mercy of finality. When we treat the Zodiac as a puzzle, as a legend, as a piece of folklore, we should never forget that the puzzle was built out of their loss.

So why does this case endure when so many others fade? Perhaps because it denies us the ending we expect. In most of the stories we tell on this show, there is eventually a knock on a door, a name, a courtroom, a measure of justice, however imperfect. The Zodiac gives us none of that. He remains exactly what he made himself: a question mark. A man who wanted to be famous and unknowable at the same time, and who, against every advance of science and every hour of human effort, has gotten his wish.

Somewhere, in a record we cannot read or a grave with no marker for what he did, the answer exists. But for now, the file stays open. The crosshairs symbol still stares out from the page. And the question he sealed inside those envelopes, all those years ago, has never been answered. Maybe it never will be. I'm your host, and this has been the True Crime Brief. Stay safe out there, and good night.
