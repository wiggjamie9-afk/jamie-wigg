Hey, I'm Victoria, and welcome to Pop Culture Now. Today we are talking about the thing your friend texts you about at midnight, the documentary you put on as background noise and then could not turn off, the case that suddenly everyone at work knows every detail of. True crime. How real, often terrible stories became one of the biggest forms of entertainment on the planet, and the big, slightly uncomfortable question underneath all of it. Is it okay that we love this so much?

Let's start by being honest about the size of it, because it is enormous. Whole channels, endless podcasts, hit series, packed conventions, all built around real crimes and real victims. Millions of people, many of them lovely, gentle, would-not-hurt-a-fly people, spend their evenings happily diving into the darkest things humans do to each other. And that's worth pausing on, because at first glance it seems like a contradiction. Kind people, drawn to cruelty. So what's actually going on?

Part of it is something deeply human and honestly kind of healthy. Curiosity about danger. For our entire history as a species, understanding a threat was how you survived it. The brain that wanted to know how the bad thing happened was the brain that lived to see tomorrow. So when we lean into a true crime story, part of us is doing something ancient and protective. We're studying the danger from a safe distance, learning the warning signs, asking the question we all secretly carry. Could this happen to me, and if it did, would I see it coming?

And there's a puzzle-box pleasure to it too, which is the part that makes it so easy to binge. A mystery has all the satisfying shape of a great story. A question at the start, clues in the middle, an answer at the end. Our minds adore that shape. We want the loose threads tied off. We want the why explained. True crime takes the most chaotic, senseless thing in the world and promises to make sense of it, and that promise is almost irresistible to a brain that hates a question with no answer.

But here is where we have to slow all the way down and be careful, because this is the part that matters most. These are not characters. The victim in that gripping story was a real person, with a real family, who are still alive and still grieving and might very well stumble across a stranger discussing their worst day as casual entertainment. That's the line that runs right through the middle of this whole thing. The story is fascinating. The pain is real. And the moment we forget the second part, we've turned someone's tragedy into a snack, and that should sit a little uneasily in all of us.

So how do we love this responsibly, because I do think you can. It comes down to a question of who the story is for. The best true crime treats the victim as the heart of it, not the killer. It remembers their name more than his. It's careful, it's respectful, it asks the audience to feel something real instead of just thrilling them. The worst kind flips it. It makes the person who did the harm into a kind of dark celebrity, lingers on the gore, treats real suffering like a haunted house ride. Same subject, completely different soul. One honors. The other exploits. And as the audience, our attention is the thing that decides which kind gets made.

Because that's the part we don't always realize we hold. We have power here. What we click, what we share, what we reward, that sends a message about what gets made next. If careful, humane, victim-centered storytelling is what we show up for, that's what multiplies. If we reward the lurid, the disrespectful, the killer-as-rockstar version, then that's what we'll get more of. We're not just consumers of this. We're quietly voting on what the whole genre becomes.

So here's my take to close us out. The fascination is human and it's fine. Wanting to understand the dark is not a flaw in you. But true crime asks something of us that pure fiction never does. It asks us to remember, the whole time, that this really happened to someone. So be a thoughtful one. Reach for the stories that treat real people like people, that center the lost instead of glorifying the one who did the losing. Stay curious, by all means. Just keep your heart switched on while you do it. That's the whole game right there.

That's the show. That's the deep dive. Be nice in the replies, drink some water, log off occasionally, and I will see you right back here next time on Pop Culture Now. Bye!
