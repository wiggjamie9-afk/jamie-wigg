Hello, this is Austin, and you're listening to Unexplained.

Tonight we do not cross an ocean or climb into the cold air. Tonight we open a book. A small book, about the size of a paperback, bound in limp vellum, its pages soft and brown with age. It has been read by some of the finest codebreakers of the twentieth century, by cryptographers who broke the ciphers of empires and won wars, and not one of them has been able to read a single sentence of it. This is the Voynich manuscript.

The book takes its name from Wilfrid Voynich, a rare-book dealer who bought it in nineteen twelve from a Jesuit college in Italy. But the manuscript is far older than that. When scientists finally subjected its pages to radiocarbon dating, they found that the vellum, the treated animal skin it is written on, was made in the early fifteenth century, somewhere between fourteen oh four and fourteen thirty-eight. So the object itself is real, and roughly six hundred years old. That much is not in doubt.

What is in doubt is everything written on it. The manuscript runs to around two hundred and forty pages, and it is covered, edge to edge, in a flowing, confident script that no one has ever identified. The letters look almost familiar, as if you should be able to sound them out, and yet they belong to no known alphabet. Around the text wind hundreds of strange illustrations. There are plants that match no species that has ever grown on Earth. There are circular diagrams that look like star charts or astrological wheels. There are pages of small naked figures bathing in green pools connected by what look like pipes or tubes. The book seems to be divided into sections, a herbal section, an astronomical one, a biological one, a pharmaceutical one. It has the shape of a textbook. But a textbook in a language from nowhere.

For a century, people have tried to break it, and failed. So let us do what we do here. Let us set the legend aside and weigh the explanations by the evidence, not by which one is the most thrilling.

The first serious explanation is that the manuscript is a real text written in a genuine cipher or an unknown language, and that we simply have not cracked it yet. This is the view that drew the codebreakers in. The script behaves, in some ways, like real writing. Computer analysis shows that the symbols follow statistical patterns similar to those of natural languages. Certain words repeat in the places you would expect words to repeat. The structure is not random noise. If this is true, then somewhere in those pages there may be a real message, a herbal remedy, a calendar, a secret philosophy, waiting for the right key. The trouble is that after a hundred years and the full force of modern computing, no key has turned the lock.

Then comes the next tier, the natural but unusual. Perhaps it is a real text, but written in a constructed or heavily abbreviated shorthand, or in a language spoken by a small group and now lost entirely, transcribed in a private alphabet. Or perhaps it encodes a real language in a way that strips out the vowels or scrambles the letters according to a system so personal that it died with its author. Under this idea the book is sincere, the work of someone who genuinely wished to record knowledge, but who buried it so deeply, by accident or design, that the meaning has simply slipped beneath the reach of anyone who came after.

Then there is a third tier, the idea that the codebreakers least like to hear. Perhaps there is nothing to break. Perhaps the Voynich manuscript is an elaborate hoax, a beautiful, meaningless book produced to look like a profound and ancient secret. It would not be the only one. The medieval and Renaissance worlds had a healthy trade in mysterious texts, and a convincing book of secret wisdom could be sold to a wealthy collector for a great deal of money. Some researchers have argued that the text could have been generated by a simple mechanical method, a set of grids and tables that produces word-like gibberish with exactly the kind of statistics we see. If so, the patterns that look like language are not a message at all, but the fingerprint of the trick used to fake one. And yet, against this, others point out how much skill and how many months of patient work it would take to fill two hundred and forty pages so consistently, with no obvious slips, for a book that, if meaningless, says nothing.

So where does that leave us? Weigh it honestly, and we are left with three doors, and no certain way to tell which one is real. Either it is a true text in a cipher we cannot yet break, or a true text in a tongue that has died, or a glorious and empty hoax. Each explanation has its strengths. None of them can be ruled out. And the most unsettling possibility is that we may never know, because the one person who could tell us, the hand that drew those impossible flowers and wrote those flowing, unreadable lines, has been dust for six hundred years.

And perhaps that is the real hold the Voynich manuscript has on us. It is not a disappearance or a death. No one was lost. No one was harmed. It is simply a book that refuses to be read, sitting today in a library at Yale University, perfectly preserved, perfectly silent. We can hold it. We can photograph every page. We can run its symbols through the most powerful machines we have ever built. And it gives us nothing.

We are drawn to it because it offends something deep in us, the assumption that writing is meant to be understood, that a message implies a meaning we can reach. The Voynich manuscript sits in the narrow gap between sense and nonsense, and it will not move to either side. It may be the greatest secret of the late medieval world. It may be the most patient joke ever played. And after six centuries, it is still keeping its own counsel.

This has been Unexplained. Sleep well, if you can.
