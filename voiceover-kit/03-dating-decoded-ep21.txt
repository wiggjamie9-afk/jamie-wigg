Welcome back to Dating Decoded, the show where we take the messy, confusing world of modern love and look at what's actually going on underneath. I'm your host. Today we're talking about something that almost nobody gets through life without going through at least once — a breakup. Specifically, how to actually recover from one. Not the version where you fake being fine, not the version where you spiral for longer than necessary, but the version where you genuinely come through it with something you didn't have before.

Let's start by saying the obvious thing that nobody says enough: breakups hurt because something real was there. The pain is not a sign that you're weak, or that you loved the wrong person, or that something is permanently broken in you. It's proportional to how much the relationship mattered. Grief is just love with nowhere to go for a while. That is a completely human experience, and you're allowed to have it fully.

The first thing most people do wrong after a breakup is try to escape the feeling as fast as possible. And I understand why — the feeling is genuinely awful. But the strategies we use to escape it tend to extend it. Immediately dating someone new to fill the space. Staying extremely busy so you never sit still long enough to feel anything. Using your ex as a crutch, texting them late, keeping a thread of contact alive so the loss never fully lands. None of these are healing. They're delay.

The only way out of grief is through it. Which doesn't mean you have to be devastated every moment. It means making enough space for the feeling to actually move through you rather than getting stuck. That can look like giving yourself real time to be sad without immediately pivoting to distraction. It can look like talking honestly to a friend who will listen rather than immediately offer solutions. It can look like writing down what you're feeling, even badly, even just for yourself. The feeling needs somewhere to go. Give it somewhere.

The next thing worth talking about is the story you tell yourself about the breakup. Because there is always a story, and the story shapes everything that comes after.

The unhelpful stories are the ones that put all the meaning on one side. Either the breakup is entirely your fault — you were too much, too needy, not enough, fundamentally unlovable — or it's entirely theirs — they were terrible, you were a saint, and they ruined everything. Neither of these is usually true, and more importantly, both of them lock you in place. Blaming yourself entirely keeps you small. Blaming them entirely keeps you from learning anything.

The more useful question is: what was this relationship actually teaching me? Not in a punishing way, not what does this say about my fundamental worth — it says nothing about your fundamental worth. But what did you learn about what you need? What did you discover about patterns in yourself you might want to understand better? What did being in this relationship show you about what you're looking for, or no longer willing to accept?

The breakup is painful, but it is also data. And data, approached honestly rather than defensively, can point you somewhere better.

The no contact question comes up constantly, and I want to give you a straightforward answer. Maintaining contact with an ex immediately after a breakup almost always slows recovery, for both people. This isn't a punishment, and it isn't about being cold. It's about the fact that the brain heals from loss through a process that requires the loss to actually register. If you're still texting every day, the loss hasn't fully registered. You're giving your nervous system mixed signals about whether the relationship is over, and it responds by staying in the anxious uncertainty of in-between rather than moving into the cleaner, quieter waters of grief and then acceptance.

Give yourself — and them — enough space for it to be real. How long that is depends on the relationship, how it ended, and how you're doing. For most people, a few months of genuine distance is the minimum for clarity to return. After that, some people find genuine friendship possible. Many don't, and that's okay too. Not every ending becomes something else. Sometimes it just ends.

Now let's talk about the rebuilding phase, because it does come. There is a point — you don't know when, it arrives unexpectedly — where you realise that you went a whole afternoon without thinking about them. And then a whole day. And you feel, underneath the sadness that's still there, the first faint edges of yourself coming back.

This is the moment to be intentional about who you're becoming on the other side of this. Not reinventing yourself dramatically — that usually fades. But genuinely asking: what do I actually want my life to look like? What relationships do I want to invest in? What parts of myself did I neglect while I was in that relationship that I want to come back to?

Breakups have a strange gift buried inside them. They create a clearing. For a while, everything is uncertain, and you can feel your own outline again in a way that long-term relationships sometimes blur. You remember what you like, what you want, what you're like when you're alone with yourself. That information is valuable. Use it.

Some practical things that help. Stay in your routines, even when they feel hollow. Eat properly. Sleep. Move your body. These things sound mundane in the face of heartbreak, but the physical and the emotional are not separate. Neglecting your body makes the grief heavier and longer. Taking care of it doesn't make the grief smaller — but it gives you more capacity to carry it.

See your people. Not to perform being okay, but to be genuinely held by people who actually know you. A breakup has a way of making the world feel small. The people who remember who you are before and after are invaluable. Let them in.

And please — be patient with yourself about the timeline. Someone in your life will inevitably say you should be over this by now. They mean well and they are wrong. There is no universal timeline for grief. What matters is that you are moving, even slowly, in a direction that is toward yourself and your own life rather than circling the same dark spot endlessly.

You will not feel like this forever. That sounds hollow when you're in it. But it is true. The people who have been where you are right now will tell you: it lifts. Not all at once. But it lifts. And on the other side of it, you will know more about yourself, and what you're worth, and what you want, than you did going in.

That's not nothing. That's actually everything.

That's it for today's Dating Decoded, and for this run of episodes. Thank you for being here, for listening, and for being brave enough to think honestly about love — yours, mine, ours. Take care of yourself. Be kind to your own heart. And when you're ready, try again. Until next time, be honest, be kind, and text them back. You'll be okay.
