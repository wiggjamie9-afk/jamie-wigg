Welcome to AI Briefing. Today: AI regulation — where the rules stand, what has changed in the last ninety days, and what organizations building with AI actually need to know.

The European Union AI Act moved into its first enforcement phase this month. As of this quarter, the provisions covering prohibited AI systems are now in effect across all member states. These are the hard lines: AI systems that use subliminal manipulation techniques to affect behavior, social scoring systems operated by public authorities, and real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces with narrow exceptions. Violations in this category carry fines of up to thirty-five million euros or seven percent of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.

For most technology companies operating in the EU, none of these prohibitions cover their current products. The practical impact of this first phase is therefore limited for mainstream AI applications. The phase that matters more to the majority of the industry is the one arriving in August of next year: the provisions governing high-risk AI systems. That category is broad. It includes AI used in hiring decisions, credit scoring, medical devices, education and vocational training, critical infrastructure management, and law enforcement. Any company with products in those domains should be working on compliance now, not in twelve months.

One element of the AI Act that has attracted less coverage but significant industry concern is the general-purpose AI model obligations. This covers the large foundation models — the Claude, GPT, and Gemini-class systems. Models trained on compute above ten to the twenty-three floating point operations are classified as general-purpose AI with systemic risk and face additional requirements: adversarial testing, incident reporting to the EU AI Office, and model evaluations against a defined set of benchmarks. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google are all affected. Meta is affected. The specifics of how these evaluations will be conducted are still being drafted, and the AI Office is actively consulting with the labs on methodology.

In the United States, the picture remains fragmented. The executive order on AI from late two thousand twenty-three produced a series of guidance documents from federal agencies over the course of two thousand twenty-four, but Congress has not passed comprehensive AI legislation. The current posture from the new administration has shifted toward lighter-touch regulation, emphasizing AI competitiveness over safety mandates. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework remains the primary voluntary standard that enterprises in regulated sectors are organizing around, but it carries no enforcement weight.

The state level is where US AI regulation is actually happening. Colorado passed an AI law governing high-risk decisions in consequential domains, modeled loosely on the EU approach. California's AI bills have had a complicated path through the legislature, with several high-profile bills passing the assembly only to be vetoed by the governor on competitiveness grounds. Illinois expanded its Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act. Texas passed legislation requiring disclosure when AI is used to generate political content. This patchwork is genuinely difficult for companies operating nationally, and legal teams are increasingly treating state AI law as a compliance category in its own right.

In the Asia-Pacific region, China has the most developed regulatory framework outside the EU, with specific rules governing recommendation algorithms, deepfake content, and generative AI services — all of which require content moderation, source labeling, and in some cases approval from regulators before launch. Australia has released a voluntary framework and is signaling it will move toward mandatory requirements in high-risk domains within two years. India is watching closely, having initially proposed a lighter framework, and is reassessing after several high-profile AI-generated disinformation incidents.

For legal and compliance teams, the immediate priority list is this: map your products against the EU AI Act risk categories before August of next year, implement the NIST framework if you are operating in the US and want a defensible paper trail, and start tracking state-level legislation as a standing compliance function rather than an ad hoc response.

The regulatory phase of AI is not coming. It is here. The pace is uneven, the standards are still forming, but the direction is clear and the window for building without compliance infrastructure is closing.

That is the briefing. The rules are taking shape, the timelines are real, and the cost of ignoring them is going up.
