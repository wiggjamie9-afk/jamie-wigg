Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. Let me start with a moment that can make a parent's stomach drop. You walk into the room, and there's the broken lamp, the crayon on the wall, the empty biscuit tin. And you ask the question. Did you do this? And your child, with chocolate quite literally around their mouth, looks you dead in the eye and says, no. It wasn't me. Today, we're talking about lying, and how to raise an honest child without turning your home into a courtroom.

Let me say something reassuring right at the start. A child who lies is not a child with bad character. Lying, especially in the early years, is one of the most normal and even one of the most developmentally healthy things a child can do. The first time your child tells a lie and gets away with it, something quite clever has happened in their mind. They've realised that you can't see inside their head. That what they know and what you know are two different things. That's a genuine leap in thinking. It just happens to be an inconvenient one for us.

So let's understand what's really going on. Most childhood lying isn't cold or calculated. It usually comes from one of a few simple places. Sometimes it's wishful thinking, the line between imagination and reality is still blurry, so I didn't break it can really mean I wish I hadn't. Sometimes it's avoiding trouble, the lie is a small door out of a big consequence. Sometimes it's to please you, telling you what they think you want to hear. And sometimes, as they get a little older, it's testing a boundary to see what happens. Knowing the reason behind the lie matters far more than the lie itself.

Here are some principles that hold up.

One. Try not to set a trap. When you already know the answer, resist asking did you do this. You're handing your child a choice between confessing and lying, and fear will often choose the lie. Instead, name what you see calmly. I can see the lamp is broken. Let's sort it out together. You remove the temptation to lie before it starts.

Two. Make the truth safe. Children lie most when honesty feels dangerous. If every confession is met with an explosion, they learn that telling the truth hurts. When your child does own up, let the first thing out of your mouth be about the honesty. Thank you for telling me. That was hard to say. The mess still gets dealt with, but truth-telling gets a soft landing.

Three. Separate the deed from the child. There's a world of difference between you are a liar and that wasn't true, and I need you to tell me what really happened. One attacks who they are. The other addresses what they did. Children live up, or down, to the names we give them.

Four. Keep your reactions proportionate. If small mistakes bring huge consequences, you're teaching your child that hiding is the smart move. Calm, fair, predictable responses make honesty the easier path.

Five. Model it yourself. Children watch us constantly. If they hear you tell the little social lies, tell them I'm not in when you are, they learn that truth is flexible when it's convenient. Let them catch you being honest, even when it costs you something small.

Let me walk through a few scenarios.

Scenario one. The obvious lie, chocolate on the face. Skip the interrogation. Sit down beside them and say, I think I know what happened, and you're not in big trouble. I just need us to be honest with each other. You make confessing safer than denying.

Scenario two. The tall tale, the wild story about a dragon in the garden. With young children, this often isn't lying at all, it's imagination. You can gently play along while marking the line. That's a brilliant story. And just between us, what really happened. No shame, just a soft nudge back to reality.

Scenario three. The older child who lies to cover something they were told not to do. Here, address both halves calmly. There's the thing they did, and there's the lie on top of it. Be clear that the lie is the part that worries you most, because honesty is how the two of you stay close. Then keep the consequence fair and the relationship warm.

Now, the bigger picture. Occasional lying is a normal part of growing up, and most children grow steadily more honest as they mature and as trust is built at home. A phase of fibbing does not mean you're raising someone untrustworthy.

But there are times to look a little closer. If lying becomes constant, elaborate, and seemingly without remorse, if it's paired with taking things that aren't theirs or with real distress, or if it suddenly increases after a big change or a difficult event, that's worth gentle attention. Talk to their teacher, and if you're worried, talk to your doctor or health visitor. Sometimes persistent lying is a child telling us, in the only way they can, that something underneath needs help.

So here's where I'd begin this week. Stop setting traps. When you know, name it calmly instead of asking. And whenever your child tells you a hard truth, make the very first thing you say a thank you. Build a home where honesty is safe, and honesty will grow.

You're not raising a child who never makes mistakes. You're raising one who feels safe enough to tell you about them. That's the foundation everything else is built on.
