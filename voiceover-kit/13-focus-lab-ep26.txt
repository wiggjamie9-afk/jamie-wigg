Welcome to Focus Lab.

If you've ever had a week where you were constantly in meetings, constantly responding, constantly available to everyone — and yet felt like you hadn't made any real progress on your actual work — today's episode is for you.

We're talking about the maker versus manager schedule, a framework introduced by programmer and essayist Paul Graham that explains one of the most common reasons creative and technical professionals feel chronically overwhelmed and underproductive.

Here's the distinction. A manager's schedule is built around one-hour time slots. Meetings, calls, check-ins, reviews — the day is divided into blocks, and the calendar is the master. This works well when your primary job is coordinating people, making decisions in response to others, and being available. The interruption cost is relatively low because the mode of work is already reactive.

A maker's schedule is completely different. Makers — writers, programmers, designers, researchers, strategists, anyone doing deep creative or analytical work — need long, uninterrupted blocks of time. A single meeting can destroy an entire half of a maker's day. Not because the meeting itself takes an hour, but because the anticipation of it, the preparation for it, and the mental reorientation required before and after it prevent the maker from ever reaching the depth of focus their work requires.

When a single meeting is scheduled at eleven in the morning, a maker can't fully commit to deep work from nine to eleven because the approaching interruption creates a kind of cognitive pressure. And after the meeting, the momentum is gone. The morning is lost. If there's another meeting at two in the afternoon, the afternoon is largely lost too. An entire day of available working hours might yield only thirty or forty minutes of genuinely productive deep work.

Understanding this framework changes how you think about your calendar. The question isn't how many meetings you have. The question is whether your schedule is designed for your type of work.

If you do mostly maker work, here are some practical steps. First, protect your longest, highest-quality focus period from meetings entirely. For most people that's the morning. Designate those hours as off-limits for anything scheduled. Second, batch your meetings. If you need to have them, cluster them together in the afternoon rather than scattering them across the day. A block of three meetings from one to four is far less damaging than three meetings spread across morning and afternoon. Third, build buffer time. Leave at least thirty minutes between any meeting and a focus block. The mental cool-down takes longer than you expect.

If you're in a role that requires both maker and manager modes — which is most knowledge workers today — consider designating certain days for each mode rather than mixing them. Some people have two or three meeting-free days per week and cluster all their collaborative work on the remaining days. The key is intentionality. Let your schedule serve your work, not the other way around.

Most people let their calendar fill up by default and then wonder why they can never get to their real work. Protecting your maker time isn't antisocial. It's professional responsibility.
