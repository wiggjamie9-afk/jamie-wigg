Today we're talking about tantrums. The big, loud, floor-of-the-supermarket, why-is-this-happening kind. And if you've ever stood there with a screaming toddler while a stranger stares — I want you to take a breath, because by the end of this episode you're going to understand what's really going on, and you're going to have a plan.

So here's the moment. You said it was time to leave the park. That's it. That was the whole crime. And now your two-year-old is on the ground, back arched, screaming like the world has ended. And part of you is thinking: what did I do wrong?

The answer is: probably nothing. Let's talk about why.

A tantrum is not your child being manipulative or spoiled. A young child's brain is still under construction. The part of the brain that handles big feelings — the emotional, reactive part — comes online early and runs hot. The part that calms those feelings down, that reasons and waits and says "it's okay, we'll come back tomorrow" — that's the prefrontal cortex, and in a toddler it is years away from being finished. Researchers sometimes describe it as an upstairs brain that's still being built and a downstairs brain that's already wide awake.

So when your toddler melts down over the wrong color cup, they are not giving you a hard time. They are having a hard time. Their feeling is genuinely bigger than their ability to handle it. The tantrum is the overflow.

That reframe matters, because it changes your job. Your job in that moment is not to win an argument. It's not to teach a lesson. A brain in full meltdown literally cannot take in a lesson. Your job is to be the calm they can't find on their own yet.

Child development researchers have a word for this: co-regulation. Before children can self-regulate — calm themselves down — they borrow your calm. Your steady voice, your slower breathing, your relaxed shoulders. A child's nervous system reads yours. So the single most powerful tool you have during a tantrum is your own regulated state.

That's also the hardest part, because tantrums are designed to push your buttons. So here's a simple, practical sequence you can lean on. I'll keep it to three steps.

One: get low and get calm. Lower yourself to their level. Soften your voice. You don't have to be cheerful — just steady. You're showing the storm there's solid ground.

Two: name the feeling, not the behavior. Try something like, "You really wanted to stay. You're so upset we have to go." You're not agreeing to stay. You're just showing them you see them. Naming a feeling actually helps the brain settle — sometimes called "name it to tame it." It won't switch the tantrum off like a light, but it lowers the temperature.

Three: hold the limit, kindly. The limit can stay — we are still leaving the park. You can be warm and firm at the same time. "We're going home now. I'll carry you, or you can walk. I've got you either way." Calm and clear. Not a debate.

And then — you wait it out. The feeling will crest and pass, the way feelings do. You don't have to fix it. You just have to stay.

Let's put that into a couple of real situations.

Scenario one: the supermarket. Your kid wants the candy. You said no. Full meltdown, public audience. The instinct is to cave to stop the noise — but if you can, hold steady. Kneel down. "You really want that. I know. We're not buying it today." Then keep moving, calmly, even while they're upset. You are not being cruel. You are being a safe, predictable wall in a moment that feels out of control to them.

Scenario two: the after-daycare meltdown over nothing. This one's classic. Your child held it together all day at daycare — that's exhausting for a little nervous system — and then they get home to you, their safe person, and it all comes pouring out over the wrong spoon. That's not bad behavior. That's actually a sign you're their safe place. Lower the demands right after pickup. Snack, water, quiet, connection first. Save the negotiations for later.

A quick word of reassurance, because parents ask. Tantrums are a normal, expected part of development, especially between about age one and four. Having them does not mean you've failed, and your toddler having them in public does not mean everyone is judging you — most parents watching have been exactly where you are. As kids grow and that upstairs brain develops, the tantrums usually become less frequent and less intense.

That said — you know your child. If tantrums are extremely frequent, intensely aggressive, lasting a very long time well beyond the toddler years, or if anything about your child's development worries you, that's a conversation for your pediatrician. Trust that instinct. Asking is never overreacting.

So here's your one thing to try this week. The next time the storm hits, before you do anything else, take one slow breath and drop your shoulders. Be the calm first. Everything else gets easier from there.

You're doing better than you think. A meltdown is not a grade on your parenting. It's just a small brain doing a big feeling.
