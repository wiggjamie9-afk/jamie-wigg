This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a crime that struck at the most famous family in America, and a child who vanished from a nursery in the dark. It is the spring of nineteen thirty-two. The most celebrated man in the world is a pilot named Charles Lindbergh, the first to fly solo across the Atlantic Ocean, a hero on a scale that is almost hard to imagine now. He and his wife have a little boy, not yet two years old, with fair curls and a sweet face. And on the evening of the first of March, in their grand new house in rural New Jersey, that little boy is taken from his crib. What follows is a kidnapping that gripped a nation, a ransom paid in marked money, a tragedy with no good ending, and a trial that was called, in its day, the crime of the century.

The night it happened, the household followed its ordinary routines. The baby was put to bed in the second-floor nursery. Sometime in the evening, his nurse went to check on him and found the crib empty. On the windowsill was an envelope. Inside was a ransom note, written in clumsy English, demanding fifty thousand dollars and warning the family not to go to the police or the press. Outside, beneath the nursery window, investigators found the marks of a homemade ladder in three sections, and the ladder itself, abandoned nearby, with one of its rungs split. The kidnapper had climbed up to the nursery, taken the child, and climbed back down into the night.

The response was enormous. Charles Lindbergh was not just any father. He was a living legend, and the disappearance of his son became headline news across the entire world. The investigation pulled in police, federal agents, and a cast of go-betweens and self-appointed negotiators. More ransom notes arrived, more than a dozen of them, each one pushing the demand higher and arranging the terms of a payment. Eventually, through a strange intermediary, a meeting was set. In a cemetery in the Bronx, in the dark, a man with a foreign accent took a payment of fifty thousand dollars in exchange for a note claiming the child was being held safely on a boat. The family searched for that boat. It did not exist.

And then, in May, more than two months after the kidnapping, the worst was confirmed. The body of the little boy was found by accident, in woodland a short distance from the Lindbergh home. He had almost certainly died on the very night he was taken. The ransom, the negotiations, the desperate hope, all of it had been chasing a child who was already gone. The grief of it landed on the whole country. People who had never met the family wept for them.

Now the question became, who. And here the investigators made a crucial decision. A portion of the ransom money had been paid in gold certificates, a kind of currency that was about to be withdrawn from circulation, and the serial numbers of the bills had been carefully recorded. For more than two years, those marked bills surfaced here and there across New York, a slow trickle, each one a faint trail of breadcrumbs. Then, in September of nineteen thirty-four, a man paid for gasoline at a service station with one of the gold certificates. The attendant, suspicious of the old-style bill, wrote down the license plate of the car. That plate led the police to a carpenter named Bruno Richard Hauptmann.

When they searched his home, they found a large amount of the ransom money hidden in his garage. They found, investigators said, that the wood in the homemade ladder could be traced, in part, to his attic, and that his handwriting resembled the notes. He was a German immigrant with a criminal record from his past, and the case against him was built from these threads, the money, the wood, the writing. Hauptmann insisted, then and to the very end, that he was innocent. He claimed the money had been left with him by a business associate who had since died. Almost no one believed him.

The trial, in a small New Jersey town in early nineteen thirty-five, was a spectacle unlike anything before it. Reporters and onlookers poured into town by the thousands. Vendors sold souvenirs. The courtroom was packed every day. It was a media circus so intense that it would later help reshape the rules about cameras and reporters in American courtrooms. Hauptmann was found guilty. He was sentenced to death, and in nineteen thirty-six, he was executed, still maintaining his innocence.

And yet, this is one of those cases that has never fully settled. Over the decades, researchers have returned to it again and again. Some are convinced the case was airtight, that Hauptmann was guilty beyond any doubt. Others have raised hard questions, about how some of the evidence was handled, about the pressure to convict, about whether one man could have acted entirely alone. The debate has never ended. There is a school of thought that says justice was done, and a school of thought that says a man may have gone to the electric chair on a case that would not survive the scrutiny of a modern court. I do not stand here to settle that argument for you. I only want you to see how a crime can be both solved and unsolved at the same time, depending on where you stand.

What the case did change, beyond any doubt, was the law. In its aftermath, kidnapping for ransom across state lines became a federal crime, in what became known as the Lindbergh Law. The full weight of the federal government would now come down on anyone who took a child for money. A nation's grief was written into its statutes.

But underneath all of that, the law and the trial and the endless debate, there remains a small, simple, unbearable fact. A little boy, not yet two years old, was lifted from his crib on a quiet night in the country, and he never came home. Everything else, the legend, the headlines, the questions that still have no clean answers, all of it orbits around that single, terrible loss.

This has been True Crime Brief.
