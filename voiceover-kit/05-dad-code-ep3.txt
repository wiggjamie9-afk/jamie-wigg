Hey. Welcome back to Dad Code. I'm really glad you're here, because today we're getting into the one that scared me the most when I sat down to plan this whole thing out. Today we're talking about discipline.

And I want to be honest right up front. I almost didn't make this episode. Not because it isn't important. Because it's the one where I have the least authority to talk. The first two weeks we talked about being present, and then about being present instead of perfect. And that stuff, I can stand behind. But discipline? Discipline is the place where I have lost it the most. Where I've heard a sound come out of my own mouth and thought, oh no. That's him. That's my dad. That's the exact thing I swore I would never do.

So if you came here for a guy who has it all figured out, I'm not your guy. But if you came here for somebody who's been in the kitchen at six in the evening, tired, hungry, with a four-year-old screaming on the floor because his toast got cut into squares instead of triangles, and felt the heat rise up the back of his neck. Yeah. I can talk about that. Let's do it together.

Here's where I want to start. Discipline and punishment are not the same thing. We mix them up constantly, and I think it's the single biggest reason this stuff goes sideways. The word discipline comes from a root that means to teach. A disciple is a learner. So discipline, at its heart, is teaching. It's the slow work of helping a small person learn how to live in the world. Punishment is something else. Punishment is making a kid pay for what they did. It's about the past. Teaching is about the future.

And here's the thing I had to really sit with. Punishment feels good in the moment. It does. When my kid has pushed me to the edge, there's a part of me that wants him to feel as bad as I feel right then. That's the honest truth. But that urge, that's not teaching. That's me trying to get rid of my own discomfort. And the second I learned to spot the difference, to feel that hot little wave and go, ah, that's the punishment urge, not the teaching one, something started to change.

So what does teaching actually look like? It looks like a boundary. And a boundary is not a threat. A boundary is calm, and it's firm, and it's about what you will do, not what you'll make them do. There's a big difference between, if you throw that one more time you're going to be in so much trouble, said through clenched teeth, and, I'm not going to let you throw that, said quietly while you gently take it out of their hand. One is a storm. The other is a wall. Kids actually relax against a wall. They push on a storm, because a storm tells them you're not in charge of yourself. And if you're not in charge of yourself, who's going to keep them safe?

That reframe helped me so much. When my kid is losing it, he is not giving me a hard time. He is having a hard time. Say that one again, because it took me about a year to really get it into my bones. He's not giving me a hard time. He's having a hard time. A meltdown is not a personal attack on me. It's a small nervous system that has run out of road. My job in that moment is not to win. My job is to be the calm one. To be the bigger, stronger, wiser, kinder one. And that is so much harder than yelling. Yelling is easy. Staying regulated is the actual work.

Now let's talk about that, staying regulated, because this is where it all lives or dies. You cannot teach calm from a place of rage. You just can't. A dysregulated adult cannot regulate a child. So before I can do anything useful for my kid, I have to do something for me. And I've got a couple of things that genuinely help, and I'll share them, but I want you to hear that they're small. They're not magic.

The first one is the breath. One slow breath out before I say a single word. Just one. Out longer than in. That long exhale is the thing that tells my own body, you're not actually in danger, the toast is fine, stand down. The second one is my feet. I literally feel my feet on the floor. Sounds ridiculous. Works anyway. It pulls me out of my spinning head and back into the room. And the third one, the big one, is permission to pause. I'm allowed to say, I need a minute, and step away before I do something I'll regret. Stepping away is not losing. Stepping away is the most grown-up move in the whole house.

Here's a phrase I keep in my back pocket. Connect before you correct. When my kid is flooded, full of feeling, there is no learning happening. None. His thinking brain is offline. So if I come in with a lecture, I'm talking to a closed door. First I connect. I get down low. I might just say, you really wanted those triangles, huh. That's it. I'm not agreeing the meltdown was okay. I'm letting him know I see him. And nine times out of ten, the storm starts to settle, because he's not alone in it anymore. Then, and only then, when he's back, we can talk about what happened. Connect, then correct.

But now I have to tell you the real part. Because all of this sounds lovely, and some days I am that calm wall. And some days I am absolutely the storm. Some days I lose it. I raise my voice. I say the sharp thing. I see his little face change, and my stomach drops, because I know I just became, for a second, the thing I promised I'd never be.

And for a long time I thought that was the end of the story. That every time I lost it, I was just proving I was a bad dad, racking up damage. But here's the thing that genuinely changed my life as a father. It's not the rupture that shapes a kid. It's whether there's a repair.

Repair. That's the whole game. Every parent loses it. Every single one. The ones who were yelled at and never, ever came back to say sorry, those are the ones who left a mark. Not because they got angry. Because they left their kid alone with it. So when I blow it now, I go back. When we're both calm, I get down low, and I say something like, hey. Earlier, when I yelled, that was too much. That wasn't about you. Your feelings were okay. I was tired and I lost my temper, and I'm sorry. I'm working on it.

And I want to be clear about what that does. It's not me groveling. It's not me handing my four-year-old the keys to the house. It's me teaching him the most important thing he will ever learn about relationships, which is this. People who love you will sometimes mess up, and then they come back, and they make it right. That's the repair. That's the model. When I apologize to my son, I'm not weakening my authority. I'm showing him what a strong person actually does. He learns that he's allowed to make mistakes too, because mistakes aren't the end. The repair is.

So I broke the cycle, not by being a dad who never gets angry. That dad doesn't exist. I'm breaking it by being a dad who comes back. My father never came back. He'd blow up, and then it was just silence, like nothing happened, and I was left holding all of it. So the chain doesn't break by being perfect. It breaks the moment you turn around and walk back into the room.

So here's what I want to leave you with this week. The next time your kid pushes every button you've got, try just one thing. One breath out before you speak. And if you lose it anyway, and you will, because you're human, don't spiral. Just remember the door's still open. Go back and repair. That's not failing at discipline. That is discipline. That's the whole thing.

You are not the worst moment of your week. You are the dad who walks back into the room. Hold onto that.
