Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

Socrates, whose thinking runs beneath all Stoic philosophy like a river beneath a city, said that the unexamined life is not worth living. It is one of the most quoted lines in the history of philosophy, and it has been repeated so often that it has started to sound decorative. But the Stoics took it with complete seriousness. They built an entire practice around it.

To examine your life, in the Stoic sense, is not to analyse it from a distance. It is to hold each day up to the question: did I live in accordance with my values? Not in a punishing way. Not to catalogue your failures and feel guilty about them. But to notice, honestly, the gap between what you say you care about and what your choices actually reveal.

Marcus Aurelius did this practice privately, in the notebooks we now call the Meditations. He never intended them for publication. They are remarkable precisely because they are not performances of wisdom. They are the working notes of a man trying, imperfectly, to close the distance between his philosophy and his life. You find him catching himself being reactive, impatient, too attached to approval. You find him returning, again and again, to the same principles, not because he had forgotten them, but because living them required constant renewal.

Seneca described the practice in a letter: When the light has been removed and my wife has fallen silent, aware of the habit that is now mine, I examine my entire day and go back over what I have done and said, hiding nothing from myself, passing nothing by. And why should I fear to review my faults when I can say: see that you do not do this again? I forgive you this time.

That quality of honest self-forgiveness is important. The examined life is not a life of self-punishment. It is a life of self-correction, undertaken with the same patience and care you might show a student who is learning something genuinely difficult.

The Stoics believed that most moral failure is a failure of attention: we do not see clearly in the moment, we are caught by the impulse, we let the day run us rather than choosing how to spend it. The evening review is the correction mechanism. Not a tribunal, but a conversation with yourself about who you are becoming and whether that direction is the one you actually want.

Here is today's practice. Tonight, before you sleep, take five minutes to ask three questions. What did I do today that I am glad I did? What did I do that I wish I had done differently? What one thing can I carry forward tomorrow that I learned from today? You do not need to write the answers down, though writing helps. You just need to ask them honestly. The examined life does not demand perfection. It demands attention.

The Stoics believed you cannot improve what you will not look at. Looking is where everything begins.
