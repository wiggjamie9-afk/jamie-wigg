Welcome to Plain Tech. Today we are talking about something that more and more people are thinking about: digital detox. What it means, why it matters, and how to actually do it without feeling like you are missing out.

We spend a lot of time on our screens. Phones, laptops, tablets, TVs — for many people, screens are the first thing they see in the morning and the last thing they see at night. And while technology has made life genuinely easier in so many ways, there is growing evidence that too much screen time — especially too much social media and news — can increase anxiety, disrupt sleep, and make it harder to focus.

A digital detox does not have to mean throwing your phone into the ocean. It just means being more intentional about when and how you use your devices.

Let's start with a simple concept: screen-free windows. Pick a specific block of time each day where you do not use your phone. The most effective ones are the first thirty minutes after you wake up, mealtimes, and the hour before you go to sleep. These three windows alone can make a noticeable difference in how you feel, without requiring any dramatic changes.

One of the most useful tools for a digital detox is actually built into your phone. Both iPhones and Android phones have built-in screen time or digital wellbeing features. On an iPhone, you go to Settings, then Screen Time. On Android, you go to Settings, then Digital Wellbeing. These show you exactly how many hours you spend on each app, and they let you set daily limits. When you hit the limit, the app locks and prompts you to take a break.

Social media is often the biggest culprit when it comes to mindless scrolling. If you want to cut back, one surprisingly effective trick is to delete the social media apps from your phone and only access them through your phone's web browser. It adds just enough friction that you will not open them out of habit. The content is still there if you genuinely want it, but you will scroll far less.

Turning off notifications is another powerful move. Most notifications are not urgent. They are designed to pull your attention back to an app. Go through your phone's notification settings and ask yourself honestly: does this app actually need to interrupt me? For most apps, the answer is no. Keep notifications on for messages from people, but turn them off for news, social media, and shopping apps.

If you use your phone as an alarm clock — which most of us do — consider buying a cheap standalone alarm clock and leaving your phone in another room overnight. The bedroom is one of the places where phones do the most damage, thanks to late-night scrolling and the blue light that disrupts sleep.

Digital detox is not about guilt. It is not about proving you are above technology. It is about designing your relationship with your devices so they work for you, rather than the other way around. Even small changes, done consistently, can dramatically improve your focus, sleep, and sense of calm.

Start with one change. See how it feels. Then add another.
