Welcome to Plain Tech. Today we are talking about AI assistants — what they actually are, what they are genuinely good at, and what their limitations are. Because there is a lot of hype around this topic, and it helps to have a clear-eyed view.

You have probably heard the terms AI assistant, chatbot, or large language model. These all refer to similar things: software that can hold a conversation, answer questions, write text, summarise documents, and help you think through problems. The most well-known examples right now are ChatGPT from a company called OpenAI, Gemini from Google, and Claude from a company called Anthropic.

You can access most of these through a website on your phone or laptop. You type a question or a request, and the AI writes back. It is a bit like having a very fast, very knowledgeable assistant who never gets tired — but one who has some important quirks you should know about.

What are AI assistants genuinely good at? They are excellent at drafting text. If you need to write a complaint letter, a birthday message, a job application, or an email to a difficult client, an AI can give you a solid first draft in seconds. You still review and adjust it, but it removes the blank page problem entirely.

They are also very good at explaining things. You can ask an AI to explain a medical term your doctor used, or to explain how your electricity bill is calculated, or to summarise a long article in plain language. They are patient and will explain the same thing multiple ways if you ask.

AI assistants are useful for brainstorming too. If you are trying to think of ideas for a birthday present, a business name, or a travel itinerary, they can generate a wide range of options quickly.

They are also helpful for straightforward research questions — the kind where you want a quick, clear summary rather than having to read ten web pages. Asking an AI something like "what are the main differences between a fixed and variable rate mortgage" will get you a clear, readable explanation immediately.

Now, the limitations. This is important.

AI assistants can be wrong. They sometimes present incorrect information confidently, and they can fill in gaps in their knowledge with plausible-sounding but false details. This is sometimes called hallucination. For anything important — medical advice, legal decisions, financial choices — always verify what an AI tells you with a qualified professional or a reliable official source.

AI assistants also have a knowledge cutoff. They were trained on information up to a certain date, so they will not know about recent news, new laws, or things that happened in the last few months.

They also do not actually understand you the way a person does. They are very good at pattern matching — predicting what a useful response looks like — but they do not have genuine feelings, opinions, or common sense the way humans do. Treating them like a search engine with better writing skills is a more accurate mental model than treating them like a person.

Privacy is worth thinking about too. Be careful about what you share with AI assistants. Do not paste in sensitive personal information, financial details, private correspondence, or anything you would not want stored on a server somewhere.

With all that said, AI assistants are genuinely useful tools when you understand what they are for. They are best used as a starting point, a drafting tool, and an explainer — not as a final authority.

Try asking one something simple today. Ask it to explain something you have always been curious about. You might be surprised how helpful it can be.
