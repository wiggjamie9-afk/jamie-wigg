Welcome to AI Briefing. Today: the automation of AI itself. Specifically, what happens when AI systems start doing the work of building and improving other AI systems. This is not a distant theoretical concern. It is already happening, it is accelerating, and it has implications for every person working in or around this industry.

Let us start with the clearest version of the story. For most of the history of machine learning, the hard work of building a model was done by people. Researchers designed the architecture. Engineers wrote the training code. Data teams cleaned and labeled datasets. More researchers tuned the hyperparameters, watched the training curves, and ran the evaluations. It was skilled, expensive, and slow.

Automated machine learning tools, sometimes called AutoML, have been trying to mechanize parts of this for years, with mixed success. But what has changed recently is different in kind, not just degree. Today, AI systems are being used to write the research code, generate and filter the training data, propose and test architectural improvements, and in some cases run the evaluations and synthesize the results. The human is still in the loop, but the loop is getting smaller.

The most direct example is what the labs call AI-assisted research. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and others have published work describing how AI models help write the code that trains the next generation of models. This is not a fully autonomous cycle yet. But the direction is clear. More of the work of building AI is being done by AI.

There is a technical name for the most ambitious version of this: recursive self-improvement. The idea that an AI system could improve its own capabilities, then use those improved capabilities to improve itself further, in an accelerating loop. This is also the idea that has historically drawn the most concern from AI safety researchers, because a system that can meaningfully accelerate its own development might do so faster than humans can understand or control.

We are not at recursive self-improvement in any dangerous sense today. To be precise: today's AI systems can help write research code, but they are not redesigning their own fundamental architectures, retraining themselves from scratch, or setting their own objectives. The human-in-the-loop is still real and still consequential. But the trend line points in that direction, and the pace is worth watching.

What does this mean in practice, today, for people whose jobs involve building AI?

The honest answer is that the work is shifting rather than disappearing, at least for now. The routine parts of AI engineering, boilerplate code, standard evaluation scripts, data cleaning pipelines, are increasingly being generated rather than written by hand. The parts that still require people are design judgment, research direction, novel problem framing, and the safety and governance work that ensures these systems are deployed responsibly.

But this shift is not uniform across roles. Prompt engineering as a standalone function is already being automated in many contexts. Basic fine-tuning workflows are increasingly push-button. Teams that were twelve people are becoming six, not because the ambitions are smaller but because the tools are doing more.

For anyone building or planning a career in AI, the implication is to move toward the parts of the work that require genuine scientific judgment, systems-level thinking, and the kinds of decisions that have real consequences. Those are the areas where human oversight is not optional, it is the point.

The briefing on AI automating AI: the trend is real, it is already changing what research teams look like, and it will continue. Today we are at assisted research and automated routine engineering. The more autonomous versions are further out and the subject of serious safety debate. Watch the trend line, not just the current position.
