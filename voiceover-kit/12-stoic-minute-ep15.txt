Welcome to Stoic Minute. I'm Harry.

Epictetus hands us a deceptively gentle line today: It is not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters.

We have all heard something like it. But Epictetus meant it with full philosophical weight, not as a comforting slogan. He taught that events themselves are neutral. Rain is just rain. A delay is just a delay. A harsh word is just sound in the air. What turns these neutral happenings into misery is the judgement we attach to them, the silent verdict that this is bad, this is unfair, this should not be. The disturbance lives in the verdict, not in the event.

This is liberating once you feel the truth of it, because it relocates power. If the world were the direct cause of your distress, you would be at its mercy entirely. But if the cause sits in your response, then a space opens up, a small but real freedom, exactly where you thought there was none. Between what happens and how you take it, there is a gap. And in that gap, you get to choose.

The Stoics did not pretend this was easy. Reacting well to a genuine setback takes practice, and you will often fail. But the skill is real and it compounds. The more you notice that the upset is in your interpretation, the more often you can loosen your grip on it, and the lighter the same events begin to feel.

So here is today's practice. The next time something small goes wrong, pause before you react. Remind yourself the event is neutral, and the meaning is yours to assign. Then choose your response on purpose, rather than letting it fire off on its own.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
