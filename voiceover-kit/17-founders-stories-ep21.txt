Welcome to Founders' Stories.

We've spent the past few episodes talking about the hard parts: the pivots, the fundraises, the failures, the growing pains. Today I want to close this run of episodes with something different. I want to talk about why.

Not why in the abstract motivational sense. Not "I wanted to change the world" or "I saw a gap in the market." I want to talk about the deep, personal, sometimes uncomfortable reason that founders keep going when every rational signal is telling them to stop.

I spoke with four founders for this episode. All four had reached a point in their journey where quitting was genuinely the reasonable option. All four kept going. And all four told me essentially the same thing in different words.

James Park built an AI-assisted legal tool for low-income tenants facing eviction. He spent two years building it with almost no revenue, sustained by a combination of grants and his own credit cards. He maxed out two credit cards. He almost lost his apartment. He told me he kept going because "stopping felt like abandoning the people who needed it." His users were facing homelessness. The stakes were too high to quit.

Amina Diallo built a supply chain transparency platform for small fashion brands that wanted to verify their ethical sourcing claims. She was rejected by twenty-three investors before getting her first yes. She told me: "I knew the problem was real because I had lived it as a buyer. I couldn't unknow what I knew."

Tom Nguyen built a mental health platform for first responders — firefighters, paramedics, police officers — a population with staggering rates of burnout and suicide and almost no appropriate mental health support. He lost his best friend, a paramedic, to suicide in two thousand and seventeen. He started the company in two thousand and nineteen. He doesn't talk publicly about the connection very often. But he told me quietly at the end of our conversation: "Every user who stays on the platform for more than sixty days is someone I think about. I want to know they're okay."

And Leila Moss, who built an educational technology company focused on early childhood literacy in rural communities, told me: "I'm a first-generation college graduate. I grew up in a town where most kids didn't have books at home. I build this company for the kids I grew up with."

Four founders. Four completely different industries. Four different reasons.

But here is the thread that connects all of them. None of them were primarily motivated by the outcome for themselves. They were motivated by the outcome for someone else. A tenant facing eviction. A fashion brand trying to do the right thing. A paramedic sitting alone in a dark apartment. A kid in a rural town who deserves to learn how to read.

This is not to romanticize poverty or martyrdom or working yourself into the ground for a cause. These are all smart people building sustainable businesses. Leila has raised three million dollars. Tom is closing a Series A. James's platform now has partnerships with three legal aid organizations in California.

But the reason they survived the hard parts — the credit cards, the twenty-three rejections, the grief, the self-doubt — is that they were connected to something larger than their own success.

That connection is not a strategy. You can't reverse-engineer it. But if you have it, protect it. Write it down. Read it back to yourself on the bad days. It's the thing that will carry you through the moments when nothing else will.

Thank you for listening to this run of Founders' Stories. If one of these episodes connected with something you're building, I'd love to hear about it.
