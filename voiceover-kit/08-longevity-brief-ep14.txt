This is The Longevity Brief. Twelve minutes, one topic, the science with the noise filtered out, and a couple of things you can actually do.

Reminder: general education, not medical advice. If you're worried about memory changes in yourself or someone close to you, please see a doctor, some causes are treatable, and early assessment matters.

Today, the fear that sits underneath a lot of longevity anxiety: not just living long, but keeping your mind. Cognitive longevity. Protecting your brain, your memory, and your independence as the decades pass. There's a lot of supplement marketing aimed at this fear, so let's separate what the evidence supports from what's just selling hope.

Start with a reassuring fact that often gets lost. Significant cognitive decline and dementia are common with age, but they are not guaranteed, and they are not simply the inevitable result of getting old. A meaningful share of dementia risk is tied to factors we can actually influence. Large expert reviews have estimated that a substantial portion of dementia cases worldwide could, in principle, be delayed or prevented by addressing known risk factors across life. That's not a promise for any one person, but it reframes the brain from something you passively lose to something you can help protect.

So what are those factors? Here's the honest, evidence-based list, and notice how familiar it sounds.

First, and this surprises people, your heart and your brain are deeply linked. The same things that damage your blood vessels, high blood pressure, high blood sugar, smoking, tend to damage the brain too. So managing blood pressure, not smoking, and keeping your metabolic health in reasonable shape are among the best-supported ways to protect your mind. What's good for the heart is good for the brain is not a slogan, it's a finding.

Second, physical activity. Regular exercise is one of the most consistently supported habits for brain health. It improves blood flow, and in studies it's associated with lower rates of cognitive decline. You don't need anything exotic, the same movement that helps the rest of your body helps your brain.

Third, hearing. This one is underappreciated. Untreated hearing loss in midlife is one of the larger modifiable risk factors for later cognitive decline. The leading theory is that struggling to hear isolates people and strains the brain. Getting your hearing checked, and using hearing aids if you need them, is a genuinely evidence-based brain move.

Fourth, social connection and mental engagement. We did a whole episode on relationships for good reason, isolation and loneliness are linked with worse cognitive outcomes. Staying socially and mentally active, through people, learning, and meaningful activity, appears protective.

Fifth, sleep. Deep sleep is when the brain runs its overnight clearance processes, and chronic poor sleep is associated with worse brain aging. Protecting sleep is protecting the brain.

Now let me be blunt about the supplement aisle, because this is where money gets wasted. For healthy people, there is no pill, no nootropic, no brain-boosting blend, with strong evidence that it prevents dementia or meaningfully slows normal cognitive aging. Many heavily marketed brain supplements have failed in good trials, or were never properly tested. If you have a specific deficiency, for example low vitamin B-twelve, correcting it matters, but that's treating a deficiency, not buying enhancement. Be skeptical of anything promising to sharpen an already healthy brain.

A quick word on brain training games. Practicing a specific game makes you better at that game. The evidence that commercial brain-training transfers into real-world memory or staves off decline is weak. Learning a genuinely new and challenging skill, a language, an instrument, a craft, is a better bet than tapping a puzzle app, and it's more rewarding.

So, practically.

One. Treat your cardiovascular health as brain health. Know your blood pressure, don't smoke, stay active. This is the highest-leverage area.

Two. Get your hearing checked, especially from midlife, and act on it if needed. It's easy to ignore and genuinely matters.

Three. Keep learning hard, new things, and stay connected to people. Novelty and relationships, not apps.

The brief on the brain: cognitive decline is common but far from fully inevitable, much of the protection comes from the same heart-healthy, active, connected life we keep returning to, plus hearing and sleep, and the supplement shortcuts mostly don't work.

One thing this week: book the blood pressure check, or the hearing check, you've been putting off, and start one genuinely new thing you have to concentrate to learn.
