The Letting Go Problem. Welcome to Episode twenty two.

There is a moment every dad dreads, and it does not arrive with a warning. One day your kid is asking you to come with them everywhere. The next day they are asking you to please drop them at the corner so their friends do not see the car. And somewhere between those two days you lost the job of being needed, and nobody told you what you were supposed to do next.

Teenage independence. The part of parenting nobody puts on the brochure.

Here is what I have learned. The instinct to hold on is not weakness. It is love, misfired. You are holding on because the stakes feel higher now. They are out in the world without you. They are making decisions you cannot see. They are spending time with people you have never met, doing things you will only hear about later if you have built the right kind of relationship with them. Which is, of course, exactly the problem. The tighter you hold, the less they tell you.

I had a version of this with my own dad. He was a good man, an involved dad, but when I hit fifteen or sixteen he could not figure out how to shift gears. He kept trying to parent me the way he had when I was nine. Which meant he got a lot of eye-rolls and closed doors, and I think that genuinely hurt him, even though he never said so.

What I understand now that I did not understand then is that he was not being controlling. He was scared. He had spent a decade and a half keeping me safe and now the job description had changed without anyone telling him.

The job description when they are small is: protect, teach, guide, solve. The job description when they are teenagers is quieter and harder. It is: be available without hovering. Be curious without interrogating. Set limits without making everything a battle. Trust them to fail small so they do not fail catastrophically later.

That last one is the one that trips most of us up. We want to save them from every bad decision. But if we always step in, we rob them of the experience of recovering from a mistake. And recovery is the skill. Recovery is what gets you through adult life. Not the absence of problems. The ability to climb back up.

So what does this look like in practice?

I started asking questions instead of giving advice. Not what happened, which sounds like an interrogation, but more like, how are you feeling about that? What are you thinking of doing? I am interested in your process, not just the outcome. That shift changed the temperature of our conversations completely.

I also started saying yes more. Yes to the sleepover. Yes to the concert with friends. Yes to staying out an hour later than last time, with a clear agreement about when to check in. Every reasonable yes earns trust. Every unnecessary no costs it. You have to spend trust carefully, and the account only stays full if you keep depositing.

And when things went sideways — because they will go sideways, that is just the maths of adolescence — I tried to be the soft landing, not the consequence on top of the consequence. They already feel bad when they mess up. What they need from you in that moment is not a lecture. It is the knowledge that you are still there.

You are not losing them. You are loaning them to the world for a while. The ones who come back and actually talk to you as adults are the ones whose dads figured out how to let go at the right speed.
