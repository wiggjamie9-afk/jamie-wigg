# PODCAST #12: STOIC MINUTE — Episode 3
**Voice to use:** Daniel (grounded, calm gravitas)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Daniel

---

Picture yourself at an airport. You have somewhere you need to be. And then the board changes, and beside your flight, in plain letters, appears the word, delayed. You feel it instantly, don't you? The tightening in the chest, the rising heat, the urge to argue with someone, anyone. But here is the strange truth at the centre of that moment. There is not one single thing you can do to make that plane leave any sooner. And almost two thousand years ago, a man born into slavery understood exactly why that moment hurts, and exactly how to set yourself free from it.

Welcome to Stoic Minute. Today, the single idea that sits at the very heart of Stoic philosophy. The teaching of Epictetus that the ancients called the dichotomy of control.

Epictetus began his most famous work with a sentence so simple it is easy to miss how much it carries. Some things, he said, are up to us, and some things are not. That is the whole of it. That is the foundation everything else is built upon. So let us be precise about which is which, because the entire practice depends on getting this line in the right place.

What is up to us? A short and honest list. Our judgements. Our choices. Our intentions. Our own actions. The effort we decide to make. The way we choose to respond to what happens. These, and really only these, belong to us. They are ours. No one can take them, no circumstance can force them. They live inside the small, quiet space of your own will.

And what is not up to us? Almost everything else. Other people, what they think, what they say, what they decide to do. Outcomes, whether the plan works, whether you win, whether the effort pays off. Your reputation, the opinion the world holds of you. Your body, in the end, and your circumstances. And the past, every moment that has already happened, sealed now and beyond all reach. These things we may influence. We may hope for them, work toward them. But we do not control them. They are not ours.

Now, why does this matter so much? Because nearly all of our suffering, Epictetus would say, comes from one simple error. We place our peace, our happiness, our sense of being all right, in the hands of things we do not control. And then we are astonished when that peace is taken from us. We tie our calm to the delayed flight, to the outcome of the meeting, to whether a particular person approves of us. And so our inner weather is forever at the mercy of the world's outer weather. We have, without noticing, handed the keys to our own contentment to people and events that never agreed to keep it safe.

Return to the airport. The flight is delayed. That fact is simply not up to you. The weather, the airline, the mechanics, none of it is in your hands. So the Stoic does not waste a single ounce of strength fighting what cannot be moved. Instead they turn, gently, to what is theirs. How will I use this hour? Will I spend it furious, poisoning my own afternoon over something I cannot change? Or will I read, or rest, or speak kindly to the equally stranded stranger beside me? The delay is not up to you. Your response to the delay is entirely up to you. And that, that small pivot, is where freedom lives.

Or consider a harder case. An unfair criticism. Someone says something about you that simply isn't true, and you feel the sting of it, the injustice of it. Notice what is happening. Their words, their opinion, their choice to speak, none of that was ever in your control. You cannot reach into another person's mind and rearrange what they think of you. What you can control is whether you let their judgement become your judgement. Whether you sit with it, conduct yourself with integrity, and let your actual character answer over time. Or whether you hand them, for free, the power to ruin your evening. The criticism is theirs. Your steadiness is yours.

Or perhaps the quietest test of all. Waiting on news. The result of the test, the answer to the application, the call that has not yet come. Here the mind loves to torment itself, running ahead into a hundred futures, none of which have happened yet. But the outcome is not up to you. It is being decided, or has already been decided, somewhere beyond your reach. What is up to you is this. Whether you spend the waiting consumed by a future you cannot touch, or whether you live the hour you actually have. The Stoic does not pretend the news doesn't matter. They simply refuse to be ruled by what they cannot command.

Now, hear this clearly, because it is the most misunderstood part of the teaching. The dichotomy of control is not a recipe for passivity. It does not ask you to stop trying, to shrug and care about nothing. Quite the opposite. It asks you to pour yourself fully into your effort, your preparation, your choices, all the things that genuinely are yours, and then to release your grip on the result, which never was. Do everything in your power. And then let the outcome be the outcome. You will find, paradoxically, that you act with more courage and more clarity once you stop demanding that the universe guarantee you a particular ending.

This is the great exchange the Stoics offer. You give up the illusion that you control the world. And in return, you receive something far more valuable. Sovereignty over yourself. A calm that does not depend on the weather, or the verdict, or the approval of strangers. A place to stand that no delayed flight, no unfair word, no anxious wait can ever take from you.

So let me leave you with one practical thought to carry into your day. The next time you feel that familiar tightening, that frustration, that anxiety rising in your chest, pause, and ask yourself a single question. Is this up to me? If it is, then act. Bring your full effort, your best self, your clearest judgement. And if it is not, then let your shoulders drop, and let it go, and return your attention to the one small kingdom that is, and always will be, entirely your own.

This has been Stoic Minute. Go gently, and remember. Some things are up to us. Some things are not. Peace begins the moment you can tell them apart.
