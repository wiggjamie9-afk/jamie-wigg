Hello, this is Austin, and you're listening to Unexplained.

Tonight our mystery is not centuries old. It happened on a single evening, in front of thousands of witnesses, on live television, in the city of Chicago, on the twenty-second of November, nineteen eighty-seven. For roughly ninety seconds, two of the city's broadcast signals were hijacked by a person in a mask, who appeared on screens across the city, said almost nothing that made sense, and then vanished. No one was ever caught. No one ever came forward. To this day, no one knows who did it. This is the Max Headroom broadcast intrusion.

To understand it, you have to picture the technology of the time. In nineteen eighty-seven, television was broadcast over the air, beamed from tall transmission towers to the antennas on people's roofs. The signal travelled through open air, and in principle, anyone with a powerful enough transmitter, pointed in the right direction, could overpower the legitimate signal and put their own pictures on the screen instead. It was difficult. It required real equipment and real knowledge. But it was possible. And on that November night, someone did it. Twice.

The first intrusion came during a sports report on a station's nine o'clock news. For a few seconds, the broadcast cut out, and viewers saw a figure wearing a rubber mask, a mask of a popular computer-generated character of the era known as Max Headroom. The figure bobbed and swayed in front of a sheet of corrugated metal that had been spun to create a strange, wobbling background. There was no intelligible audio, just noise, and then the station regained control. The engineers managed to fight the signal off by switching the frequency of their studio link. It was bizarre, but it was brief.

The second intrusion, about two hours later, is the one that has haunted people ever since. During a late-night broadcast of a Doctor Who episode on a public television station, the screen cut out again, and the same masked figure returned. This time the audio worked. And what followed was about ninety seconds of deeply unsettling, disjointed performance. The figure ranted and giggled and quoted advertising slogans. It made crude jokes. Near the end, it lowered its trousers while an accomplice, off camera, struck it on the backside with a flyswatter. And then, as suddenly as it had begun, the signal cut, and the Doctor Who episode returned, as if nothing had happened.

It would be easy to treat this as a simple prank, and in one sense it was. But the mystery has never been who they were as people so much as how they did it, and how they got away with it so completely. So let us do what we do here. Let us set the legend aside and weigh the explanations by the evidence, not by which one is the strangest.

The strongest explanation, and the one almost everyone accepts, is the most grounded. This was a deliberate act of signal hijacking carried out by skilled amateurs, people with serious knowledge of broadcast engineering. To override two professional television stations on the same night, the perpetrators would have needed a powerful transmitter and the technical understanding to aim it precisely at the stations' receiving links. This points to insiders or near-insiders, people who worked in or around broadcasting, or who had deep hobbyist expertise in radio and television transmission. The Federal Communications Commission investigated. Engineers analysed what had happened. And the consensus was clear. This was not magic. It was someone who understood the system well enough to break into it for ninety seconds.

Then comes the next tier, the question that keeps the case alive. If the how is broadly understood, why was the who never found? Broadcast signal intrusion is a federal crime, and the penalties were severe. A serious investigation was mounted. And yet, almost forty years later, no one has ever been identified, charged, or convicted. No reliable confession has ever been verified. For a stunt performed in front of an entire city, the silence afterward is remarkable. Some argue this points to a small, careful, technically sophisticated group who simply never spoke of it again, to anyone, ever. The discipline required for that is, in its own way, as impressive as the hijack itself.

Then there is a third tier, the further-out ideas, the ones the evidence supports least. Theories that the intrusion carried a hidden message, that the nonsense was code, that it was the work of a secret organisation, or that the perpetrators were protected by someone powerful. These ideas grow in the vacuum left by the unsolved case. But there is no good evidence for any of them, and the simplest reading of those ninety seconds is not a coded message. It is a prank, crude and chaotic and a little frightening, performed by people who found the act of breaking in more important than anything they actually said.

So where does that leave us? Weigh it honestly, and the most likely truth is almost entirely grounded. A small number of technically gifted people overrode two Chicago television stations on a November night in nineteen eighty-seven, performed a deliberately absurd and unsettling sketch, and then disappeared back into the city and never told a soul who could not be trusted. There is no supernatural force here. There is only human skill, human strangeness, and a discipline of silence that has held for nearly four decades.

And perhaps that is exactly what makes it linger. Most mysteries are old, their witnesses long dead, their evidence rotted away. This one is recent. It was recorded. You can watch those ninety seconds today, the bobbing mask, the spinning metal, the laughter with no joke behind it. The footage is crisp. The act is plain. And still, the most basic question, who was behind the mask, has never been answered.

We are drawn to it because it sits in a very modern blind spot, the moment when our most controlled and public medium was quietly seized by an unknown hand. For ninety seconds, the screen that was supposed to show us the official world showed us something no one had authorised, no one understood, and no one could trace. The signal was reclaimed. The broadcast resumed. But the question stayed in the air, and it is still there. Somewhere out in the world, perhaps, someone knows exactly who put on that mask. And whoever they are, they have kept the secret far better than anyone expected.

This has been Unexplained. Sleep well, if you can.
