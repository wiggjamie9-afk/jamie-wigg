# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 10
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. Today we're talking about something quiet and slow and a little bit out of fashion, and I think it might be one of the most important gifts you can hand a kid. Today we're talking about raising a reader. About how you grow a child who reaches for a book on their own, in a world that is fighting tooth and nail for their attention.

Let me start with a confession, because that's how we do it around here. I am not a natural reader. I want to be the dad with the wall of books and the reading glasses and the deep thoughts. The truth is I read about four books a year, and two of them are about football. So when my daughter started getting into stories, I had this quiet panic. What if she asks me about a book and I haven't read one in months? What if I'm a fraud?

And here's the first thing I worked out, and it took the pressure right off. You do not have to be a great reader to raise one. You have to be a dad who reads in front of them. That's a completely different job. Kids don't need you to be clever. They need to see that a grown man, a dad, the biggest person in their world, sometimes sits down and chooses a book over a phone. That's the whole lesson. They're not listening to your opinion on the book. They're watching what you pick up when you've got ten free minutes.

Now let me tell you what nearly killed reading in our house, so you can avoid it. I turned it into homework. I made it a performance. Right, sit up, sound out the word, no, try again, that's a B not a D, come on, concentrate. And I watched the light go out of my kid's eyes in real time. I took the one thing that's supposed to feel like magic and turned it into a test she was failing in front of her dad. Never again.

So here's the reframe. The goal, for years, is not to teach reading. School does that bit. Your job is to make books feel like warmth. Like the good part of the day. Like Dad's voice and the lamp on and nowhere else you have to be. You're not building a skill. You're building an association. Books equal safe. Books equal close. Get that right and the skill follows on its own, almost as a side effect.

Right, the practical bit. Three moves.

One: read to them way longer than feels necessary. There's this idea that once a kid can read, you stop reading to them. That's a mistake. Keep reading to them long after they can do it themselves, because the bedtime story was never really about the words. It was about the closeness. A nine-year-old still wants to be read to. They just won't admit it, so you have to keep offering.

Two: let them see your choices, including the rubbish ones. Don't curate yourself into a saint. Let them catch you reading the back of a cereal box, a comic, a daft thriller, the news. Reading isn't a holy act reserved for important books. It's just a thing this family does. The casualness is the point. A kid who thinks reading is only for serious people will quietly decide it's not for them.

Three: never, ever make finishing a book mandatory. If a book is boring, let them ditch it. We don't make them clean a plate of food they hate, and we shouldn't make them grind through a story they're not feeling. The freedom to abandon a bad book is what protects the love of a good one. Adults who hate reading are very often just adults who were once forced to finish something they couldn't stand.

Here's what I want you to sit with. The screens are not going anywhere, and I'm not asking you to wage war on them. I'm asking you to put one other thing in the running. A quiet thing, with your voice in it. Because a kid who loves to read is a kid who can go anywhere, be anyone, survive being bored, and sit with their own mind. That last one might be the rarest skill of the whole century.

You don't have to be well-read to do this. You just have to read with your eyes, out loud, with the lamp on, for as many years as they'll let you.

Next week we get loud. Sport. Competition. Winning, losing, and that strange dad-instinct to live through your kid on the sideline. I have got some sins to confess on that one. See you then.

This has been Dad Code. Now go let your kid catch you reading something.
