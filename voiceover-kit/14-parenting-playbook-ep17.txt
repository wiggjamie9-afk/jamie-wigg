Welcome back to the Parenting Playbook. Today we're going into emotional development — specifically, how children learn to understand and manage their feelings, and what parents can do to support that process.

Emotions are not a distraction from childhood. They are the curriculum. A child who can identify what they feel, tolerate discomfort without being overwhelmed, and express themselves without hurting others or shutting down — that child is going to navigate school, friendships, and eventually adult life with much greater ease.

The technical term for this capacity is emotional regulation. And the important thing to understand is that it develops over time, with support from co-regulating adults. Children are not born able to calm themselves down. They learn it by watching us, by being helped through it repeatedly, and gradually by practicing it on their own.

So what does co-regulation look like in practice? It means that when your child is upset, your nervous system becomes a kind of anchor for theirs. If you stay relatively calm, they have something to borrow. If you escalate — raising your voice, tensing up, getting reactive — their system often escalates further. This is not about being a robot. It's about learning to create a pause between the trigger and your response.

One of the most evidence-supported tools here is something called emotion coaching, developed by psychologist John Gottman and his colleagues. The basic steps are straightforward. First, notice the emotion before it becomes a behavior problem. Second, treat the feeling as a valid opportunity rather than an inconvenience. Third, put a label on the emotion together with the child. Fourth, set limits on behavior while validating the feeling. Fifth, problem-solve with the child when they are calm enough to engage.

That second step is worth pausing on. Many parents respond quickly to the behavior and skip right past the feeling underneath it. Your child throws something and you address the throwing. That's appropriate. But the underlying feeling — frustration, jealousy, powerlessness — often gets left unaddressed. When children feel their emotions are not seen, those emotions tend to get louder over time, not quieter.

Labeling emotions is especially powerful for young children. There is genuine neurological evidence that naming a feeling — you look really frustrated right now — activates the thinking parts of the brain and reduces the intensity of the emotional experience. Language and emotion are deeply linked.

For older children and teenagers, emotional development looks different. They need space to feel things without being immediately problem-solved or reassured. Sometimes sitting quietly next to a struggling teenager and just being present without talking does more than any advice could.

The long game here is worth keeping in mind. You are not just managing today's tantrum or today's meltdown. You are building the architecture of emotional intelligence that will serve your child for the rest of their life.
