Welcome to Stoic Minute. I'm Harry.

Epictetus gives us a strange and beautiful image today. We have two ears and one mouth, he said, so that we can listen twice as much as we speak.

It sounds almost like a joke, but underneath it is a whole philosophy of how to move through the world. Epictetus taught that wisdom rarely arrives while we're talking. It arrives while we're paying attention. And most of us have the ratio backwards. We listen just long enough to find the gap where we can insert our own reply. We're not really hearing the other person. We're waiting, loading our response, rehearsing our turn.

The Stoics prized self-control above almost everything, and they understood that the tongue is one of the hardest things to govern. It races ahead of judgement. It says the clever thing, the cutting thing, the thing we can't take back. Epictetus elsewhere warns that on any given occasion it's better to say little, or nothing, than to speak and regret it. Silence, for the Stoic, is not emptiness. It is a kind of strength, a space where understanding can actually form.

And there is generosity in it too. To truly listen to another person, without planning your rebuttal, is to give them something rare. The feeling of being genuinely heard. People will forgive a great deal in someone who really listens.

So here is today's practice. In your next conversation, hold back your reply by just a few seconds. Let the other person finish completely. Ask one more question before you offer your own view. Listen, as Epictetus would say, twice as much as you speak.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
