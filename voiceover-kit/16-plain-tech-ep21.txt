Welcome back to Plain Tech. Great to have you here.

Today we are talking about privacy settings. And I want to start by saying that this is not going to be a scary episode. I am not going to tell you that everything is hopeless and you are being watched and there is nothing you can do. I do not believe that, and I do not think it is helpful. Instead, I am going to give you a calm, practical tour of the settings that actually matter, explain what they mean in plain language, and help you make a few quick choices that give you more control with very little effort.

Let us start with the big picture. When you use apps on your phone, those apps often ask for permission to access parts of your phone: your location, your camera, your microphone, your contacts, your photos. Sometimes that makes sense. A maps app genuinely needs your location to give you directions. A camera app genuinely needs the camera. But many apps ask for permissions they do not actually need, simply because more data about you is valuable to them.

The good news is that you have the power to say no. And your phone makes it fairly easy to review and change these decisions.

On any modern phone, there is a Privacy section in the Settings app. It usually has a list of things like Location, Camera, Microphone, Contacts, Photos, and so on. If you tap on any of them, you see a list of every app on your phone that has asked for access to that thing. And you can turn individual ones off.

The most important one to check is Location. Go to your phone's settings, find Location or Location Services, and have a look at which apps can see where you are. You will probably find several apps that you barely use, or would never have expected, have access to your location all the time. For most apps, you can change this from Always to Only While Using the App, which means they can see your location when you are actively using them, but not in the background when the app is closed. This is a reasonable middle ground that lets navigation and maps apps work while stopping a shopping or weather app from silently tracking your movements around the city.

The other one worth checking carefully is Microphone. There are apps that have microphone access and you would struggle to explain why they need it. A shopping app. A recipe app. Something you downloaded once for a specific thing and forgot about. If an app does not have a clear reason to listen through your microphone, turn that permission off. Nothing bad happens to the app. It just loses access to something it probably should not have had in the first place.

Now let us talk about what your apps know about you beyond permissions.

Many apps have their own privacy settings buried inside them. Social media apps in particular often have settings that control who can see what you post, whether your profile is searchable, whether the app can track your activity across other websites and apps, and whether it can use your data to show you targeted ads. These settings are not always easy to find, but they are there.

A useful thing to know: there is a setting on both iPhones and Android phones that controls whether apps can track your activity across other companies' apps and websites to build an advertising profile of you. On iPhone, this appears when you open each app that wants to track you — a prompt asking whether to allow tracking. You can say no to every one of those and it makes no difference to how the app works. It just means you see less tailored advertising, which most people consider a fine trade-off. On Android, there is a similar setting called Delete Advertising ID or Opt Out of Ads Personalisation in the privacy settings.

None of this stops you from being shown ads. It just makes those ads less specifically targeted to your habits and interests. If that bothers you less than the idea of a company building a detailed profile of your daily behavior, it is worth five minutes of your time.

One more thing: app permissions are not a decision you make once forever. Apps update and sometimes request new permissions. It is worth going to your phone's privacy settings every few months, just for a quick five-minute scan. You are looking for apps that have access to things they should not, apps you barely recognise or no longer use, and anything that seems odd. It is like a quiet audit of who has keys to your house, and it keeps things tidy.

A brief word on your social media profiles. Most people set them up in a hurry and never revisit the privacy settings. Most social platforms default to showing your information as widely as possible, because the more connected and visible you are, the more valuable the platform is to advertisers. It is worth spending ten minutes in the privacy or account settings of any social platform you use regularly and checking: who can see your posts, who can search for you by name or email, whether your profile is visible to people who are not logged in, and whether old posts have visibility settings you would change today. You are allowed to be more private. Tightening these settings does not mean leaving the platform. It just means choosing what you share and with whom.

To wrap up. Go to your phone's location settings and check which apps can see your location always versus only when you are using them. Do the same for microphone access and remove anything that should not be listening. On iPhone, tap no to tracking requests. On Android, find the advertising ID setting. Revisit app permissions every few months. And spend ten minutes in the privacy settings of your main social media app to check who can actually see what.

Your homework today: open your phone's settings, find the privacy section, and check one thing: which apps have permission to access your location all the time. Just that one. It takes about three minutes and it is often quite revealing.
