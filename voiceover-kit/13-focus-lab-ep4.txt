This is Wade, and you're in the Focus Lab. Let me start with a number that should bother you. Studies on knowledge workers have found that the average person switches what they're doing roughly every three minutes. Three minutes. And once they've been interrupted, it can take more than twenty minutes to get back to the original task. Do that math across a single working day, and you start to understand why you feel busy from morning to night and yet somehow finish almost nothing that mattered. Today we're putting multitasking on trial, because it is quietly the most expensive habit you own.

Here's the first thing you need to accept, and it's uncomfortable. You cannot actually multitask. Not really. What your brain is doing when you think you're multitasking is something researchers call task-switching. You are not running two streams of attention at once. You are flipping between them, fast, and paying a fee on every single flip. People love to believe they're the exception, the rare person who's genuinely good at juggling. The research is brutal here. The people who multitask the most are often the worst at it, and the worst at filtering out distraction. The confidence is the trap.

So what does each switch actually cost you? Three things. First, time. Every time you jump from writing to checking a message and back, your brain has to reload the context of what you were doing. Where was I, what was I thinking, what comes next. That reload isn't free. Second, accuracy. Switching while you work measurably increases errors, because part of your mind is still tangled in the last thing. And third, and this is the sneaky one, it costs you energy. Task-switching is mentally tiring in a way that deep, single-tasked work is not. You end the day drained, and you can't even point to what you produced.

Let me give you a picture for this. Imagine your attention is a spotlight, and you're standing in a dark theatre. When you focus on one thing, the spotlight is bright and tight and the actor is lit up perfectly. Now imagine trying to light two actors on opposite sides of the stage. You're swinging that spotlight back and forth, and in between, while it's travelling across the dark, nobody is lit at all. That dark travel time, that's the cost of multitasking. It's the moment where no real work is happening, and you do it hundreds of times a day without noticing.

There's a particular version of this that destroys focus, and it's worth naming. Background multitasking. The email tab left open. The phone face-up on the desk. The group chat you're half-watching. You're not actively reading them, but your brain knows they're there, and a slice of your attention stays parked on them, waiting. It's like trying to read a book while someone might tap you on the shoulder at any second. You never fully sink in, because part of you is braced for the interruption that hasn't even come yet.

So let's fix it. Tool number one. Single-task on purpose, and make it visible. Pick one task. Say it out loud if you have to. For the next twenty-five minutes, this is the only thing that exists. Close every other tab. Not minimise, close. An open tab is an open invitation.

Tool number two. Batch the small stuff. The reason you keep switching is that little tasks feel urgent the moment they appear. They almost never are. Instead of answering every message as it lands, collect them. Process all your email in two or three dedicated windows a day, not continuously. You'll be shocked how little breaks when you stop being instantly available.

Tool number three. Put a wall between you and the trigger. Most of your switching isn't a decision, it's a reflex to a notification or a buzz. So remove the trigger. Phone in another room. Notifications off, not on silent, off. Every switch you prevent is a switch you don't have to recover from.

Tool number four. When you catch yourself mid-switch, name it. Just quietly notice, I'm switching right now. That tiny moment of awareness is enough to break the reflex and pull the spotlight back. You won't catch every one. You don't need to. You just need to catch more than you did yesterday.

So here's your challenge. Tomorrow, pick one block of work, just one, and do it the way nobody does anymore. One task. Everything else closed. Phone in another room. And watch how much further you get in twenty-five honest minutes than you usually get in an hour of switching. The cost of multitasking is invisible, which is exactly why it's so expensive. Once you can see it, you can stop paying it. Go try it. I'll see you next time on Focus Lab.
