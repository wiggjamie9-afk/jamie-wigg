Welcome to Founders' Stories.

There is a specific kind of problem that only appears when things are going well. It doesn't have a dramatic name. It doesn't show up in crisis mode. It sneaks up on you in the middle of a good quarter, when the team is growing and the metrics are climbing and the press coverage is positive and you keep waking up at three in the morning anyway.

It's the problem of scaling.

Today I want to talk about what it actually feels like to go from ten employees to fifty, from fifty to two hundred, from a company where you knew everyone's birthday to one where new people start on Monday and you don't find out until Friday.

Sofia Reyes built a consumer fintech app in Colombia that helped gig workers track their income, manage irregular cash flow, and access small credit lines. She launched with three people. By the time she raised her Series A, she had twenty-two employees. A year after that, she had eighty-nine.

She told me the jump from twenty-two to eighty-nine was the hardest thing she has ever done professionally. Not the fundraise. Not the product challenges. Not the regulatory complexity of operating in financial services. The people.

Here is what nobody told her about scaling a team.

When you have twenty people, culture is something that exists naturally because everyone is in the same room most of the time, sharing the same information, making decisions together. Culture at twenty people is ambient. You don't need to manage it because it's just the air you breathe.

At eighty people, culture has to be intentional. It has to be written down. It has to be lived and reinforced and repeated, not because the values changed, but because new people joined who were never in that original room. They didn't share the early failures. They didn't sit through the all-hands meetings where the runway was three months and you all had to figure out together whether to keep going. They showed up after the hard part, which means they need to understand the values that came out of the hard part in a different way.

Sofia said the biggest mistake she made during the scale-up was assuming that great individual contributors would naturally become great managers. She promoted people who were brilliant at their jobs into leadership roles because they had institutional knowledge and the team trusted them. Some of those people thrived. Several of them burned out completely within eight months, managing people for the first time with no framework and no support.

She now has a specific transition program for first-time managers inside her company. It includes a coaching relationship, a monthly peer group with other new managers, and a six-month check-in process. It costs money and time. It costs much less than the two people she lost during the first scaling wave who might still be with the company today if she'd invested in them differently.

The other thing she told me about scaling is this: communication breaks down in ways you don't expect. When you have twenty people, an update you share in a team meeting reaches everyone immediately. When you have eighty people, that same update gets filtered through three or four layers of management. By the time it reaches the newest engineer on the product team, it's been interpreted, summarized, and re-contextualized twice. You think everyone knows the plan. A surprising number of people have a different version of it.

Sofia now sends a weekly written update to the entire company. Every week, no exceptions. It covers what happened, what's coming, and what she's thinking about. It takes her about ninety minutes to write. She says it's the best ninety minutes she spends every week.

Scaling is not just about adding people. It's about maintaining coherence as you grow. The founders who do it well are the ones who treat communication and culture as core infrastructure — not nice-to-haves that can wait until the team is bigger.
