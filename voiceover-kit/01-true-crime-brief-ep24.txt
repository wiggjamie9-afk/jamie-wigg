Tonight, a crime without a confirmed perpetrator, a set of injuries without a fully agreed-upon cause, and an investigation that has stretched across three presidential administrations without producing a public arrest or a public prosecution. What began in Havana, Cuba in two thousand and sixteen has since touched American diplomats and intelligence officers on nearly every continent. It is known, most commonly, as Havana Syndrome. And what exactly it is remains one of the most contested questions in the intersection of national security and medicine.

In late two thousand and sixteen, American diplomatic and intelligence personnel stationed at the United States Embassy in Havana began reporting something unusual. A sudden onset of pressure in the ears. A piercing sound, directional — heard strongly on one side of a room, disappearing when they moved. Nausea. Cognitive fog. Some reported that they could hear it only when standing in a specific part of their residence, that moving a few feet in any direction made it stop. Within months, dozens of embassy employees were reporting symptoms. Some were medevaced to the United States for evaluation. Several sustained what neurologists described as measurable brain abnormalities — damage to white matter, changes in how the brain processes information — though the specifics varied significantly between patients.

The United States eventually withdrew more than half its embassy staff from Havana. Relations with Cuba, which had only recently been cautiously normalized under the Obama administration, deteriorated sharply.

Then the reports spread. Personnel in China. Officers in Russia. Individuals stationed in Vienna, in Colombia, in Georgia, in Poland, in India, in Australia. Some were intelligence community employees. Some were staffers from other agencies. Some reported symptoms at home in the United States. By two thousand and twenty-one, the number of cases being tracked by the federal government had surpassed one thousand.

The leading hypothesis among government investigators became the possibility of directed energy — a pulsed microwave or radiofrequency weapon of a kind that has been theorized and in some forms developed by multiple militaries for decades. In two thousand and twenty-three, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence produced a classified assessment that most intelligence agencies concluded it was unlikely that a foreign adversary had carried out a deliberate, sustained campaign to injure American personnel using such a weapon. The dissent from that conclusion was notable. Some agencies, including the CIA, assessed that a subset of the cases could not be explained by that conclusion.

The investigation has not ended. The question of who — if anyone — carried out an organized campaign remains officially unresolved. Congressional hearings have been held. Legislation was passed to compensate affected personnel, acknowledging that something real had happened to them even if the cause could not be officially named.

What makes Havana Syndrome a genuinely unusual case in the context of criminal and national security investigation is not the absence of suspects — investigators have named several, Russia chief among them — but the extraordinary difficulty of building a prosecutable case around an injury caused by a weapon that cannot be produced in a courtroom, whose mechanism of harm is still disputed among scientists, and whose victims are dispersed across classified postings around the world.

Some of the people who reported symptoms have recovered. Some have not. Some have left government service entirely. The career of the CIA director who was most publicly associated with the investigation included her own reported symptoms on a foreign trip.

Whether Havana Syndrome is the signature of a covert weapons program, an unexplained phenomenon, or some combination of factors that medicine and intelligence are still sorting through may not be answered publicly for many years, if ever. But for the people who sat in apartments in Havana in two thousand and sixteen, listening to something move through the walls, something clearly had changed. And whatever changed it has, so far, escaped any accounting.
