This is History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, a rebellion that began with a dream, ended in one of the bloodiest civil wars in human history, and is still almost unknown outside the country where it happened.

In the year eighteen thirty-six, a young man named Hong Xiuquan sat an imperial examination in China, and he failed. He went home and fell ill. And while he lay sick with fever, he had visions. In those visions, he saw an old man with a golden beard who told him he was his father. He saw a middle-aged man who told him he was his elder brother. He saw himself given a sword. He saw himself commanded to go down to earth and slay the demons.

Hong Xiuquan recovered from his fever and failed the examination a second time, and then a third. And then, years later, he came across a pamphlet given to him long before by a Christian missionary, and reading it he became convinced he understood his visions at last. The old man with the golden beard was God the Father. The elder brother was Jesus Christ. And Hong Xiuquan himself was the younger son of God, sent to China to purify the land, drive out the Manchu rulers of the Qing dynasty, and establish a Heavenly Kingdom on earth.

He began to preach. And the extraordinary thing — the thing that still stuns historians — is that people listened. In the countryside of southern China, where poverty was grinding, where the Qing government was corrupt and weak, where floods and famine had left millions desperate, a man claiming to be the brother of Jesus Christ gathered followers by the hundreds, and then by the thousands, and then by the tens of thousands.

By eighteen fifty-one, Hong Xiuquan had declared the establishment of what he called the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. His army — the God Worshippers, they called themselves — had grown into a military force capable of taking cities. And take cities they did. They swept north through China, capturing town after town, and in eighteen fifty-three they took Nanjing, one of the great cities of the country, and made it their capital, and Hong Xiuquan ruled it from a palace built for him at the centre of the city.

The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom lasted until eighteen sixty-four. Eleven years. And in those eleven years it became a state of its own, with its own currency, its own laws, its own theology, its own examination system, and its own extraordinary contradictions. Women were given some rights they had never previously held. Foot binding was banned. At the same time, the regime enforced rigid separation of the sexes, even between husbands and wives. Hong Xiuquan himself, the man who had founded the movement on visionary purity, kept dozens of concubines in his palace and rarely left it in his final years, growing increasingly erratic and removed from reality.

The war that eventually ended the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom was one of the most devastating conflicts of the nineteenth century. The Qing dynasty, with support from Western military commanders including a British officer named Frederick Townsend Ward and later the famous Charles Gordon, fought the Taipings for more than a decade across the richest and most densely populated provinces of China. The death toll was staggering. Modern historians estimate that the Taiping Rebellion and the wars it triggered may have killed between twenty million and thirty million people. Some estimates go higher. By any measure, it was one of the deadliest conflicts in all of human history, greater in its toll than the entirety of the First World War.

And yet it is almost entirely absent from the western historical imagination.

Hong Xiuquan died in eighteen sixty-four, possibly by suicide, just as the Qing forces were closing in on Nanjing. His son, who briefly succeeded him, was captured and executed. The Heavenly Kingdom fell. The palaces were burned. The followers were slaughtered or scattered. And the extraordinary, terrible experiment of a failed exam student who believed himself the son of God was over.

The world barely noticed. But China has never forgotten.
