Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. Tonight, I want you to picture the dark. It's bedtime, the lights are out, and a small voice calls from the bedroom. There's a monster. Or maybe it's the morning of a birthday party, and your child, who was so excited, is suddenly clinging to your leg, frozen, refusing to go in. Or it's the worry that comes out of nowhere at the dinner table. What if you die? Today, we're talking about childhood fears and anxiety, and how to be the steady, calming presence your child needs when the worries come.

Let me start with something I really want you to hold on to. Fear is not a flaw in your child. Fear is one of the oldest, most useful feelings a human being has. It exists to keep us safe, to make us pause at the edge of things, to keep us alive. A child who feels fear is not broken or weak. They have a perfectly healthy alarm system. Sometimes that alarm just goes off when there's no real danger, and our job is not to rip the alarm out. It's to help them learn to read it.

So let's understand what's happening inside. When your child is frightened, the thinking, reasoning part of their brain goes quiet, and the older, more primitive part takes over, the part that shouts danger and floods the body with that fight or flight feeling. This is why, in a moment of real fear, logic simply doesn't land. Telling a terrified child there's no such thing as monsters is like talking to someone who can't, in that moment, hear you. Their alarm is too loud for words.

So here's the first and most important principle. Never dismiss the fear, even when it makes no sense to you. When we say, don't be silly, there's nothing to be scared of, we mean to comfort. But what the child hears is, my big feeling is wrong, and I'm on my own with it. Instead, we start by joining them where they are. That feels really scary to you. I'm here. You're not laughing at the fear, and you're not feeding it either. You're simply standing beside them in it.

The second principle. Be the calm they can borrow. We talked in an earlier episode about being the anchor in the storm, and it's just as true here. A frightened child reads your body before they hear your words. If your face is calm, your voice is low, and your shoulders are soft, you are telling their nervous system, the world is safe, I am not afraid, you can settle now. Your steadiness is contagious. Lend it to them.

The third principle, and this one is subtle but powerful. Try not to over-rescue. When a child is scared of something that is actually safe, a dog on a lead, the swimming pool, a sleepover, our instinct is to swoop them away from it entirely. But constant rescue quietly teaches the child, you were right to be terrified, and you can't cope. Instead, we help them face the scary thing in small, gentle, supported steps. Not thrown in, but led in, one brave inch at a time, with you holding their hand.

The fourth principle. Give the fear a shape they can handle. Young children think in pictures and stories, so meet them there. The monster under the bed can be sprayed away with a bottle of magic monster spray, which is really just water. The worry that loops at bedtime can be told to a worry box, or a worry doll, or whispered into your ear so you can carry it for the night. You're not lying to them. You're handing their imagination a tool to fight back with.

Let me give you two quick scenes. Scene one. The bedtime fear of the dark or of monsters. Here, you validate first, then empower. You say, I can see the dark feels scary right now. Let's make your room feel safe together. A soft night light, a checked-under-the-bed, a brave teddy who guards the door. You're not arguing the monster away. You're building a fortress of safety around them.

Scene two. The anxious child who panics at new situations, the party, the first day, the strange place. Here, prepare them gently in advance. Talk through what will happen, who'll be there, what it'll look like. The known is far less frightening than the unknown. And then go slow. Stay close. Let them watch from the edge before they join in. Brave doesn't mean unafraid. Brave means scared, and doing it anyway, with you nearby.

Now, a really important note. Everyday fears, the dark, dogs, strangers, new places, are a completely normal part of growing up, and most fade with time and gentle support. But anxiety can sometimes grow larger than that. If your child's fears are stopping them from doing the ordinary things of childhood, if they're intense, constant, or causing real distress, or if your gut tells you this is more than a passing phase, please reach out to your doctor, health visitor, or a child specialist. There is wonderful help out there, and seeking it is one of the bravest, most loving things a parent can do.

Here's my last thought for you tonight. Your child does not need you to make the whole world safe. No one can do that. What they need is to know that whatever frightens them, they will never have to face it alone, because you are right there beside them. Courage isn't the absence of fear. Courage is a small hand in a bigger one, walking toward the scary thing together. Be that hand. Be gentle with them, and be gentle with yourself. You're doing better than you think. I'll see you next time on Parenting Playbook.
