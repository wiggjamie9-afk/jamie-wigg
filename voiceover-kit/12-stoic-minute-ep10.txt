Welcome to Stoic Minute. I'm Harry.

Today's line comes from Seneca, writing to a friend who was always rushing toward some better tomorrow: While we are postponing, life speeds by.

It lands like a quiet accusation, because most of us are postponing something. The real rest begins after this project. The real living starts once the money is sorted, the house is finished, the children are grown. We treat the present as a waiting room, a corridor we hurry through on the way to a life that is always just ahead.

Seneca's point is severe but kind. The day you are rushing through is not the corridor. It is the room. This ordinary Tuesday, with its small tasks and its grey sky, is not a rehearsal for living. It is the living itself. And every hour spent leaning into the future is an hour of your actual, only life handed away for nothing.

The Stoics did not mean that we should never plan. They meant that we should not mortgage the present to a future that may never arrive, and that even if it does, will simply become another present we are too distracted to inhabit. The habit of postponement does not end when circumstances improve. It follows us. It has to be broken now, or not at all.

So here is today's practice. Find one moment you would normally rush through, and stop rushing. Drink the coffee without your phone. Walk the familiar street as if you had never seen it. Give the person in front of you your whole attention. Let one part of today be lived rather than merely passed through.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
