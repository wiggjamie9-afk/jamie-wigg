You're listening to Wanderlust — twelve minutes to take you somewhere. I'm your guide, and today we're going to Kyoto. Close your eyes for a second, if you're not driving. I want you to picture this.

It's just before dawn. The city of Kyoto is still asleep, but you're not. You're walking up a quiet stone path on the eastern hills, and the air smells like cedar and cold morning. Then you turn a corner — and there it is. Kiyomizu-dera. A wooden temple built into the side of a mountain on a platform of giant pillars, with no nails holding it together. Just joinery, the way it was raised in 1633. The first light hits the wide veranda, and below you the whole city unfolds in mist.

Kyoto was Japan's imperial capital for over a thousand years, and it kept its soul when other cities lost theirs. There are more than 1,600 Buddhist temples here and around 400 Shinto shrines. You could spend a month and not see them all. But you don't need a month. You need a good morning and a plan.

Let me take you to three of them.

First, Fushimi Inari. You've seen the photo even if you don't know the name — thousands of vermilion gates, called torii, marching up a hillside in an endless tunnel of orange. Each gate was donated by a person or a business, their name painted on the back. Walk through enough of them and the crowds thin out, the light goes green and gold through the wood, and it gets quiet. It's a two-hour climb to the summit if you go all the way. Most people quit at the first viewpoint — which is exactly why you should keep going.

Second, the temple I started with: Kiyomizu-dera. 'Kiyomizu' means pure water. There's a waterfall here split into three streams, and pilgrims drink from one — for love, for success, or for long life. You're not supposed to drink from all three. That's considered greedy. I love that a place this old still has rules about not wanting too much.

Third, and my favorite: the moss gardens and the temple of Ginkaku-ji, the Silver Pavilion. It was never actually covered in silver. The shogun who built it died before it was finished, and somehow the unfinished, restrained version is more beautiful than the gold one across town. There's a meticulously raked cone of sand in the garden meant to reflect moonlight. Six hundred years of someone raking sand, every day, so the moon lands right.

Here's the thing about Kyoto that no guidebook quite tells you. The magic isn't in any single temple. It's in the contrast. You walk out of a 1,000-year-old shrine, turn left, and you're in a convenience store buying a perfect egg sandwich and a hot canned coffee. A geisha — properly, a geiko — hurries across a lane in the Gion district at dusk, and behind her is a vending machine glowing in the rain. Old and new don't fight here. They share a street.

My favorite memory of Kyoto is the most ordinary one. I got lost looking for a famous bamboo grove, ended up in a residential neighborhood, and an elderly woman sweeping her step pointed me the right way without a word of shared language — just a smile and a hand. That's travel. Not the postcard. The kindness you didn't plan for.

Okay — here's how you actually do Kyoto well, from a well-travelled friend who's made the mistakes for you.

Tip one: go early or go late. The temples open around 6am. If you're at Fushimi Inari or Kiyomizu-dera by 7, you'll have them nearly to yourself. By 10am they're packed. Jet lag is your secret weapon here — you'll be awake at 5am anyway, so use it.

Tip two: buy a prepaid IC card — an ICOCA or Suica card — the minute you land. Tap it on every bus, train, and subway, even some vending machines. No fumbling for change, no working out fares. It turns the whole city into a tap-and-go.

Tip three: Kyoto is a bus city more than a train city for the temples. Get a one-day bus pass if you're temple-hopping. But honestly? Rent a bike. Kyoto is flat in the center, and you'll cover three times the ground and see the in-between streets that are the real reward.

Tip four: book your accommodation in the Higashiyama or Gion area if you can swing it, so you can walk to the eastern temples at dawn before the day-trippers arrive from Osaka. If that's out of budget, stay near Kyoto Station — everything radiates out from there.

Tip five — and this one's important — temples are active places of worship, not film sets. Bow slightly at the gate. Don't eat while walking through a shrine. Photography is often banned inside the main halls, and there are signs. In the geisha district of Gion, some private lanes now ban photos of geiko entirely, with fines, because tourists were mobbing working women on their way to appointments. Be the traveler the locals are glad they let in.

Tip six: shoulder season. Go in late October into November for the autumn leaves, or early-to-mid April for cherry blossom — but know April is peak crowds and peak prices. My quiet pick is the second week of November. The maples turn the temple grounds blood-red, and the summer crowds are gone.

Tip seven: eat where the monks eat. Look for 'shojin ryori' — Buddhist temple cuisine, entirely vegetarian, served in little lacquered bowls. It's one of the great meals of your life and most tourists walk right past it.

Kyoto doesn't shout. That's its whole personality. It asks you to slow down, to look at a single raked line of sand, to drink from one waterfall and not three. In a world that wants you to do everything, Kyoto is a thousand-year-old argument for doing less, but doing it with your whole attention.
