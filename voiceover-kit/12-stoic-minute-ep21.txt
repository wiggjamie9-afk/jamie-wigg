Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

There is a passage in Marcus Aurelius that tends to stop people the first time they encounter it. He writes: When you wake up in the morning, tell yourself: the people I deal with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and surly. They are this way because they cannot tell good from evil. But I have seen the beauty of good, and the ugliness of evil, and have recognized that the wrongdoer has a nature related to my own, not of the same blood or birth, but the same mind, and possessing a share of the divine.

That final turn is where the passage lives. Marcus does not end with the catalogue of human difficulty. He ends with kinship. The very same people he has honestly acknowledged as sometimes ungrateful, sometimes arrogant, sometimes unjust, he also recognizes as sharing his nature. They are not enemies to be defeated. They are fellow participants in the same difficult business of being human.

The Stoics were committed to what they called cosmopolitanism, a word they coined, meaning citizen of the world. They believed that reason was the common property of all human beings, and that this shared rationality created a kind of kinship that crossed all the boundaries of city, tribe, rank, and nation. Marcus, writing as emperor of the most stratified civilization in the western world, kept reminding himself that the person of lowest status he encountered was, at the deepest level, the same kind of being as himself.

This is not a sentimental idea. Marcus is not pretending that people do not cause harm or act badly. He has already acknowledged that they do. The philosophical move is more precise: when you understand that wrongdoing comes from confusion rather than pure malice, from a failure to see clearly what is good rather than a settled desire to destroy you, the anger transforms. Not into approval of the bad act, but into something closer to the feeling you might have for someone who is lost. You still respond to the situation. You do not have to pretend harm is not harm. But you respond without contempt.

Here is today's practice. Think of one person who has been difficult for you recently. Not your most serious conflict, something ordinary: the colleague who frustrates you, the person in your life whose behaviour grates. Try for a moment to hold the Stoic thought that they, like you, are navigating this with incomplete understanding. They are not simply bad. They are, very likely, doing their best with what they can see. Notice if that thought loosens anything, even slightly.

Connection, the Stoics believed, is not a reward for people behaving well. It is a discipline you choose to practice regardless.
