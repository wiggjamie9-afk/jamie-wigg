Welcome back to the Parenting Playbook. Today we are talking about co-parenting — sharing the raising of a child with someone who may no longer be your partner, your friend, or even someone you particularly like. This is a topic that deserves honesty, not just optimism.

Co-parenting after separation or divorce is one of the most sustained interpersonal challenges adults face. You are asked to maintain a functional working relationship with someone you have complex history with, across years or decades, through disagreements about money, schedules, rules, and values — all while trying to shield your children from the weight of that complexity. It is genuinely hard.

The research on what matters most for children is consistent and clear. The single strongest predictor of child wellbeing after parental separation is not the custody arrangement, not the family structure, and not whether parents live close together. It is the level of conflict between parents. Children who are caught in the crossfire of ongoing parental hostility show measurable increases in anxiety, behavioral problems, academic difficulty, and long-term emotional dysregulation. This holds even when the conflict does not involve the child directly.

This means the most protective thing co-parents can do for their children is manage their relationship with each other. Not for the sake of the other parent. For the sake of the child.

What does workable co-parenting look like? Researchers distinguish between cooperative co-parenting and parallel parenting, and both can serve children well depending on the situation. Cooperative co-parenting involves active communication, shared decision-making, and reasonable flexibility. It works well when the former relationship was functional and both parents can manage disagreement without escalation. Parallel parenting involves lower communication, more rigid structures, and clear separation of spheres — each parent manages their own household independently. It is often the more realistic option when conflict is high, and it can still protect children effectively as long as they are not being used to carry messages, gather information, or manage adult feelings.

There are behaviors that consistently harm children in co-parenting situations. Badmouthing the other parent in the child's presence. Using the child as a messenger or informant. Asking the child to choose sides or keep secrets. Withholding information the other parent needs. These behaviors place the child in an impossible position — loving both parents and being asked to be loyal to only one.

Children need permission to love both parents. Saying this out loud — I want you to have a great time with your dad this weekend — costs very little and means a great deal.

Effective co-parenting is a long game. Agreements that feel unfair today may be renegotiated in two years as circumstances change. The goal is not to win. It is to create the most stable, loving environment possible for a child who did not choose any of this, but who deserves the best outcome you can give them.
