The forests of the Pacific Northwest contain something. Or so thousands of people over more than a century have reported. They describe a large bipedal creature, covered in dark hair, standing between seven and ten feet tall, walking upright through the tree line, leaving behind footprints that stretch sixteen inches long with a stride pattern no human could replicate. The creature has been called Bigfoot in North America, Sasquatch in the indigenous languages of the Pacific coast, and by dozens of other names across cultures that have no contact with each other but describe the same thing.

The Bigfoot phenomenon is easy to dismiss. The Pacific Northwest is one of the most hiked, hunted, and surveilled wilderness regions on Earth. Modern camera traps, trail cameras, infrared sensors, and drone surveillance have documented bear populations, mountain lion corridors, and migration patterns with extraordinary precision. They have not produced a verified image of a large unknown primate. In a world where mountain lions in suburban Los Angeles get photographed on doorbell cameras, the absence of clear Bigfoot imagery is a powerful argument.

And yet the footprint evidence is not as easily dismissed as popular skepticism suggests. In nineteen sixty-seven, Roger Patterson and Robert Gimlin filmed a creature at Bluff Creek in Northern California. The film, known as the Patterson-Gimlin footage, remains controversial but has never been definitively debunked. Frame analysis by biomechanical researchers has noted a walking gait that human actors in a costume would struggle to reproduce, particularly a specific knee flex and hip rotation inconsistent with standard human bipedal locomotion. Multiple attempts to recreate the footage with a suited performer have failed to convincingly replicate the movement.

The footprints are even more interesting. Not because there are many of them, but because of what researchers have found in specific casts. Dermal ridges, the tiny skin texture lines found on the undersides of primate feet, have been found in some Bigfoot casts by forensic analysts familiar with dermatoglyphics. A skin suit cannot produce dermal ridges. A carved wooden fake foot cannot produce them. If the ridges in certain casts are genuine, something with primate skin architecture made those tracks.

The indigenous knowledge angle is often overlooked. Many First Nations peoples of the Pacific Northwest, the Coast Salish, the Sts'ailes, the Kwakwaka'wakw, have oral traditions that describe a large forest-dwelling being as a literal, physical entity, not a spirit, not a metaphor. These traditions predate European contact by many generations. They describe a creature that avoids people, lives deep in the forest, smells strongly, and occasionally comes close to camps or villages. The descriptions across different groups who have no historical contact with each other are remarkably consistent.

Zoologists argue that a breeding population of large primates in North America would require extensive habitat and would leave undeniable biological evidence. Hair, scat, bones, remains. None have been found and verified. Genetic analysis of submitted hair samples has consistently returned known species or contamination.

The most likely explanation is a confluence of misidentification, folklore, and motivated reporting, in which black bears observed upright, known animals moving in low light, and the human brain's deep sensitivity to detecting other large creatures creates the conditions for sincere but inaccurate reports.

But sincerity alone does not produce dermal ridges in footprint casts. It does not produce an identical creature description across a dozen unconnected indigenous cultures. The thing in the forest may be nothing more than our ancient fear of what watches from the dark. Or the Pacific Northwest may contain something that has simply never been unlucky enough to die somewhere we could find it.
