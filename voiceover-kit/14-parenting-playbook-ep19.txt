Welcome back to the Parenting Playbook. Today's topic is conflict resolution — specifically, how to help children navigate disagreements, manage anger constructively, and develop the skills to work through conflict without it destroying relationships.

Let's be honest about something first. Conflict is not the problem. Conflict is normal and inevitable in families, friendships, and eventually workplaces. The goal is not to raise children who avoid conflict. It's to raise children who can move through conflict without losing themselves or the relationship.

Children learn conflict resolution primarily by watching adults. If the adults in their lives scream when angry, shut down when hurt, or never repair after a fight, children absorb those patterns as the default. If the adults in their lives name their feelings, set limits, take breaks when flooded, and come back to talk — children learn those patterns instead.

So the first thing to say is this: how you handle conflict in front of your child matters as much as any lesson you deliberately teach.

Now let's talk about what to actually do when conflict happens between siblings, or between parent and child.

Step one is de-escalation. When nervous systems are activated — when someone is yelling or crying or slamming doors — very little productive problem-solving can happen. The prefrontal cortex, the part of the brain responsible for reasoning and empathy, goes offline under high stress. The first job is to lower the temperature. That might mean a brief, calm separation. Not a punitive timeout, but a genuine pause. I can see we're both getting really heated. Let's take five minutes and then come back to this.

Step two is naming what happened from each person's perspective. Not who was right and who was wrong — what each person experienced. This is hard for children, and honestly it's hard for adults. But the practice of being able to say I felt left out when that happened, separate from I was wrong, is foundational to emotional intelligence.

Step three is problem-solving together. What would make this better? What do each of us need? Can we find something that works for both of us? This is where children develop negotiation skills, perspective-taking, and creativity. It takes longer than a parent just imposing a solution — but children who practice this process become dramatically more capable.

Sibling conflict deserves a special note. Research by developmental psychologist Judy Dunn suggests that sibling relationships are actually a primary training ground for social skills. The conflicts siblings have — and the ways they resolve them — build capacities that peers and teachers simply cannot replicate. That does not mean you ignore all sibling conflict. It means you treat it as an opportunity rather than a failure.

One practical tool: teach children a simple repair phrase early. Something like I'm sorry I hurt you. I want to fix it. Those words, practiced and normalized, lower the cost of repair and make the behavior far more likely.

Conflict does not have to leave a mark. Handled well, it builds something.
