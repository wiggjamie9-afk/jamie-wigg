When Your Parents Become Your Project. Welcome to Episode twenty four.

There is a version of fatherhood nobody warns you about. You have just about figured out how to be a dad to your kids, maybe they are teenagers or young adults now, you are getting the hang of the letting-go part, and then life adds a layer.

Your own parents get older. And not in a gentle, theoretical way. In a practical, logistical, emotionally complicated way. Suddenly you are a dad and also a son, and both jobs are pulling on you at the same time, and neither one can wait.

I started noticing it in small things first. My dad could not read the restaurant menu anymore without holding it at arm's length and squinting. My mum started writing things down that she would never have needed to write down before. The drive to their house felt a bit longer each year, and I was not sure if the distance had actually changed or if I had just started feeling it differently.

And then the bigger things started. The fall. The hospital appointment that turned into several hospital appointments. The conversation about driving that nobody in the room enjoyed. The first time I found myself on the phone to a doctor, advocating for my own father, the way he had once sat in hospital corridors advocating for me.

That role reversal is one of the stranger experiences available in adult life. You spend the first part of your life being looked after by these people. You spend the middle part of your life being busy and assuming they are fine. And then one day you look up and they need you, and the dependency has quietly reversed, and nobody made an announcement about it.

Here is what I have figured out about being a dad while also caring for ageing parents.

The guilt is going to show up either way. If you are with your kids, you are thinking about your parents. If you are with your parents, you are thinking about your kids. That guilt is not a signal that you are failing. It is a signal that you love people in multiple directions. That is not a flaw. That is the actual state of being a parent at this stage of life.

What helps is being honest with both generations about where you are. Your kids can handle knowing that you are stretched. In fact, watching you show up for your parents teaches them something they will one day need to know themselves. You are modelling what loyalty to family looks like across generations. That is not a small lesson.

With your own parents, the hardest thing is usually the conversation about help. Most of the parents I have spoken to about this resisted help for far longer than was sensible, because accepting help felt like a surrender of independence. My dad in particular would rather have attempted something dangerous than asked me to come over and do it safely.

What worked better than offering help was just showing up. Not with a list and a plan, but just turning up. Being there. Having a cup of tea and a conversation before the practical things got done. People who feel seen accept help more easily than people who feel managed.

And for you, in the middle of all of this, please do not forget that you are also allowed to find it hard. You are allowed to grieve what is changing. The relationship with your parents is shifting into something new and that comes with loss even when it is handled well. Talk to someone about it. Your partner. A friend. A professional if you need one.

You are not just a dad. You are also someone's child. Both of those things are true at the same time, and both of them need tending to.
