This is History Uncovered. I'm John. And tonight I want to tell you about a war that no one talks about — fought in a place that barely exists on any map — that may have changed the entire course of the modern world.

In the year eighteen sixty-four, a country called Paraguay decided to go to war. And it decided to go to war against its three largest neighbours simultaneously. Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay. Together, those nations had a combined population of more than eleven million people. Paraguay had fewer than half a million. By any reasonable calculation, this was not a war Paraguay could survive. And it did not.

The man who launched this catastrophe was named Francisco Solano Lopez. He was the son of the previous Paraguayan dictator, educated in Europe, obsessed with Napoleon Bonaparte, and convinced that he was a military genius. He had modernised the Paraguayan army. He had built railways and telegraph lines. He had transformed his small, landlocked country into something that looked, on paper, like a serious regional power. And he believed, with absolute certainty, that he could win.

He was catastrophically wrong.

The war that followed lasted from eighteen sixty-four to eighteen seventy. Five years. And what happened to Paraguay in those five years is almost impossible to describe without resorting to words that feel too large. The country was invaded from three sides. Its cities were shelled. Its towns were burned. Its army, which fought with extraordinary and terrible courage, was ground down battle by battle, year by year, retreat by retreat, until there was almost no army left. Lopez conscripted children. He had boys as young as ten dressed in false beards and fake moustaches and sent them to the front lines. He executed suspected traitors without trial, and his circle of suspected traitors grew wider every month, until he was ordering the arrest and execution of people who had served him faithfully for decades, including members of his own family.

By the time the war ended, in eighteen seventy, the allied armies had chased Lopez into the jungle. He was cornered on the bank of a small river in the north of the country. He refused to surrender. An officer called out to him that he should give himself up. Lopez answered by charging with a spear. He was shot down, and killed, and the war was over.

And what was left of Paraguay?

The statistics that historians give for the aftermath of the War of the Triple Alliance are so extreme that scholars have argued about them for a hundred and fifty years. The most widely cited figures suggest that Paraguay lost somewhere between sixty and seventy percent of its total population. Not soldiers killed in battle. The entire population. Men, women, children. Killed by fighting, by disease, by starvation. By the time it ended, there were towns where not a single adult man remained alive. In some regions, the ratio of women to men was ten to one. The country that had gone to war was, for all practical purposes, erased. It would take generations to recover, and some historians argue it never fully did.

And the tragedy is compounded by this: no one made Paraguay do it. Lopez chose this. He chose war against impossible odds. He refused peace overtures when they were offered. And he drove his own people into the catastrophe with a certainty that was indistinguishable from madness.

The War of the Triple Alliance is the bloodiest war in the history of the Western Hemisphere. More proportionately devastating than anything Europe endured in the twentieth century. And almost no one outside South America has ever heard of it.

That silence is its own kind of wound.
