Welcome to Longevity Brief.

Today we are talking about NAD+. If you have been following health and longevity research over the past decade, you have almost certainly encountered this molecule. The claims can sound extravagant. But the underlying biology is genuinely compelling, and it is worth cutting through the hype to understand what we actually know.

NAD+ stands for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. It is a coenzyme found in every living cell, and it is central to how your cells produce energy. Think of it as a biological currency — it carries electrons between molecules during the metabolic processes that generate ATP, your cell's primary fuel source. Without adequate NAD+, your mitochondria — the energy-generating organelles in your cells — simply cannot do their job efficiently.

But NAD+ does far more than power metabolism. It is a critical substrate for a family of enzymes called sirtuins, which regulate DNA repair, gene expression, stress responses, and inflammation. It also activates PARP enzymes, which detect and repair DNA strand breaks. And it fuels CD38 and other enzymes involved in immune signaling.

Here is the central problem. NAD+ levels decline steeply with age. By the time a person reaches their fifties, NAD+ levels in many tissues are roughly half of what they were at twenty. This decline has been observed in muscle, liver, brain, and skin. The consequences cascade across nearly every system — reduced mitochondrial function, diminished DNA repair capacity, impaired sirtuin activity, and increased inflammatory signaling.

Why does NAD+ decline? A few reasons. First, as we age our cells consume more NAD+ to repair the accumulating DNA damage and manage inflammation. Second, the enzyme CD38 — which consumes NAD+ — becomes more active with age. Third, the production of NAD+ from its dietary precursors appears to become less efficient over time.

This has made NAD+ precursors one of the most commercially active areas of longevity supplementation. The two most studied are NMN, or nicotinamide mononucleotide, and NR, or nicotinamide riboside. Both are upstream precursors that the body converts to NAD+. Studies in mice showed impressive results — improved metabolic function, enhanced physical performance, better insulin sensitivity, and longer healthspan in some models. Human clinical trials are now accumulating.

Early human data suggests that oral NMN and NR supplementation can raise blood and tissue NAD+ levels. A two thousand twenty-three trial at Washington University in St. Louis showed that NMN supplementation raised NAD+ in muscle tissue of older adults. Modest improvements in physical performance measures were observed. However, we do not yet have long-term randomized controlled trials demonstrating that raising NAD+ in humans translates to meaningful reductions in disease or extension of lifespan.

What we do know is that NAD+ precursor supplementation appears to be safe at doses studied so far — generally two hundred fifty to one thousand milligrams per day for NMN.

Beyond supplementation, lifestyle factors powerfully influence NAD+ levels. Exercise is the most robust intervention. Resistance training and endurance training both increase NAD+ and sirtuin activity. Fasting and caloric restriction also raise NAD+ by reducing consumption and activating production pathways. Getting adequate B vitamins — particularly niacin, or vitamin B three — from your diet supports the NAD+ biosynthetic pathway from the ground up.

So where does this leave the practical recommendations?

Prioritize the lifestyle interventions first. Exercise regularly. Incorporate periods of time-restricted eating if that fits your health context. Eat a varied whole-food diet with adequate B vitamins — lean meats, fish, legumes, leafy greens, and fortified foods all contribute. If you are considering NMN or NR supplementation, the emerging evidence is encouraging but not yet conclusive. It is a reasonable bet for many people over fifty, but it is not a replacement for the foundational habits.

NAD+ is one of the most exciting molecular targets in longevity science. The coming decade of clinical research will tell us considerably more about whether supplementation delivers on its early promise.
