This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a man who threw parties for his neighbors, who dressed as a clown for sick children, who shook the hand of a first lady, and who buried the bodies of his victims beneath the floor of his own house. His name was John Wayne Gacy. To the people of his suburb outside Chicago, he was a friendly, hard-working contractor, a pillar of the community, the kind of man who organized the block party and ran the local political events. Beneath that mask, in the crawl space under his ordinary home, was one of the worst series of murders in American history. This is the story of how a respected neighbor was revealed to be a monster, and how the smell of his own house finally gave him away.

Gacy built his outward life carefully. He owned a construction business. He was active in his community organizations. He hosted lavish summer gatherings for hundreds of guests. And in his spare time, he performed at children's hospitals and neighborhood events in a clown costume he had designed himself, a character he called Pogo. He cultivated the image of a generous, civic-minded man, and for years, it worked. People liked him. People trusted him. That trust was the camouflage that let him hunt in plain sight.

His victims were young men and teenage boys. Many were workers he lured with the offer of a job at his construction company, or young men he encountered around the city. He would bring them to his house. And there, behind the door of that unremarkable suburban home, he killed them, one after another, over a period of years in the nineteen seventies. Then he hid the bodies in the one place he believed no one would ever look. The crawl space beneath his own floorboards. He buried victim after victim in the earth under his house, living above them, hosting his parties above them, for years.

The thread that finally pulled the whole thing apart was a single missing boy. In December of nineteen seventy-eight, a teenager named Robert Piest disappeared after telling his mother he was going to meet a contractor about a job. That contractor was John Wayne Gacy. The boy's family pushed hard, and the police, following the last person known to have spoken with him, turned their attention to Gacy. They began to watch him. They put him under surveillance. And the more they looked into his past, the more troubling it became. There had been complaints before. There had been young men who said they had been assaulted. There had been other disappearances connected, however loosely, to his name.

What truly broke the case was physical. When investigators obtained a warrant and entered the house, one of them noticed a smell, a foul, unmistakable odor rising from the crawl space. At first it was not understood for what it was. But when they finally dug into the earth beneath the home, the horror revealed itself. Body after body. Remains layered in the soil under the floor where Gacy lived his ordinary-seeming life. In the end, the remains of more than two dozen young men and boys were recovered, twenty-six from the crawl space and elsewhere on the property, with several more victims he had disposed of in a nearby river when, by his own account, he had run out of room to bury them. The final count came to thirty-three known victims. It was, at the time, the largest number of victims attributed to a single killer in the country's history.

When Gacy was confronted, he eventually confessed, in chilling and matter-of-fact detail, describing how he had carried out the killings. And yet, characteristically, he also spent years afterward trying to muddy the story, shifting blame, suggesting others had been involved, claiming at times that he could not be held responsible. The mask never entirely came off. Even in custody, he tried to manage how the world saw him, painting pictures, giving interviews, performing a version of himself for anyone who would watch.

His trial, in nineteen eighty, turned on the question of sanity. His defense argued that he was mentally ill, that he could not be held fully responsible for what he had done. The prosecution argued that the very carefulness of his crimes, the planning, the concealment, the double life maintained for years, showed a man who knew exactly what he was doing and worked hard to avoid being caught. The jury agreed with the prosecution. Gacy was convicted of thirty-three murders and sentenced to death. He spent fourteen years on death row before he was executed in nineteen ninety-four.

There is a particular kind of horror to the Gacy case that sets it apart, and it is not only the number of the dead, terrible as that is. It is the contrast. The clown costume. The block parties. The handshake with a first lady at a political event, captured in a photograph that still circulates today, the smiling community man standing beside a figure of national stature. The Gacy case forced people to confront, in the starkest possible terms, the idea that evil does not always announce itself, that it can wear a friendly face and live on a quiet street and dig graves under the very house where it hosts the neighbors for dinner.

In the years since, there has been painstaking work to identify victims who, for a long time, had no names. Advances in genetic testing have allowed investigators to return to the remains of young men buried for decades as unknowns, and to finally restore their identities, to give grieving families, after all this time, an answer. That work continues. Each name recovered is a small act of justice, a refusal to let these young men remain only the anonymous victims of a famous killer.

Because that is the danger with a case like this. The killer becomes the legend, the clown, the monster of a thousand documentaries, and the victims fade into a number. They were sons. They were brothers. They were young men with futures who crossed paths with a man whose friendliness was a trap. When we remember John Wayne Gacy, we should remember them first, and remember that the most dangerous mask of all is an ordinary, smiling, trusted face.

This has been True Crime Brief.
