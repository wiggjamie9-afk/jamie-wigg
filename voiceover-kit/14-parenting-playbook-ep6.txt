Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. I want to start today with two little words that come out of almost every parent's mouth a hundred times a day. Good job. Your child shows you a drawing, and you say it. They finish their dinner, and you say it. They climb to the top of the slide, and you say it. We mean it with our whole hearts. But today, I want to gently explore whether the way we praise our children is helping them as much as we think. Today's topic is praise versus encouragement.

Now, please don't hear this as, you've been doing it all wrong. You haven't. Praising your child comes from love, and love is never the wrong starting point. But there's some fascinating research about how different kinds of words land in a child's mind, and a few small shifts can make a surprisingly big difference to how confident and resilient your child becomes.

Let's begin with the heart of it. There's a researcher named Carol Dweck who studied something she called mindset. In simple terms, a child can grow up believing one of two things. Either, I am smart or talented, and that's a fixed thing I either have or don't. Or, I can get better at things by trying, learning, and sticking with them. The first is called a fixed mindset. The second is called a growth mindset. And here's the part that matters. The way we praise can quietly push a child toward one or the other.

Here's how. When we constantly say, you're so clever, you're so talented, you're so smart, we're praising the child for a fixed quality, something they didn't choose and can't control. It sounds wonderful. But over time, a child praised this way can become afraid of challenge, because if they fail, it might mean they're not clever after all. So they start avoiding hard things to protect the label. The praise meant to build them up can quietly make them fragile.

Now compare that to encouragement. Encouragement notices the effort, the strategy, the journey, not just the result. Instead of, you're so clever, you say, you worked really hard on that, and you didn't give up. Instead of, what a beautiful painting, you might say, you used so many colours there, tell me about it. Do you feel the difference? One labels the child. The other lights up the process. And the process is the thing they can actually repeat tomorrow.

So let me give you some principles to carry with you. One. Praise the effort, not the trait. Notice the trying, the practising, the persistence. You kept going even when it got tricky. That's the message that builds a child who isn't afraid to struggle.

Two. Be specific. A vague good job tells a child almost nothing. But, I noticed you shared your blocks with your friend even though you really wanted them, tells them exactly what they did well, and quietly invites them to do it again. Specific words are like a torch shining on the behaviour you want to grow.

Three. Notice the strategy. When a child solves a problem, you can say, how did you figure that out? This teaches them that the interesting part isn't being right, it's the thinking that got them there.

Four. Let some things go unpraised. This one surprises people. Not every single act needs a reward of words. If we cheer for everything, our praise becomes background noise, and children can become dependent on it, looking to us to know whether they should feel proud. Sometimes the most powerful thing you can say is simply, you must feel really proud of yourself. That hands the good feeling back to them, where it belongs.

Let me give you two quick scenes. Scene one. The child who gives up the moment something is hard, who says, I can't do it, and pushes it away. This is often a child who has learned that being good at things means it should come easily. Here, you gently celebrate the struggle itself. You say, this is the tricky part, and tricky is where your brain grows. Let's try one more way together.

Scene two. The child who constantly asks, is this good? Do you like it? Am I the best? This is a child who has learned to look outside themselves for their worth. Here, you gently turn the question back. What do you think of it? Which part are you most proud of? You're teaching them to carry their own approval inside.

And a note of reassurance. None of this means you should stop being warm, loving, and delighted by your child. Cuddles, joy, and I love you are never up for debate. This is only about the achievement words, the ones about doing and making and winning. Keep your affection flowing freely. Just aim your praise at the effort.

Here's my closing thought for tonight. The goal isn't to raise a child who needs us clapping in the front row for the rest of their life. The goal is to raise a child who can try hard things, fail, get back up, and feel proud of themselves from the inside. Your words are the seeds of that inner voice. So plant them with care. Be gentle with them, and be gentle with yourself. You're doing better than you think. I'll see you next time on Parenting Playbook.
