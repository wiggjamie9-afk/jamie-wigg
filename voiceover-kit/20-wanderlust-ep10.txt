Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we are going somewhere that arrives at you all at once, through every sense, before you have even had time to set down your bag. Vietnam. A long, slender country that curls down the eastern edge of Southeast Asia like a question mark laid on its side, hugging two thousand miles of coastline. Close your eyes and picture a street corner in the old quarter of Hanoi at six in the morning. The light is the colour of weak tea. A woman crouches over a charcoal brazier, fanning the coals, and the steam from her pot of broth rises and curls and disappears into the cool air. Motorbikes flow past in their thousands, an endless gentle river of them, and somehow it all works, and somehow no one is in a hurry. This is a country that runs on warmth, on noise, on the smell of star anise and fish sauce and rain on hot pavement.

Let me give you the shape of it, because Vietnam is long, and the temptation is to try to see all of it in ten days, and that is the one mistake you must not make. The country has three rough movements, like a piece of music. The north, with Hanoi and the limestone islands of Halong Bay and the green rice terraces climbing the hills near Sapa. The centre, where the old imperial city of Hue gives way to the lantern-lit town of Hoi An. And the south, hot and humid and electric, where Ho Chi Minh City crackles and the great Mekong Delta spreads out into water and rice and floating markets. Pick two of those three. Not all of them. Give yourself the gift of going slowly.

We begin in Hanoi. The old quarter is a maze of thirty-six ancient streets, each one named, centuries ago, for the trade once practised there. The street of silk. The street of tin. The street of bamboo. Get lost in it on purpose. Sit on one of those tiny plastic stools that come up to your knees, the ones that line every pavement, and order a bowl of pho. This is the soul food of the north, a clear beef broth simmered for many hours, with flat rice noodles and a fistful of fresh herbs you tear in yourself. It will cost you perhaps the equivalent of two dollars, and it will be better than almost any meal you have paid fifty times that for. In the late afternoon, find a balcony and order a cup of egg coffee, a Hanoi invention, dark coffee crowned with a whipped sweet cloud of egg yolk and condensed milk. It tastes like a warm custard and a strong espresso made peace and had a child.

From Hanoi, give two nights to the water. Halong Bay is the postcard, nearly two thousand limestone islands rising sheer out of jade-green sea, draped in jungle, hollowed with caves. But the postcard is busy now, so let me give you the local secret. Go instead, or as well, to Lan Ha Bay or Bai Tu Long Bay, just next door, quieter, with the same towering karst scenery and a fraction of the boats. Sleep one night on a small wooden junk, out among the islands, and wake before dawn. The mist sits low on the water, the great rocks float in it like sleeping animals, and for a few minutes the whole bay belongs to you alone.

Then travel to the centre, to Hoi An, and here I must simply tell you to slow down completely. Hoi An is a small, perfectly preserved old trading port, its narrow streets lined with mustard-yellow merchant houses, and every evening the whole town is lit by silk lanterns in red and gold and blue. People float paper lanterns down the river. It is unashamedly romantic and you should let it be. Hoi An is also the tailoring capital of the country. You can have a suit, a coat, a dress made to measure, fitted to your body, in a single day, for a price that will make you laugh. Get measured on your first morning and collect it on your last.

Now, the practical heart of the matter. Number one, the food. Eat on the street, always, where the queues are longest and the locals are oldest. The busiest stall is the safest stall, because the ingredients never sit. Look for the grandmother who has clearly been making one single dish on one single corner for thirty years. Number two, crossing the road. In the cities the traffic never stops, so you cannot wait for a gap, because there is no gap. The trick the locals know is this. Step off the kerb slowly and steadily, keep a constant, predictable pace, do not stop, do not run, do not panic, and the motorbikes will simply part around you like water around a stone. It feels insane the first time. By day three you will do it without looking up from your phone. Number three, the coffee culture. Vietnam is the second largest coffee producer on earth, and a Vietnamese iced coffee, dark and sweet over ice in the heat of the afternoon, is one of the great small pleasures of travel. Number four, bargain gently, and with a smile. In the markets the first price is an opening, not an insult. Offer a little over half, settle somewhere in the middle, and treat the whole thing as a friendly game, never a fight.

And here is the thing I want you to carry home from Vietnam. It is a country that was, within living memory, broken by war, and what you will meet instead is an almost overwhelming generosity. People will wave you over. People will feed you. People will laugh with you across a language you do not share. You will leave lighter than you arrived, and a little ashamed of how little it took to make you happy.

So your homework this week. Learn just three words before you go. Hello, which is xin chao. Thank you, which is cam on. And delicious, which is ngon. Say that last one to the woman at the food stall, point at your empty bowl, and watch what happens to her face. That single word will open more doors than any guidebook ever could.
