Dropping Them Off and Driving Home Alone. Welcome to Episode twenty three.

If you have not done it yet, let me describe the experience. You drive your kid to university. You carry boxes. You assemble flat-pack furniture that does not have enough screws. You meet the housemates and say something embarrassing. You take one last look at the room that now contains the entire known universe of your child's next chapter. And then you hug them, you say something like call us, which comes out approximately forty percent more strained than you intended, and you get in the car.

And then you drive home.

And the car is very, very quiet.

I have talked to a lot of dads about this moment and here is the interesting thing. Almost none of them were ready for it, even the ones who thought they were. Because the intellectual understanding that your kid is leaving is completely different from the physical reality of the empty passenger seat on the way back.

One dad I know pulled over on the motorway because he could not see properly. Not because something was wrong with his eyes. He was just not expecting the size of the feeling.

Here is what I want to say about that, directly and without any embarrassment: that feeling is not weakness. That is the invoice arriving for eighteen or so years of being somebody's most important person. Of course it hits hard. Of course it should.

What I think catches dads off guard is that nobody told us this part was coming. We got warnings about the nappies and the toddler tantrums and the homework battles and the teenage attitude. Nobody sat us down and said, by the way, one day you will walk out of a university dormitory and feel like a significant part of your identity just moved out with them. Prepare accordingly.

Because being Dad is not just a role. For a lot of us it became the organizing principle of the whole week. The reason the evenings had a shape. The reason you were home by a certain time. The reason Saturdays meant something. And when that shape changes, you notice the absence of it in ways you did not expect.

So what do you do?

First: let yourself feel it. Do not rush past it or stay busy to avoid it. It is telling you something true about what mattered. That is important information. Sit with it for a bit.

Second: redefine what being involved looks like now. You are not less of a dad because they have left the house. You are a different kind of dad. The one who sends a voice message with a recipe they liked. Who texts a dumb meme at exactly the right moment. Who picks up every single time they call. Who is available without being intrusive. That is an art form and it is worth getting good at it.

Third: talk to your partner about this if you have one, because they are going through their own version of it and the two of you can either grieve it together or past each other. Together is better.

And fourth: find something that is yours. Something that fills a bit of the newly available time in a way that is not just waiting for the phone to ring. Pick the thing you kept saying you would do when the kids were older. The time is now.

They will come home for breaks. They will call more often than you think, eventually. They will send you photos of the meal they cooked — the one that took them three attempts and a ruined pan — and it will be the best photo you have received in years.

The transition is real. The loss is real. And so is the next thing. Give yourself time to find it.
