Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

The Stoics had a very particular view of emotion, and it has been widely misunderstood. The popular image of the Stoic is someone who feels nothing, who walks through fire with a flat expression, who has traded their emotional life for a kind of philosophical numbness. That is almost the opposite of what they actually taught.

Marcus Aurelius felt grief. He felt frustration. He felt the pull of desire and the sting of ingratitude. What the Stoics were working toward was not the absence of feeling, but mastery of the response to feeling. They called it the discipline of assent: the gap between experiencing an emotion and deciding what to do with it.

Epictetus described it this way: Between stimulus and response there is a space. It is in that space that our freedom lives.

The Stoics observed that most suffering is caused not by events themselves, but by the judgements we attach to events. When something goes wrong, the first wave of response is often automatic: the frustration arrives, the anxiety spikes, the anger rises. But the second movement, the rumination, the catastrophising, the story we build around the initial feeling, that is where we have a choice. The first wave is natural. The second wave is the one we create.

They distinguished between what they called good emotions, which included joy, caution, and a form of wishing well for others, and what they called the passions, the distortions that arise when we mistake bad things for good or good things for bad. Anxiety, for example, is not wrong because it feels unpleasant. It becomes a problem when it is based on treating genuinely uncertain things as if they were certain catastrophes. Anger is not wrong because it is uncomfortable. It becomes destructive when it is based on the belief that we alone are entitled to a world that never frustrates us.

The practice is not suppression. The Stoics were explicit about this. Suppression, pushing feelings down, pretending they are not there, is not strength. It is a debt you defer. What they were building, through years of daily practice, was the capacity to feel an emotion fully and then respond from choice rather than from compulsion.

Here is today's practice. The next time you feel a strong emotion, whether it is irritation, worry, or the sharp desire to react, try to name what you are actually feeling before you do anything with it. Not to analyse it away, just to name it. I am feeling anger. I am feeling fear. I am feeling hurt. The naming creates the small distance the Stoics called the gap. In that gap, you are no longer the emotion. You are the one observing it. And from that position, response is possible.

Mastery is not the absence of feeling. It is feeling without being controlled by it.
