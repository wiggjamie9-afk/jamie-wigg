Welcome to Stoic Minute. I'm Harry.

Seneca offers a sharp little observation today, the kind that stings because it is accurate: We are more often frightened than hurt, and we suffer more from imagination than from reality.

Sit with how true that is. Run back over the things you have dreaded this past year. The difficult conversation, the bill, the diagnosis, the meeting you couldn't sleep before. How many of them, when they finally arrived, were as terrible as the fear had promised? A few, perhaps. But most were smaller than the shadow they cast. The dread was the heavier load, and we carried it for days before the thing itself took an hour.

Seneca's insight is that the mind is a fertile place for invented suffering. It rehearses disasters in vivid detail, none of which have happened, many of which never will. We pay, in advance and with interest, for catastrophes that never come. And even when the hard thing does arrive, we have already exhausted ourselves grieving it ahead of time, so we meet it depleted.

The Stoic remedy is not blind optimism. It is precision. When fear grips you, ask plainly what is actually happening right now, in this present moment, as opposed to what your imagination is staging for some future scene. Almost always, the present moment is bearable. It is the imagined future that is unbearable, and the imagined future is not real.

So here is today's practice. When you notice dread today, pause and separate the two. Name what is genuinely happening now. Then name what your mind has merely invented. Set the invention down. You are allowed to deal with trouble only when it actually arrives.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
