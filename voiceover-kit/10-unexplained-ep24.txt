Somewhere in the Pacific Ocean, beneath roughly five thousand meters of water, there are cities. Not metaphorical cities. Stone platforms, carved columns, ramps, and what appear to be roads, all resting on the seafloor off the coast of the Japanese island of Yonaguni. They were found in nineteen eighty-six by a diver named Kihachiro Aratake, who was searching for hammerhead sharks and instead discovered what looked, in the half-light of depth, like the ruins of something built.

The Yonaguni Monument, as it came to be called, is one of the most contested geological or archaeological sites on the planet. It covers an area of around sixty-six thousand square meters. It features right angles, flat surfaces, and terraced steps that repeat in patterns you do not commonly see in natural rock formations. There are what appear to be carved channels, and one section that researchers have described as resembling a large stage or ceremonial platform. A Japanese geologist named Masaaki Kimura spent years diving the site and became its most prominent advocate, arguing that the structures are the remnants of a lost civilization that flourished during the last ice age, when sea levels were substantially lower, and that the site was submerged approximately ten thousand years ago as the glaciers melted and the ocean rose.

The sceptical case is also strong. Many geologists who have studied the site argue that the Yonaguni Monument is entirely natural, the product of the way the local sandstone fractures. The rock around Yonaguni has a tendency to split along flat planes and right angles because of its natural grain. What looks like a carved step may simply be a fracture line. What looks like a road may be a natural joint. The ocean and its currents are capable of producing shapes that challenge the human instinct to read design into surfaces.

The argument hinges on one question: is there anything about the Yonaguni site that cannot be explained by natural geological processes? Kimura pointed to what he believed were tool marks and carvings, including what he described as a human face carved into the rock. Mainstream geology has largely pushed back on each of these claims, identifying natural analogs for most of the features.

But the timing is interesting regardless of which side is correct. Ten thousand years ago, when the site would have been submerged, something significant was already happening on this planet. The last ice age was ending. Human populations were growing. Agriculture was beginning in multiple places simultaneously. There are other submerged sites around the world, structures in the Gulf of Cambay off India, anomalous formations in the waters around Cuba, that have prompted similar debates. The possibility that coastal civilizations were wiped from the record by rising seas is not fringe speculation. It is something archaeologists take seriously, because the coasts were where people lived, and the coasts were what the sea reclaimed.

The Yonaguni Monument may be entirely natural. Or it may be the most significant submerged archaeological site on earth. As of today, no definitive answer has been accepted by the scientific community.

The sharks that Aratake was looking for when he found it still circle the formation. Whatever it is, they seem indifferent to the debate.
