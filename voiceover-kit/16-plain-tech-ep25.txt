Welcome to Plain Tech. Today's episode is for anyone helping a parent, grandparent, or older friend get more confident with technology — or for older listeners who want to feel more at home with their devices.

Technology can feel overwhelming when you did not grow up with it. The screens change, the menus move, and things that seemed straightforward suddenly require six steps. That frustration is completely understandable and it is not a sign that someone is not smart enough. It is usually a sign that the technology was not designed with them in mind.

So let's cover some practical things that make a real difference.

First: text size. This is one of the most common and most fixable problems. On both iPhones and Android phones, you can make all the text significantly larger. On an iPhone, go to Settings, then Display and Brightness, then Text Size. On Android, go to Settings, then Display, then Font Size. Dragging the slider up makes everything on screen larger and easier to read. There is also a separate setting called Bold Text on iPhones which makes all text heavier and clearer.

Second: brightness. Screens that are too dim are hard to read in daylight, and screens that are too bright can be uncomfortable indoors. Swiping down from the top of most phones gives you a quick brightness slider. Setting auto-brightness on can help the phone adjust automatically depending on the light around you.

Third: video calling. For many older adults, video calling has become the most important feature on their phone — more than social media or email. FaceTime works beautifully for Apple users. WhatsApp video calls work well across all devices and are free even for international calls. The key is practising once with a family member or friend so it feels familiar before you need it in a hurry.

Fourth: voicemail and answering calls. Spam calls are a genuine problem for everyone, but older adults are disproportionately targeted. A simple rule: if you do not recognise the number, let it go to voicemail. Legitimate callers will leave a message. No real bank, government agency, or utility company will demand immediate action by phone. If someone calls claiming to be from a bank or government department and asks for personal information, hang up and call the official number yourself.

Fifth: charging habits. A lot of phone anxiety comes from having a nearly dead battery. Getting into the habit of charging your phone overnight — the way you might charge a hearing aid — removes a lot of daily stress. Most modern phones handle overnight charging just fine.

Sixth: keeping things updated. Phone updates feel disruptive, but they fix problems and improve security. If you help an older person with their phone, helping them run updates when the phone prompts them is one of the kindest things you can do. On an iPhone you go to Settings, then General, then Software Update.

Finally, the best advice for supporting someone older with technology is patience and repetition. It often takes several times before something clicks. Write down steps in plain language and keep the paper near the device. And most importantly, make it about what they want to do — whether that is seeing photos of grandchildren, reading the news, or playing a word game — not about the technology itself.

Tech is a tool. The goal is connection and confidence, not mastery for its own sake.
