This is History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, a city under the ground — and the people who kept it alive for centuries.

In the region of Cappadocia, in what is now central Turkey, the landscape looks like another planet. Volcanic rock, shaped by millions of years of erosion into tall, thin spires called fairy chimneys, rising from a pale and dreamlike plateau. It is one of the most extraordinary terrains on earth. And beneath it, carved by human hands across many centuries, is something even more extraordinary. A city. Not a room, not a tunnel, not a shelter. A city, with streets and churches and wine cellars and stables and wells and ventilation shafts, all carved directly into the volcanic rock, descending level after level after level into the earth.

The largest of these is called Derinkuyu. And it is not fully explored even today.

Derinkuyu means deep well in Turkish. It was discovered in modern times in nineteen sixty-three, when a man renovating his house broke through a wall and found a passage leading down into the rock. Archaeologists who followed that passage found a carved underground city that descended at least eighteen levels below the surface, reaching a depth of roughly sixty metres. The deeper levels may go further still. The city could have sheltered as many as twenty thousand people, along with their animals, their food stores, and everything they needed to survive for months without coming to the surface.

There were schools carved into the rock. Places of worship. Kitchens, with the blackened ceilings that preserve the memory of ancient fires. Wine and oil presses. The remains of livestock pens. Massive stone discs, shaped like millstones, that could be rolled across the doorways to seal entire sections of the city from within — doors that could only be opened from the inside. Against an enemy who did not know the layout of the tunnels, who could not see in the dark, who could not reach the people sealed behind several tonnes of rolling stone, the underground city was almost impenetrable.

Who built it? The answer spans centuries. The volcanic rock of Cappadocia is unusually soft and easy to carve, and people had been making shelters in it since the Bronze Age. But the great underground cities seem to have been expanded most dramatically during periods of intense threat. The early Christian communities of the region are believed to have deepened and extended them during Roman persecution. Later, during the Arab raids of the seventh and eighth centuries, the local population retreated underground again, sealing themselves in while armies passed above. The rock kept them safe, kept them hidden, kept them alive.

And then the threat would pass. And they would climb back up into the light, and tend their fields, and press their grapes, and go back to whatever ordinary life they had before the signal fire warned them to go underground once more.

That is perhaps the most haunting aspect of this story. Not the engineering — though the engineering is extraordinary. Not the scale — though the scale is almost incomprehensible. But the way of life that these cities represent. The idea that for centuries, generation after generation, the people of Cappadocia lived with the knowledge that at any moment they might need to take their children and their animals and their wine and descend into the earth and pull a stone across the door and wait in the dark. That this was simply the texture of their lives. That they built permanent cities underground not as a curiosity, but as a survival strategy so necessary and so routine that they carved churches down there. Schools. Rooms for teaching children, in the dark, in the earth, while the world above them burned.

The underground cities of Cappadocia are open to tourists now. You can walk down into Derinkuyu on a summer afternoon, past the ventilation shafts and the wine presses and the millstone doors, and come back up into the sun. It takes about an hour.

It took the people who built it their entire civilisation.
