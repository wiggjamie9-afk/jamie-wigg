For three thousand years, a language lay dead on the walls of Egypt. The pyramids, the temples, the tombs of the pharaohs were covered in thousands upon thousands of beautiful little pictures, birds and eyes and snakes and reeds, and not a single living person on earth could read a word of them. The voices of an entire civilisation had fallen silent, locked behind a script no one could open. And then, by an accident of war and the obsession of a young man who taught himself a dozen languages, the lock was broken.

Welcome to History Uncovered. I'm John. Today, the cracking of the hieroglyphs.

For most of history, ancient Egypt was a riddle. Its monuments stood for thousands of years, vast and silent, and travellers gazed up at the carved symbols and could only guess what they meant. For a long time scholars believed the hieroglyphs were not really writing at all, but mystical symbols, each picture standing for an idea, a kind of sacred code of pure meaning. They thought a picture of an owl might mean wisdom, a picture of the sun might mean god. They were almost entirely wrong, and that single mistaken idea held back understanding for centuries. The truth was stranger and simpler. But to reach it, the world needed a key.

That key was found in the summer of the year seventeen ninety-nine, and it was found by accident. The French general Napoleon had invaded Egypt, bringing with him not only an army but a small crowd of scholars and scientists to study the wonders of the land. Near a town in the Nile delta called Rosetta, French soldiers were rebuilding an old fort, and as they tore down a crumbling wall, one of them noticed a slab of black stone built into it, covered in writing. He had the wit to realise it might matter, and he set it aside.

The stone was not beautiful. It was a broken, dark grey slab, a little over a metre tall, with a chunk missing from the top. But it carried something extraordinary. Upon its surface were three blocks of writing, three different scripts, one above the other. At the top, the mysterious hieroglyphs. In the middle, another unknown Egyptian script. And at the bottom, in clear, readable letters, ancient Greek. And ancient Greek was a language that scholars could read perfectly well.

The realisation was electric. If all three blocks of text said the same thing, and people knew the meaning, then the bottom panel was a translation of the top. The Greek was a dictionary for the hieroglyphs. Somewhere on that stone was the answer to a three thousand year old riddle. The Greek, it turned out, was a rather dull official decree from a council of priests, praising a young king. But it did not matter what it said. What mattered was that it said the same thing twice, in a language people knew and a language they did not.

And yet, having the key did not mean the lock turned easily. For more than twenty years, the finest minds in Europe struggled with the stone and failed. The problem was that old false idea, the belief that each picture was a symbol of meaning rather than a sound. As long as scholars searched for hidden ideas behind each little image, they got nowhere. An English scientist named Thomas Young made real progress. He correctly guessed that the oval rings carved around certain groups of symbols, called cartouches, contained the names of royalty, and that the symbols inside them might spell out sounds. But he could not let go of the old assumptions entirely, and he stalled.

The man who finally broke through was a Frenchman named Jean-François Champollion, and he had been preparing for this moment almost his entire life. As a boy he was a prodigy with languages, devouring them the way other children devour stories. By the time he was a teenager he had taught himself Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, Persian, and several others. And crucially, he had learned Coptic, the language of Egyptian Christians, which was the very last descendant of the ancient Egyptian tongue, still spoken in church services. He was, in a sense, the only man alive who could still hear an echo of how the people of the pharaohs had actually spoken.

His great breakthrough came in the year eighteen twenty-two. Studying the cartouches, he worked out that the hieroglyphs were not pure symbols and not pure sounds, but a mixture of both. Some pictures stood for sounds, like letters in an alphabet. Some stood for whole words or ideas. And some were there simply to tell the reader which kind of word was meant. It was a living, working system of writing, as flexible and as human as any other, not a mystical code at all. He proved it by spelling out royal names, sound by sound, and showing they matched the names known from Greek history. The story goes that when the final piece fell into place, he ran to his brother's office, cried out that he had done it, and collapsed, overcome, and did not wake properly for days.

The moment the script gave up its secret, an entire silent world began to speak. Suddenly the millions of inscriptions on every temple and tomb and scroll could be read. The Egyptians were no longer a mystery of stone faces and guesses. They had names and feelings and grievances and jokes. We could read their prayers and their love poems, their tax records and their school exercises, their letters to the dead and their complaints about lazy workmen. A doctor's notes on how to treat a wound. A father's pride in his son. A king's boast carved on a wall. Three thousand years of human voices, abruptly audible again after a silence of two millennia.

This is the strange power of a single object and a single mind. Without the chance survival of that broken black stone, built by accident into a fort wall and noticed by an alert soldier, the key might never have been found. Without a boy who could not stop collecting languages, the key might never have turned. The two had to meet, the lucky stone and the obsessed scholar, across more than twenty years and the chaos of a war, for the ancient world to find its voice.

So what does this teach us? Perhaps that the past is never quite as lost as it seems. The voices of the dead can wait a very long time, patient on the walls, for someone with the right key and the stubbornness to use it. And perhaps it teaches us something about how knowledge advances at all. For centuries, brilliant people stared at the hieroglyphs and saw nothing, because they were looking with the wrong idea in their heads. The truth was there the whole time, plain on the stone. It only needed someone willing to set aside what everyone believed, and look again.
