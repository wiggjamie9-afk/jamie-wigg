Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

The Stoics had a word for what they considered the highest human capacity. Logos. It is usually translated as reason, but it carries more weight than that English word tends to hold. Reason, in the Stoic sense, is not just calculation. It is the ability to step back from an impulse, to see clearly what is actually happening, to respond to reality as it is rather than as fear or desire is insisting it is.

Marcus Aurelius returns to this capacity again and again in his journals. In one passage he writes: You have power over your mind, not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength.

This is not the strength of suppression, the kind that grinds its teeth and white-knuckles its way through difficulty. The Stoics were explicitly against that misreading. They were not asking people to become stones, to feel nothing, to march through life with a locked jaw. What Marcus is describing is something subtler and more available than that. It is the strength that comes from seeing clearly.

The ordinary experience of a difficult emotion is that it feels like the whole world. Anger, in the moment, seems completely justified and absolutely urgent. Anxiety, at its height, feels like an accurate report on a genuine catastrophe. The emotion occupies everything. What Stoic practice does, very gradually, is create a small gap between the feeling and the full occupation. You still feel the anger. But you also notice that you are feeling it. And in that noticing, a choice becomes possible.

The ancient word askesis, from which we get ascetic, meant practice, or exercise. The Stoics used it to describe the daily work of strengthening this capacity. Like any skill, it is built through repetition. You practice noticing one impulse before you act on it today. You practice seeing one situation with a degree of detachment, and then letting that practice compound over weeks and years until the skill becomes natural.

Here is today's practice. At some point today you will feel a strong pull in one direction, impatience, frustration, anxiety, the urge to say the sharp thing or check the phone for the twelfth time. When you feel it, try to add one word to your inner experience: noticing. I am noticing frustration. I am noticing the urge to react. The noticing does not have to change the outcome immediately. It just creates the gap. Over time, the gap is everything.

The strength Marcus describes is not dramatic. It is quiet, daily, and completely within reach.
