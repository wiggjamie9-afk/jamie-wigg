Hello, this is Austin, and you're listening to Unexplained.

Tonight we go to the desert of Peru, to a high, dry plateau where almost no rain has fallen in living memory. From the ground, you would see nothing but scattered stones and pale earth stretching to the horizon. But rise into the air, climb a few hundred feet, and the ground beneath you transforms. Lines. Hundreds of them. Vast figures carved into the desert floor, a hummingbird, a monkey, a spider, a creature with a hundred straight lines radiating out like the spokes of some impossible wheel. Some of these figures are larger than a football field. They have lain here for around two thousand years. And no one is entirely certain why they were made. These are the Nazca Lines.

The Nazca Lines were created by the Nazca people, who lived along the southern coast of Peru from roughly the centuries on either side of the year zero. The technique itself is no great secret. The desert floor is covered in a layer of dark, iron-stained stones, and just beneath them lies pale, lighter ground. By carefully removing the dark stones and piling them at the edges, the Nazca exposed the light earth below, drawing lines and shapes the way you might trace a finger through dust. Because this is one of the driest and most windless places on Earth, the marks have survived for two thousand years, barely touched.

There are three broad kinds of figures. First, there are the straight lines and the great geometric shapes, long perfectly straight tracks that run for miles across the desert, along with triangles and trapezoids, some so large and so even that they look as if they were cut by a machine. Second, there are the biomorphs, the famous animal and plant figures, the spider, the monkey with its curling tail, the hummingbird, the condor, drawn in a single unbroken line. And third, there are stranger, humanlike figures on the hillsides, including one wide-eyed figure that some have nicknamed the astronaut. They are extraordinary not only for their size but for their precision. Standing among them, you cannot even see them. They only resolve into shapes from high above. And that single fact, that they seem made to be seen from the sky, is the root of nearly every theory about them. So let us do what we always do. Let us set the wonder aside and rank the explanations by the weight of the evidence.

The strongest explanations come from archaeology, and they are grounded in patient fieldwork rather than spectacle. The leading view today is that the lines were deeply tied to water and to ritual. In a desert this brutal, water was everything, and the survival of the Nazca depended on underground streams and seasonal flows. Many researchers believe the lines were sacred pathways, walked in religious processions, perhaps as ceremonies to call for rain and fertile fields. Pottery and offerings have been found along some of the lines, and several appear to point toward sources of water or places of ritual importance. In this view, the figures were not meant for distant eyes at all. They were meant for the gods, and for the feet of the people who walked them in prayer.

Then comes the next tier, the natural but more specialised ideas, which sit comfortably alongside the first. One long-standing theory holds that the lines form a giant astronomical calendar, that certain lines align with the rising and setting of the sun, the moon, or particular stars, marking the seasons in a place where knowing when the water would come could mean life or death. Careful studies have found that some lines do seem to align with solstices and significant celestial events, though others insist that with so many lines pointing in so many directions, some alignments are bound to occur by chance. The truth is probably layered. The Nazca almost certainly used these figures for more than one purpose, ritual and water and sky woven together, the way sacred things so often are. There is also the simplest practical question, of how they achieved such precision. And experiments have shown it is entirely possible with the tools of the time, wooden stakes, cords, and careful sighting along straight lines, no advanced technology required.

Then there is the third tier, the further-out idea, the one the evidence supports least but which has captured the popular imagination most completely. It is the claim, made famous in the nineteen sixties and seventies, that because the figures can only be appreciated from the air, they must have been made for, or even by, visitors from the sky. Landing strips for ancient spacecraft. Messages to beings above. It is a seductive idea, and it explains the one haunting feature, the view from above, in a single dramatic stroke. But it collapses under scrutiny. The straight lines are scraped only inches deep into soft ground, useless for any landing craft. The animal figures are drawn in flowing single lines, like art, not like runways. And we know who made them, the Nazca, with their pottery, their tools, and their gods. The theory survives not because the evidence supports it, but because it flatters us, by suggesting that something so grand could not have been the work of ordinary human hands.

So where does that leave us? Weigh it honestly, and the most likely answer is the human one, and it is in no way diminished by being human. A people living at the edge of survival, in one of the harshest landscapes on Earth, carved enormous sacred images into the desert, walked them in ceremony, watched the sky for the rhythms of the seasons, and prayed for the water that meant life. They did not need spaceships. They needed rain. And they expressed that need on a scale so vast that the only proper audience was the heavens.

And perhaps that is the part that truly unsettles us. Not that the lines are alien, but that they are so completely human, and yet so beyond what we expect of human hands two thousand years ago. We look down from an aeroplane window at a hummingbird the size of a stadium and we feel the same vertigo the legend feeds on, the suspicion that ordinary people could not have done this. But they did. With cord and stake and an enormous patience, and a faith that someone above was watching.

We are drawn to the mystery because it sits exactly where our certainty ends and our wonder begins. We can date the pottery. We can map the lines. We can copy the methods. But we cannot quite stand in the mind of a Nazca walking that spiral at dawn, and know what the gods meant to them, or what they were asking for as they walked. That space, the one we cannot reach, is where the legend lives. And it is why, two thousand years later, we keep climbing into the sky above the desert, looking down, still trying to read a message that was never written for us.
