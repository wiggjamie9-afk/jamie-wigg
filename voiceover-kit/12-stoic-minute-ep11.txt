Welcome to Stoic Minute. I'm Harry.

Marcus Aurelius offers us a sentence today that reads like an instruction to himself, scrawled in the margin of a hard day: Waste no more time arguing about what a good man should be. Be one.

There is something bracing in how little patience it shows. Marcus, the most powerful man in the world, had every excuse to theorise, to debate, to wait for the perfect understanding before acting. Instead he cuts it all away. Stop discussing virtue. Practise it. The argument about goodness has a way of becoming a substitute for goodness itself.

We know this temptation well. It is easier to read another book about discipline than to do one hard thing. Easier to talk about the kind of person we want to become than to behave, today, as that person would. The mind loves preparation because preparation feels productive while costing us nothing. But character is not built in the planning. It is built in the doing, in the small unglamorous choice made when no one is watching.

The Stoics judged a philosophy not by how well you could explain it, but by how it showed up in your conduct. Epictetus said do not explain your philosophy, embody it. A good man, in their eyes, was not the one with the finest theory of goodness. It was the one who was patient when patience was costly, honest when honesty was inconvenient, kind without an audience.

So here is today's practice. Pick one virtue you keep meaning to grow into. Patience, honesty, generosity. Then, just once today, act it out in a single concrete moment. Don't define it. Be it.

Remembering that you will die is, strangely, one of the most life-giving things you can do. It returns you to the one day you're actually holding: this one.
