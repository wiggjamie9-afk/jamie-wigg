Picture a beach town in Costa Rica where the surf breaks just outside the village. The fishermen are out before dawn. The morning coffee is grown on the slopes behind the town. And the hotels — all three of them — are small, locally owned, and careful about the water they use and the food they serve. Tourism is happening here. But it is happening gently.

This is sustainable tourism in practice. Not a marketing campaign. Not a certification badge in a lobby. A genuine commitment by a community to welcome visitors in a way that does not cost the place its soul.

Sustainable travel has moved from niche concern to mainstream conversation, and for good reason. Tourism is one of the world's largest industries, responsible for around eight percent of global carbon emissions when you include aviation. The infrastructure that supports mass tourism — the cruise ship terminals, the all-inclusive resorts, the souvenir factories — can overwhelm fragile ecosystems and price local people out of their own neighbourhoods. Venice flooded with visitors. Barcelona's residents protesting in the streets. Bali's sacred sites used as backdrops for content. The consequences of getting this wrong are visible.

But sustainable travel is not about guilt, and it is not about travelling less. It is about travelling differently.

The most tangible shift is where you put your money. Staying in locally owned accommodation keeps your spending in the community. Eating at family-run restaurants supports farmers and cooks rather than multinational chains. Hiring local guides — people with genuine knowledge and genuine stakes in protecting what they show you — enriches your experience while building livelihoods. These choices matter. Tourism dollars have an address, and that address determines who benefits.

Transport choices shape your carbon footprint more than almost anything else. Flying is unavoidable for long international journeys, but once you are somewhere, trains, buses, ferries, and bicycles can carry you far. Choosing a direct flight over a connecting one makes a meaningful difference, since takeoff and landing burn the most fuel. Slower ground travel between destinations is not just lower-impact — it often yields the best views and the most interesting conversations.

Overtourism is a term we hear a lot, but the solution is more nuanced than simply avoiding popular places. It is about timing, distribution, and behaviour. Visiting famous sites in the off-season reduces pressure and often improves your own experience. Going just slightly off the beaten path — the village next to the famous one, the valley rather than the peak — spreads economic benefit while protecting the most vulnerable places.

And then there is the practice of leaving places better than you find them. Participating in a local beach cleanup. Learning a few words of the language. Not buying products made from wildlife or stolen heritage. Being a guest who is genuinely interested in the place, rather than just passing through it.

The world's most remarkable places need travellers who love them thoughtfully. That is the heart of it. Go. But go like you plan to come back.
