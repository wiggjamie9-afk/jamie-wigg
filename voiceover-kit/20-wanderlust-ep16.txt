Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we're going somewhere most people spell incorrectly and even more people mispronounce. Today we're going to Kyoto.

Not the Kyoto of a million travel photographs, all cherry blossoms and filtered light, though that Kyoto exists and it is worth every cliche. I'm talking about the Kyoto that sits underneath the tourist surface. The Kyoto that wakes up before you do, that smells of incense and cedar and morning river air, that reveals itself only to people willing to walk slowly and look sideways.

Kyoto was Japan's imperial capital for over a thousand years. That's a thousand years of palaces, temples, garden designs, tea ceremonies, geisha districts, and craft traditions accumulating in a single city. When Tokyo became the political centre in the eighteen hundreds, Kyoto was left largely untouched. World War Two largely spared it as well. What survived is the most intact version of old Japan that exists anywhere on earth.

There are over sixteen hundred Buddhist temples in Kyoto. Sixteen hundred. You could spend a week doing nothing but temples and still not finish. But here's the thing no guidebook tells you clearly enough: most people crowd into the same six. The famous ones. Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion. Fushimi Inari, with its thousands of red torii gates snaking up the mountain. They are extraordinary, yes. Go to them. But go early. The Golden Pavilion at six in the morning, when mist is still sitting on the water and there are perhaps a dozen people there instead of two thousand, is a completely different experience from the Golden Pavilion at noon.

The neighbourhoods are where Kyoto earns its reputation. Gion, in the east, is the geisha district. Its streets are made of old wooden machiya townhouses, narrow lanes, and paper lanterns glowing at dusk. Walk Hanamikoji Street at around five in the evening, and if you are very quiet and very patient, you may see a maiko, an apprentice geisha, moving quickly between engagements. Do not follow her. Do not push a camera in her direction. Simply appreciate that you are standing in an unbroken cultural tradition stretching back centuries.

Nishiki Market is called Kyoto's kitchen, and the name is accurate. It is a covered arcade about six hundred metres long, packed tightly with stalls selling pickled vegetables, fresh tofu, grilled skewers, matcha sweets, miso paste, and several things you will not recognise. This is where the city's chefs have shopped for four hundred years. Eat everything you can reach. Budget about two thousand yen and spend an hour.

The bamboo grove at Arashiyama is one of those places that photographs cannot capture. You walk into a corridor of bamboo that towers fifteen metres above your head on both sides, and the sound changes. Wind through bamboo is a very particular sound, a soft hollow percussion that feels ancient and calming in a way you do not expect. Go at seven in the morning before the tour buses arrive. Bring nothing but yourself.

Practical notes for Kyoto. Stay in a traditional guesthouse if your budget allows, even for one night. A proper ryokan includes a tatami room, a yukata robe to wear, a multi-course kaiseki dinner, and a communal hot spring bath. It is expensive but worth understanding at least once. Budget options exist in guesthouses clustered around the Kyoto train station and along the Kamo River.

The city is best explored by bicycle. Flat, grid-planned in its core, and dotted with shrines and gardens you will stumble across by accident. Rent a bike for a day and let yourself get slightly lost.

Kyoto is a city that rewards patience over efficiency. The person who slows down, sits at a tea house by a garden pond for forty minutes doing nothing productive, and walks home through the wrong street will see more of the real city than the one who checks off temples on a list.

Your homework this week: Find one lesser-known temple in Kyoto. Ginkaku-ji, the Silver Pavilion. Ryoan-ji and its famous rock garden. Daitoku-ji, a vast Zen complex most visitors walk past entirely. Pick one, go in the early morning, and give it an hour with nothing else planned afterward.
