Welcome back to Dating Decoded, the show where we take the messy, confusing world of modern love and look at what's actually going on underneath. I'm your host. Today we're talking about one of the most common fault lines in relationships — commitment timing. How do you know when you're ready to take the next step? And what happens when one person is ready before the other?

Let me start by naming the thing clearly. The next step looks different depending on where you are. For some people it's making things official. For others it's moving in together, or getting engaged, or deciding whether to start a family. What these milestones have in common is that they require a level of intentionality that early dating doesn't. You're not just enjoying someone's company anymore. You're choosing them specifically, for something that matters.

And the question of when is genuinely complicated, because readiness is not a single thing. There are people who are ready for commitment on paper but not emotionally — they have the stable life, the job, the apartment, and still feel a kind of low-level panic at the thought of choosing one person. There are people who feel emotionally ready but whose circumstances are genuinely not — they're still working through something significant, still rebuilding, still in a transition that would be unfair to bring a partner into fully. And there are people who say they're not ready who, if you pressed them honestly, are ready for a relationship in general but not quite sure about this particular person.

That last category is the one nobody talks about directly, and it's probably the most common source of commitment timing conflicts.

Here's something worth sitting with if you're the one being asked to wait. The phrase I'm not ready yet is almost always incomplete. Not ready for what, specifically? Not ready to commit to anyone? Not ready to commit to you? Not ready right now but on a clear path to being ready by a specific point? These are very different situations, and they deserve very different responses from you.

It is completely reasonable to ask for more specificity. Not as a pressure tactic, but because you are a person with your own timeline and your own needs, and you deserve to have enough information to make your own decisions. If you have been in an undefined relationship for two years and the answer to when is a vague soon or I just need more time, that is not actually an answer. You are allowed to ask what soon means, and you are allowed to factor the answer into what you do next.

Let me say something that feels obvious but often gets lost in the fog of loving someone. Waiting indefinitely for someone to be ready is a choice. And it is only a good choice if the thing you're waiting for is genuinely on its way. Hope is not a plan. Patience is a virtue right up until the point where it becomes self-abandonment.

Now let's talk about the other side of this — what if you are the one who is not ready?

If you're in a relationship where someone is asking for more commitment than you currently feel able to give, the most honest and kind thing you can do is be specific about why. Not I'm not ready, full stop. But something more like: here is what's going on for me. Here is what I'm working through. Here is what I actually need before I can show up fully. That conversation is harder, but it respects the other person's ability to decide what they want to do with that information. Keeping someone in limbo with vague reassurances, while buying yourself time at their expense, is not considerate. It is a way of getting what you want — their continued presence — without giving them what they need — honesty about where this is going.

Sometimes the honest answer is that you're not sure this is the right relationship. That is one of the hardest things to say, particularly when you genuinely care about the person. But caring about someone and choosing them as a long-term partner are not the same thing, and confusing the two is how people end up in relationships that drift for years without ever becoming what one person needed them to be.

What does actual readiness look like? Not perfect. Not all circumstances aligned. Not no fear. Fear is normal. Choosing to commit to someone is genuinely consequential and your nervous system knows that. What readiness looks like is: I want this person, I am choosing them, and I am prepared to show up for the work that this requires. It is a decision, not a feeling that simply arrives and removes all doubt. Waiting until every doubt disappears is often a way of never having to choose at all.

For couples who find themselves misaligned on timing — one ready, one not — the thing that tends to determine outcomes is whether both people can be honest about what they actually want. If both people want the same destination and are just on slightly different timelines, there is usually a path. If what one person wants and what the other is willing to offer are fundamentally different, the kindest thing for both of them is to stop pretending otherwise.

Love is not usually the issue in these situations. People who are misaligned on commitment timing often genuinely love each other. The issue is the future. And you cannot build a shared future with someone who imagines a different one.
