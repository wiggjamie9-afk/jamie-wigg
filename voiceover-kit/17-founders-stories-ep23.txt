Welcome to Founders' Stories.

When Priya Chandrasekaran first walked into a venture capital meeting in two thousand and fourteen, the partner across the table asked her who else was on the founding team.

She said it was just her.

He nodded slowly and wrote something on his notepad. She never found out what.

She didn't get the funding.

Priya had spent twelve years as a software engineer before she founded her company — a platform that helped small hospitals in South and Southeast Asia manage medication inventory without expensive enterprise software. She understood the problem from the inside. She had grown up watching her father, a doctor in Chennai, work in facilities that lost medication to spoilage and mismanagement every single year. She knew exactly what she was building and exactly who it was for.

But what she heard, again and again in those early meetings, was some version of the same question dressed in different clothes. The question was never asked directly. It didn't need to be. It lived in the slight hesitation before a handshake, in the way certain investors talked about leadership, in the questions about whether she had thought about hiring a chief executive with more business experience. She heard those questions as what they were.

She raised a small angel round from a network of South Asian professionals who had been through their own versions of the same experience. She built the product in two years. She launched in three hospital networks in two thousand and sixteen. By two thousand and nineteen she had partnerships with forty-seven facilities across four countries.

I asked her what made the difference. She said two things.

The first was finding her actual customers before finding her investors. Her customers — the procurement staff, the nurses, the administrators — they didn't ask about her background or her team composition. They asked whether the software worked, whether it was fast, whether it would still function when the internet connection was patchy, and whether she would pick up the phone when something went wrong. She answered yes to all four. The product proved it. Word spread.

The second thing was building a community of founders who looked like her and sharing everything she knew. She joined — and eventually helped run — a network of women in health-tech across South Asia. Not as a networking exercise. As an act of infrastructure. She understood that information is power, and that women in her position were routinely denied access to the informal networks where deals are made and knowledge passes between people who trust each other.

She told me something I've been thinking about since. She said: "Closed doors don't stay closed forever. But you have to keep building while you wait for them to open. If you stop building, they win."

Priya eventually raised a Series A in two thousand and twenty from a fund specifically focused on health technology in emerging markets. The lead partner told her the forty-seven facilities across four countries made the decision straightforward. She had simply built her proof in a different room.

What she had, the whole time, was undeniable evidence. And she understood that patient, methodical proof-building is sometimes the most powerful argument available when the conventional doors aren't opening.
