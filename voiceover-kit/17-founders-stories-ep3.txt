# PODCAST #17: FOUNDERS' STORIES — Episode 3
**Voice to use:** Daniel (engaging storyteller)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Daniel

---

Before we begin, a quick note. The story I'm about to tell you is assembled from widely-shared public accounts, interviews, and reporting that have circulated for years. It's not insider reporting, and I wasn't in the room. So treat it the way you'd treat any good story that's been told and retold. The shape of it is true, even if a few details have been polished smooth by time.

Now. Picture two guys in San Francisco, late two thousand and seven. Their names are Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia. They'd met years earlier at design school in Rhode Island, and they'd made a pact, the kind of pact that sounds romantic when you're young and terrifying when the rent is due. The pact was that one day they'd start a company together. Something that mattered.

So Brian packs up his life, drives across the country, and moves into Joe's apartment in San Francisco. And almost immediately, reality arrives. The landlord raises the rent. Brian has, by some accounts, about a thousand dollars to his name, and that rent increase is more than he can cover. He's broke. Genuinely broke. The kind of broke where you start counting what's in the fridge.

And here's where the story turns, because this is the part everybody remembers. There was a big design conference coming to San Francisco that week. Every hotel in the city was sold out. Designers were flying in from all over the world and they had nowhere to stay. And Joe has this thought. A slightly ridiculous thought. He says, what if we just put some air mattresses on the floor of our apartment and rent them out to people who can't find a hotel room?

They had three air mattresses. They blew them up. They threw together a quick little website and called it, I am not making this up, AirBed and Breakfast. Air mattress, plus breakfast in the morning. That was the whole pitch. A floor to sleep on and some cereal when you wake up.

And it worked. Three people paid to sleep on those air mattresses. A woman from Boston, a guy from Utah, a father of four from India. And Brian and Joe didn't just take the money. They made these strangers breakfast, they showed them around the city, they treated them like guests. And somewhere in that weekend, the two of them looked at each other and thought, wait. There might be something here. This wasn't just a way to make rent. This was a way for anybody, anywhere, to turn their spare room into a small business, and for travelers to feel at home in a strange city.

So they bring in a third co-founder, an engineer named Nathan Blecharczyk, and they start building it for real. And I want to be honest with you about what happened next, because the myth of the overnight success is a lie, and this story is the best medicine for that lie that I know.

Nobody wanted it. Investors thought it was insane. Strangers, sleeping in strangers' homes? They pitched and they pitched and they got rejection after rejection. There's a famous moment Brian has talked about, where he kept the actual rejection emails from investors who passed. Polite nos. We don't think this is a fit. Interesting, but not for us. They were drowning. They had maxed out credit cards. They were tens of thousands of dollars in debt. The company was, by any rational measure, dying.

And here comes my favorite part of the entire story. The cereal.

There was a presidential election heating up at the time, two thousand and eight, Barack Obama against John McCain. And the founders, desperate and creative and a little bit out of their minds, decided to make novelty breakfast cereal. They designed special-edition boxes. Obama O's, the breakfast of change. And Cap'n McCain's, a maverick in every bite. They got the boxes printed, they assembled them by hand, gluing them together late at night, and they sold them as collector's items around the convention season.

And it worked. They sold something like a thousand dollars' worth of cereal, by some accounts they cleared around thirty thousand dollars total from the whole stunt. They funded their failing tech startup by selling cereal. Let that sit with you for a second. When the front door is locked, sometimes you have to climb in through a window made of breakfast cereal.

That cereal, oddly enough, is part of what got them into Y Combinator, the famous startup program. The story goes that the founders showed Paul Graham, who ran it, those cereal boxes, and Paul basically said, if you can convince people to pay forty dollars for a four-dollar box of cereal, maybe you can convince strangers to sleep in each other's homes. They got in.

And inside Y Combinator, Paul Graham gave them a piece of advice that I think about constantly. They were trying to grow, trying to get millions of people to sort of half-care about their product. And Paul told them something close to this: it's better to have a hundred people who love you than a million people who only kind of like you. Don't chase the lukewarm crowd. Go find the few who genuinely love what you're doing, and make them ecstatic.

So they asked themselves, where are our people? And the answer was New York. They had listings there, but nothing was taking off. And the photos on those listings were terrible. Dark, blurry phone pictures of rooms that looked like nobody would ever want to sleep in them.

So they did the most un-tech thing imaginable. They got on a plane. They went door to door, host to host, in New York City, with a rented camera, and they re-photographed the listings themselves. Beautiful, bright, warm photos. And almost immediately, bookings in New York started to climb. They later said it was one of the most important things they ever did, and it broke every rule of how a software company is supposed to scale. You're not supposed to fly across the country and knock on doors. You're supposed to write code that does it for you. But the code couldn't do it yet, so they did it with their own two feet.

And from there, slowly, then quickly, the thing took off. The name got shortened to something snappier, Airbnb. The air mattresses gave way to entire homes, castles, treehouses, apartments in every city on earth. The company that no investor wanted became one of the most valuable travel companies in the world, worth tens of billions of dollars. Those rejection emails became, in hindsight, some of the most expensive nos ever sent.

So before I let you go, let me pull out three lessons. And I've picked them carefully, because not one of them requires money. That's the point.

Lesson one. Your problem might be everyone's problem. Brian and Joe weren't trying to build an empire. They were just trying to make rent. But the cleverest solution to your small, embarrassing, immediate problem is very often a solution a million other people are quietly desperate for too. Don't be ashamed of starting from need. Need is honest. Need is where the best ideas hide.

Lesson two. When the door is locked, find the window. They sold cereal. Cereal. When the obvious path to survival was closed, they didn't sit in the dark waiting for permission. They got resourceful and a little weird. Whatever you're building, there is almost always a strange, scrappy, slightly undignified way to buy yourself one more month. Pride is expensive. Survival is free.

And lesson three, the one I love most. Do things that don't scale. Get on the plane. Knock on the door. Take the photo yourself. A hundred people who love you will always be worth more than a million who shrug. In the early days of anything, whether it's a business, a craft, a friendship, the unglamorous, hands-on, personal work is the work that matters. You can't automate love. You have to show up for it.

Two broke designers, three air mattresses, and a box of cereal with a president's face on it. That's where it started. Remember that the next time the rent is due and the answer feels impossible.

I'm so glad you spent this time with me. Take care of yourself, and I'll see you on the next one.
