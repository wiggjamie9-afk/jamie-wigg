Welcome back to Plain Tech. Really glad you are here today.

We are talking about fake reviews. Specifically, how to tell the difference between a real review written by a real person who actually used something, and a fake one that was paid for, automated, or simply made up. This matters because reviews are probably the main way most of us decide what to buy, and the system has been gamed badly.

Let me give you a quick picture of how it happens. When a product gets good reviews, it sells more. When it sells more, the seller makes more money. So there is a very strong financial incentive to get good reviews by any means. Some sellers pay people in exchange for a positive review and a refund on the product. Some use software to generate hundreds of fake reviews at once. Some offer a free product in exchange for a five-star rating. The result is that in certain categories on major shopping sites, the review section is basically unreliable. You cannot just look at the star rating and trust it.

So how do you tell?

The first thing to look at is the pattern of reviews over time, not just the total. A product that has no reviews for two years and then suddenly gets five hundred five-star reviews in one week is almost certainly a fake flood. A product that has been slowly collecting reviews over many months, with a mix of ratings, looks much more real. Some sites let you see a chart of reviews over time, which makes this very easy to spot.

The second thing is the language. Fake reviews tend to have a few telltale signs. They are often generic. They use the full product name in an odd way, as if they are filling in a template. They go into detail about shipping and packaging but not about whether the thing actually works. Real reviews usually say something specific — this broke after three weeks, the zipper was stiff but the fabric is great, the battery does not last as long as they say. Specific complaints and specific praise are signs of a real human.

Reviews that all sound oddly similar are also a flag. If ten reviews use almost the same phrasing, they were probably written from the same template or by the same source.

Third, look at one-star and two-star reviews, and look at what they say. Every product has some unhappy customers. A product with no negative reviews at all is suspicious. But also read the ones that are there. If the main complaints are all about the same thing — the stitching fails, the battery dies, the app does not work — that is a pattern worth knowing about before you buy.

There are some free tools and browser add-ons designed to do some of this analysis automatically. I will not name specific brands today, as the market changes fast, but if you search for something like review analysis checker you will find options. They can show you what percentage of a product's reviews look suspicious, all in one click. A useful extra layer if you are spending serious money.

Beyond individual product reviews, here are a couple of broader habits that help.

Buy from a retailer with a clear and easy returns policy. Even if a product turns out to be less than advertised, being able to return it without a fight removes most of the sting. Read their returns policy before you buy, not after.

For bigger purchases, look beyond the shopping site. Search the product name along with the word review or with the word problems and see what comes up. Real users often share their experiences in forums, Facebook groups, subreddits, and tech blogs. These communities are much harder to flood with fake feedback than a single shopping site.

And for things like appliances, gadgets, or tools, independent review sites and consumer organisations often test products themselves and publish their own verdicts. These are not paid for by the manufacturers and are generally much more trustworthy than any review section on a shopping site.

To wrap up. Do not trust star ratings at face value. Look for a natural spread of reviews over time, not a sudden flood. Look for specific, human-sounding details, not generic packaging praise. Read the one-star and two-star reviews for patterns. Use a review-checker tool for expensive purchases. Search outside the shopping site for real-world experiences. And check the returns policy before you buy.

Your homework today: next time you are about to buy something online, before you click purchase, spend two minutes reading the one-star reviews and see if there is a pattern. That two minutes has saved me from some very disappointing purchases.
