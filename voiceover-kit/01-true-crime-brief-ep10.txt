This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, we go back further than we have ever gone. Past the floppy disks and the DNA databases, past the police sketches and the coded letters. We go back to the autumn of eighteen eighty-eight, to the gas-lit streets of the East End of London, to a district called Whitechapel. A place of fog and poverty and narrow lanes where the lamplight could not reach. And we go in search of a man who was never caught, never named, and never seen clearly by anyone who lived to describe him. The world remembers him by a name he may have given himself in a letter. Jack the Ripper.

To understand this case, you have to understand the world it happened in. Whitechapel in the eighteen eighties was one of the poorest corners of the richest city on earth. Tens of thousands of people crowded into crumbling lodging houses. Many women, with no other way to survive, worked the streets at night. They were the forgotten people of a vast empire, and for the most part, no one in power paid them much attention at all. Until, in the space of a few terrible months, five of them were killed in a way that horrified the entire nation.

The murders began in late summer. The first victim widely linked to the killer was a woman named Mary Ann Nichols, found in the early hours on a Whitechapel street. Then came Annie Chapman. Then, on a single night at the end of September, two more, Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes, in what the press called the double event. And finally, in November, Mary Jane Kelly, the youngest of them, killed indoors in a small rented room. Five women. Historians still argue about whether there were others, before and after, but these five became known as the canonical victims. The ones almost everyone agrees were the work of the same hand.

What turned a string of local murders into a worldwide legend was partly the savagery of them, and partly the timing. Because eighteen eighty-eight was the age of the cheap newspaper. For the first time, ordinary working people could buy a paper for a penny and read, in lurid detail, about the latest horror in Whitechapel. The press fed on the fear. The fear sold papers. And the killer, whoever he was, became a kind of dark celebrity, a phantom stalking the imagination of millions of people who had never set foot in the East End.

And then came the letters. The police and the newspapers received a number of letters claiming to be from the killer. One in particular, written in red ink, was signed with a chilling sign-off. Yours truly, Jack the Ripper. Another, a postcard, referred to the double event. There was even a parcel sent to a local official that contained part of a human kidney, with a note claiming it had been taken from one of the victims. To this day, no one knows for certain whether any of these letters were genuine, or whether they were the work of journalists and cranks looking to stoke the panic. But it did not matter. The name stuck. From those letters, the legend got its title.

The investigation was, by the standards of our age, almost impossibly limited. There was no fingerprinting in regular use. There was no forensic science as we would understand it. There were no cameras on the streets, no records to cross-reference, no way to test the few scraps of evidence that were found. The police questioned hundreds of people. They followed countless leads. They flooded the district with officers. And still the killer moved through the fog like smoke, striking and vanishing, leaving almost nothing behind but the dead and the terror.

Over the years that followed, and over the more than one hundred and thirty years since, the list of suspects has grown enormous. A local barber. A failed lawyer. A doctor. A member of the royal family, in one of the wilder theories. A painter. Even, in some accounts, a woman. Whole books have been written arguing for one name or another. Some have claimed, with great confidence, to have solved the case once and for all, only for other researchers to pick their arguments apart. The truth is that the physical evidence is long gone, the witnesses are more than a century in their graves, and the documents that survive are incomplete. We are left chasing a shadow through the records of a vanished city.

Here is what I want you to hold on to, though. Because for all the fascination, for all the tours and the films and the theories, this case is built on the bodies of five real women. They had names. They had children, some of them. They had hard lives in a hard place, and they did not deserve what was done to them, and they did not deserve to be remembered only as the backdrop to a monster's legend. For a long time, history treated them as little more than footnotes. In recent years, careful researchers have worked to give them back their stories, their faces, their humanity. That work matters. The victims are not the puzzle. They are the point.

So why does Jack the Ripper endure when so many killers have faded from memory? I think it is because he was the first of his kind to be born into the modern media age, and because he was never caught. He is a perfect mystery, a blank space onto which every generation projects its own fears. He has no face, so he can wear any face. He has no name, so he can have any name. The fog never lifts. The file never closes. And somewhere in the unrecorded history of that vanished East End, the answer was buried long ago, in a grave we will never find.

The streets of Whitechapel have changed almost beyond recognition. The lodging houses are gone. The gaslight is gone. But the question he left behind still hangs in the air, more than a century later, exactly as he left it. Who was he? We do not know. We may never know.

This has been True Crime Brief.
