Welcome back to Plain Tech. Good to have you here.

Today is an important one, and I want to start with a quick question. If your phone or laptop completely died tonight — screen went black, would not turn on, gone — what would you lose? Your photos? Your contacts? Important documents? Work files? Messages you wanted to keep?

For most people, the honest answer is: a lot. Maybe everything.

And the fix is genuinely simple. It is called backing up. Today I am going to explain what that actually means, why it matters, and what you can do in the next thirty minutes to make sure you are protected. This is one of those episodes where the homework is worth doing today, not someday.

Let me start with the plain definition. Backing up just means making a copy of your important files and keeping that copy somewhere separate from the original. The key word is separate. A copy on the same device is not a backup. If the device fails, both copies fail together. A real backup is on a different device, a different drive, or in the cloud.

The reason this matters so much is that devices fail. They fall, they get stolen, they flood, they just stop working for no obvious reason. And ransomware — a type of malicious software that locks all your files until you pay — is increasingly common. If you have a good backup, ransomware is an inconvenience. If you have no backup, it is a catastrophe.

Let us start with the easiest and most important thing to back up: your photos and videos. These are irreplaceable. Your device can be replaced. The photos from your child's first birthday cannot.

Almost every phone has a built-in way to automatically back up photos to the cloud. On most iPhones, this is called iCloud Photos. On most Android phones, there is Google Photos. Both of these can be set to back up your photos automatically, over Wi-Fi, without you doing anything after the initial setup. You turn it on once and it works quietly in the background forever. If you have not done this yet, this is the most important thing I could possibly suggest you do today.

The free storage that comes with these services is usually enough to start. If you have many years of photos and videos, you may eventually need to pay a small monthly fee for more space. It is usually a few dollars a month and is genuinely worth it for the peace of mind.

Now for computers. Documents, spreadsheets, tax files, downloaded forms — all the things you would be very unhappy to lose. There are two good approaches.

The first is an external drive. This is a small device, roughly the size of a thick credit card or a small wallet, that you plug into your computer's port and it stores copies of your files. You can get a very good one for under fifty dollars. The system is simple: plug it in regularly — say once a week or once a month depending on how much important work you do — and copy your key files across. Some software can do this automatically every time you plug the drive in, which removes the need to remember.

The second approach for computers is cloud backup, which works the same way as photo backup but for your whole computer. Services exist that quietly copy your files to the cloud in the background, all the time, without you having to remember to plug anything in. If your computer fails, you sign into the service on a new machine and your files come back. These services usually cost a small monthly subscription, and for anyone who creates or stores anything important on a computer, the peace of mind is well worth it.

You might be wondering: which approach is better? The honest answer is both, if you can manage it. An external drive is your quick, local rescue. Cloud backup is your protection against fire, flood, theft, or losing the drive itself. The classic advice from people who work in IT is called the three-two-one rule. Three copies of anything important. Two on different types of storage. One stored somewhere other than your home. You do not have to follow this exactly, but it gives you the idea. More copies in more places means a smaller chance of losing everything at once.

A quick note on contacts. Your phone contacts are usually automatically backed up if you are signed into an account on your phone. Make sure that is set up. Losing your entire contact list is one of the more disorienting things that can happen with a phone failure, and it is completely preventable.

And passwords. If you set up a password manager from our very first episode, good news: your passwords are already backed up through that service. If you have not, please do — it is the same solution to the same problem.

To sum up. Backing up is just making a separate copy of things you cannot afford to lose. For photos on your phone, turn on automatic cloud backup today. For documents on your computer, use an external drive and plug it in regularly, or use a cloud backup service. For contacts, make sure your phone account backup is switched on. And for passwords, a password manager handles it all.

Your homework is urgent: go check right now whether your photo backup is turned on. On an iPhone, open Settings, tap your name, then iCloud, then Photos, and make sure it says on. On an Android phone, open Google Photos, tap your profile picture, and look for backup settings. Two minutes. Do it today.
