# PODCAST #20: WANDERLUST — Episode 3

Close your eyes for a moment, if it's safe to. Imagine a small café on a narrow street in a city you've never been to. The light is golden and slanting. There's a coffee in front of you, and a notebook, and absolutely no one waiting on your decision about where to go next. The whole day is yours. Only yours. No compromising, no committee, no one tugging your sleeve toward the museum when you'd rather wander down to the water. Just you, and a city, and a quiet thrill in your chest that says, I did this. I came here, alone.

Welcome back to Wanderlust. Last time, we whispered about budget travel secrets, all the little ways to see the world without emptying your savings. But today I want to talk about something braver, and softer, and more transformative than any deal or discount. Today I want to make the case for traveling alone. At least once. At least once in your life.

I know. I can already feel some of you tensing up. Because the idea of solo travel comes wrapped in fears, doesn't it? And I don't want to pretend they aren't real. So let's hold them up to the light, gently, one by one.

The first fear is loneliness. The worry that you'll be sad, that you'll have no one to share the sunset with, no one to turn to and say, can you believe this. And I'll be honest with you. There may be a moment or two of that. But here's what surprises almost everyone. Solo travel is rarely lonely, because you are never quite as alone as you think. When you travel by yourself, you become wonderfully open. You make eye contact. You strike up conversations you'd never have had if you were buried in a group. The other person on the train, the host at your guesthouse, the fellow wanderer at the next table. The world, it turns out, is full of people, and when you arrive on your own, you let them in.

The second fear is safety. And this one deserves real respect, not a wave of the hand. Especially for women, and I'm speaking to you directly now. Your caution is not weakness. It's wisdom, and we'll talk about the practical habits that keep you safe, because they matter. But I want you to hear this too. Millions of women travel alone every single day and come home glowing, fuller, more themselves. Safety is not about staying small. It's about being smart. And smart is something you absolutely can learn.

And the third fear, the funny one that's somehow the biggest. Eating alone. Sitting at a restaurant by yourself, certain that everyone is looking, everyone is wondering why that poor person is dining solo. Oh, my dear. Nobody is thinking that. And the first time you sit down with a book, or just your own thoughts, and a beautiful plate of food in a place you traveled to reach, you'll realize something. It's not lonely. It's peaceful. It might be one of the most luxurious meals of your entire life.

So those are the fears. And here's the secret. They melt. Not all at once, but they do. Usually by the second day, that knot in your stomach loosens, and in its place comes something I can only describe as freedom. Real, dizzying freedom.

Because when you travel alone, the trip is entirely yours. You wake when you wake. You linger where you want to linger. You change the whole plan on a whim because a stranger mentioned a little village by the sea. There's no negotiating, no guilt, no half-pleasing two people and fully pleasing no one. Every choice, large and small, is yours. And something quietly remarkable happens in all that space. You start to hear yourself again. The you underneath all the roles you play at home. You discover what you actually like, what you're drawn to, how you move through the world when no one's watching. Solo travel isn't really about the place. It's about meeting yourself there.

Now, I never want to leave you only with the dream, so let's get practical, because I want you to actually do this.

First, start small. You do not need to vanish into the Himalayas for six weeks. Take a single weekend in a city a few hours from home. A short, gentle trial run. Prove to yourself that you can do it, and let that confidence grow.

Second, pick a friendly destination. For your first solo trip, choose somewhere with a reputation for being welcoming and easy for newcomers, somewhere with good public transport and lots of other travelers. This is not the time for the remote and the rugged. Choose somewhere that wants to be visited. You'll find places like that the world over.

Third, stay somewhere social. Hostels are not just for the young, I promise you, and many now have private rooms with a sociable common space. They are the easiest place on earth to find a dinner companion. And take a walking tour on your very first morning. It gets your bearings sorted, and walking tours are absolutely full of other solo travelers, all just a little nervous, all secretly hoping someone will say hello. Be the one who says it.

Fourth, the safety habits, and I want you to really keep these. Share your itinerary with someone back home, and check in with them. Trust your gut, always. If a street or a situation feels wrong, you are allowed to simply turn around. Keep a copy of your important documents somewhere separate. Arrive in new places during daylight when you can. And carry a little less in the way of valuables and a little more in the way of awareness. These aren't rules to make you afraid. They're the quiet structure that lets you relax into the adventure.

And fifth, stay connected. A local sim card or a travel data plan is a small price for enormous peace of mind. A maps app that works offline. A way to call home. Connection is what turns the unknown from frightening into simply new.

And then, with all of that in place, you let yourself go.

Because here's the deeper payoff, the real reason I wanted to talk to you today. When you travel alone, you learn, in your bones and not just in your head, that you can rely on yourself. You navigate the strange train station. You solve the small crisis. You find your way when you're lost, and you discover that being lost is not the end of the world, that you are far more capable than you ever gave yourself credit for. You come home carrying a quiet, unshakable knowledge. I can do hard things. I did them alone, and I was alright. More than alright.

And I want to say a special word to those of you who feel it's too late. The ones who are older, who never did this in their twenties, who think solo travel belongs to someone younger, braver, freer. It does not. Some of the most radiant solo travelers I've ever encountered set off for the very first time in their fifties, their sixties, their seventies. There is no expiration date on wonder. If anything, you arrive with more wisdom, more patience, more of an eye for beauty. The world has been waiting for you. It does not care how long you took to come.

So here is your gentle homework, the way I like to leave you. This week, I'm not asking you to book anything yet. I'm only asking you to choose. Pick one place, just one, somewhere not too far, somewhere friendly, where you could imagine spending two or three days entirely on your own. Picture yourself there. The café. The notebook. The whole day yours. Write the name of that place down where you can see it. That's all. Just plant the seed.

Because dreams become trips the moment we let ourselves name them out loud.

Travel gently, travel bravely, and remember, the most beautiful place you'll ever discover might just be the person you become when you finally go alone. I'll be here next time. Until then, keep dreaming with your eyes open.
