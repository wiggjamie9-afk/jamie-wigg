On the fifteenth of August, nineteen seventy-seven, a radio telescope at Ohio State University received a signal. The telescope, known as Big Ear, was part of an ongoing Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence program scanning the sky for narrow-band radio signals that might indicate a non-natural source. The signal came from the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. It lasted seventy-two seconds, the exact window during which the telescope could observe that region of sky before Earth's rotation moved it on. It was so striking that the volunteer researcher who reviewed the data printout circled it and wrote a single word in the margin.

Wow.

The Wow signal is still the most compelling candidate for an extraterrestrial transmission ever detected. It arrived at a frequency of one thousand four hundred and twenty megahertz, which is the frequency at which neutral hydrogen atoms in space naturally emit radio waves. Scientists have long suggested that any technologically advanced civilization wishing to communicate across interstellar distances would use this frequency as a universal beacon, a wavelength known to anyone who understands atomic physics. The signal matched every predicted characteristic of an intentional extraterrestrial broadcast. It was narrow-band. It was far stronger than background noise. It came from outside our solar system.

It was never detected again.

Radio telescopes searched the same region of sky repeatedly in the months and years following the detection. Nothing. The signal arrived once, in a seventy-two second window, from deep space, and then went silent. The silence is the most destabilizing part of the story. An intermittent or one-time signal is exactly what you would expect from a source that is either sweeping a beam across the cosmos at intervals far longer than a human lifetime, or from a civilization that sent a single pulse and waited.

In two thousand sixteen, a professor named Antonio Paris proposed a natural explanation. Two comets, designated two hundred and sixty-six P Christensen and P slash two thousand and eight Q four Garradd, were in the vicinity of the signal's origin during the nineteen seventy-seven detection. Comets emit hydrogen gas clouds that can produce radio emissions at the hydrogen line frequency. The hypothesis was attractive to those seeking a natural explanation. But critics pointed out that the signal's intensity was far higher than cometary hydrogen clouds typically produce, and that the comets were not confirmed to be in that precise location in nineteen seventy-seven with the positional data available.

The Wow signal sits in a peculiar place in the history of science. It is not a hoax. The data is real and has been examined by qualified astronomers for nearly fifty years. It is not explainable by known natural phenomena to everyone's satisfaction. It is not explainable as interference or equipment error. It simply arrived, once, from far away, bearing every signature of intelligent design, and then the sky went quiet.

What does that mean? It could mean a comet. It could mean a one-time geological or astrophysical event we have not yet characterized. It could mean that somewhere out there, a signal was sent into the dark, and by extraordinary coincidence, our planet was in the path of the beam for exactly seventy-two seconds on a summer night in Ohio in nineteen seventy-seven.

If it was a message, no one knew we were listening. Or perhaps someone did. The signal has never come back. The margin note on a strip of thermal paper at a now-demolished telescope remains the closest we have ever come to hearing another voice in the universe.

Wow.
