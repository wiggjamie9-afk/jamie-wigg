Hey, welcome back to Plain Tech. So glad you're here again.

Today we're talking about the thing your kids won't stop mentioning, the thing on every news channel, the thing that apparently is going to change everything: artificial intelligence. AI. And specifically, the AI assistants — the ones you can type to or talk to that answer back in plain English.

Now, I know the conversation around this stuff is exhausting. Half the people say it's magic. The other half say it's going to end the world. Today, we're going to ignore both of them and ask the only question that matters to you: what can this actually do for me, a normal busy person, this week?

These AI assistants are, at their heart, very, very good guessing machines for words. They've read a staggering amount of text, and they've gotten extremely skilled at predicting what helpful words should come next when you ask something. That's it.

What that means in practice: they're brilliant at language tasks — explaining, summarizing, drafting, rephrasing, brainstorming. And it also means they sometimes confidently make things up. The industry calls this hallucination — the assistant states something wrong as if it were true. So we trust it for help, and we double-check it for facts.

Things It's Genuinely Great At:

One: It's a fantastic explainer. Got a letter from the council, or an insurance document full of jargon? Paste it in and say, Explain this to me like I'm not a lawyer. It'll translate the gobbledygook into plain language.

Two: It's a brilliant first-draft writer. Dreading writing a tricky email — to your landlord, to a difficult relative, to customer service? Tell it the situation and the tone you want — firm but polite — and it'll write you a starting point. You then fix it so it sounds like you. A first draft from nothing is the hardest part.

Three: It's a patient tutor. You can ask it the questions you'd feel silly asking a person. What's the difference between a stock and a bond? How do I get red wine out of carpet? Explain the offside rule. It never sighs at you.

Four: It's a planner and a brainstormer. Give me a week of simple dinners for two using chicken and what's cheap right now. Help me plan three days in a city I'm visiting. I have a tiny budget — birthday ideas for a ten-year-old? It's wonderful for getting unstuck.

Where It Falls Down:

Do not trust it as a fact machine for things that matter. Medical questions, legal questions, money decisions, current news, exact prices, dates, phone numbers — it can sound completely sure and be completely wrong. Use it to understand a topic, then confirm the actual facts with a real source or a real professional.

And one privacy point: assume that whatever you type into a free assistant could be seen by the company that runs it. So don't paste in your bank details, passwords, or anything you'd be upset to have read by a stranger.

The one skill that separates frustrating answers from brilliant ones: tell it who you are and what you want. Instead of tips for travel, try I'm fifty-eight, traveling alone to Italy for the first time, nervous about getting around — give me five practical tips. The more context you give, the better it gets.

And if the first answer isn't right, just say so. Shorter. Simpler. More formal. Now make it funny. It's a conversation, not a vending machine.

By category: you almost certainly already have access to a capable free AI assistant. Start free. Only consider paying a monthly fee once you're using it most days and bumping into limits.

So, to sum up. AI assistants are exceptional at language — explaining, drafting, tutoring, planning. They're unreliable for hard facts, so trust them for help and verify them for facts. Keep your private details out. Give them context to get great answers. Start with a free one.

Your homework, if you want it: tonight, pick one annoying task — a tricky email, a confusing document, a meal plan — and let an AI assistant take the first swing at it. Just one. See how it feels.
