In an age when a respectable young woman was expected to stay quiet, to marry well, and to leave the dangerous work of the world to men, one woman talked her way into a newspaper job, had herself committed to a madhouse to expose what happened inside it, and then, just to prove a point, set off alone to race around the entire planet faster than any human being ever had. She did it all before she was thirty. Her name has been half forgotten. It should not be.

Welcome to History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, the story of Nellie Bly, the woman who would not be told what a woman could not do.

She was born Elizabeth Cochran, in the year eighteen sixty-four, in Pennsylvania. Her father died when she was young, leaving the family in difficult circumstances, and she grew up sharply aware of how few doors were open to a woman without money. Her first step into the world of newspapers came almost by accident. A newspaper in Pittsburgh printed a column arguing that women belonged in the home and were good for little else. The young Elizabeth was so furious that she wrote a blistering reply and sent it to the editor. The editor was so impressed by the fire in her writing that he offered her a job. And following the custom of the time, she took a pen name. She became Nellie Bly.

But the paper wanted her to write about fashion and gardening and society parties, the topics judged suitable for a lady. Nellie Bly had no interest in any of it. She wanted to write about the real world, about the poor, about working women, about injustice. She pushed and pushed until at last she made her way to New York City, to one of the most powerful newspapers in the country, and there she proposed an assignment so daring that it would make her famous overnight.

She would have herself declared insane, and committed to the asylum on Blackwell's Island, in order to report on it from the inside. There were terrible rumours about what happened to the women confined there, but no one on the outside truly knew, because the patients had no voice. So Nellie Bly decided to become one of them. She practised wild expressions in a mirror. She checked into a boarding house and behaved strangely, refusing to sleep, talking nonsense, frightening the other residents until they called the authorities. She was examined by doctors, who pronounced her insane, one after another, with barely a real examination at all. And just like that, she vanished behind the walls of the asylum, where no one knew who she really was.

What she found there was a horror. The food was rotten, the water foul, the rooms freezing. Patients were bathed in icy water, beaten, tied down, and treated with casual cruelty by the nurses. And most disturbing of all, Nellie realised that many of the women locked inside were not insane at all. Some were simply poor. Some were immigrants who could not speak English well enough to defend themselves and had been swept up by mistake. Some had been perfectly sane when they arrived, and were being driven toward madness by the conditions themselves. She concluded that the place was, in her own words, a human rat trap. Easy to get into, but almost impossible to get out of, no matter how sane you were.

After ten days, the newspaper arranged for her release, and she wrote it all down. Her report caused a sensation. It was published to enormous attention, and it forced the authorities to act. A grand jury investigation followed. Conditions were examined, reforms were ordered, and a substantial increase in funding was directed toward the care of the mentally ill. A single young woman, by the simple, fearless act of walking into the darkness and reporting back, had improved the lives of thousands of people who had no voice of their own. She had invented a new kind of journalism, the undercover investigation, and she had done it by putting her own freedom and her own sanity on the line.

And then she did something even more astonishing. A few years later, in the year eighteen eighty-nine, she set out to do something no one had ever done. There was a popular novel of the time in which a character circles the globe in eighty days, a feat presented as the very edge of what was possible. Nellie Bly proposed to do it in reality, and to do it faster, and to do it alone, as a woman, with a single small bag of luggage. Her own editors at first doubted a woman could manage such a journey unescorted. She told them that if they would not send her, she would find another paper that would, and go anyway. They sent her.

She left New York and travelled east, by steamship and by train, across the Atlantic, through Europe, down through the Suez Canal, across Asia, by way of Ceylon, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Japan, then across the Pacific and home overland by rail. She braved storms at sea, missed connections, and raced against the clock at every turn, sending back reports as she went so that the whole nation could follow her progress. It became a phenomenon. People placed wagers on whether she would make it. And in the end, she circled the entire planet in just seventy-two days, a record that no one had ever set, beating the fictional eighty days of the novel and stunning the world. A young woman, travelling alone, had gone around the earth faster than any person in history.

She did not stop there. Later in her life she became an inventor and an industrialist, holding patents in her own name at a time when that was nearly unheard of for a woman. And during the First World War, well into middle age, she went to the front in Europe and reported from the trenches, a war correspondent when such a thing was almost unimaginable for a woman to be.

So what does the story of Nellie Bly teach us? Perhaps that the limits placed upon us are very often not real limits at all, but only agreements that everyone has quietly decided to obey. Nellie Bly was told, again and again, what a woman could not do, and each time she simply went and did it. She walked into a madhouse to free the forgotten. She raced around the world to prove it could be done. She did not wait for permission, because she understood that permission was never going to come. The walls around her were made of habit and assumption, and she discovered that such walls fall down the moment someone is brave enough to walk straight through them. Her name was Nellie Bly, and she was here, and the world is wider because she refused to stay where she was put.

This has been History Uncovered. Until next time, look a little more closely at the ordinary day around you. It is the only thing any of us are ever really given.
