Perth, Western Australia. January nineteen ninety-six. The city had already been frightened. Three young women — Sarah Spiers, Jane Rimmer, and Ciara Glennon — had vanished from the entertainment district of Claremont over the course of thirteen months. Sarah disappeared in January ninety-six. Jane in June. Ciara in March ninety-seven. Jane and Ciara were found in bushland south of the city. Sarah was never found. Their disappearances sent fear through Perth in a way the city had not experienced before. Claremont was a safe suburb. These were women leaving bars and nightclubs at night, and vanishing. The case became known as the Claremont serial killings, and it produced the largest criminal investigation in Western Australian history.

For more than two decades, no one was charged. The investigation was enormous — tens of thousands of potential witnesses, hundreds of suspects, DNA samples collected across the state. And still, no arrest. The case became a wound in the city, the kind of unresolved fear that never fully heals.

Then came a development that none of the original investigators could have anticipated, because the technology did not exist in nineteen ninety-six. In two thousand and sixteen, forensic scientists working on the case used a technique that had not been available at the time of the murders — analyzing DNA found on the victims' clothing using a method that could identify an individual from a single cell. The science had advanced to a point where trace material that could not have been processed twenty years earlier could now be analyzed.

The DNA found on Ciara Glennon's hair pointed to a man named Bradley Robert Edwards, a Telstra technician who had worked in the Claremont area and had prior convictions for sexual assault. He was arrested in December two thousand and sixteen, more than twenty years after the first disappearance.

His trial was one of the longest and most complex in Western Australian history. The prosecution presented both old evidence, revisited through new forensic methods, and newly discovered evidence, including DNA found on a cardigan belonging to another victim. In September two thousand and twenty-one, Bradley Robert Edwards was found guilty of the murders of Jane Rimmer and Ciara Glennon, and of other offenses. He was sentenced to life in prison with no possibility of parole. The murder of Sarah Spiers, whose body has never been found, was not the subject of the conviction, and officially that case remains open.

The families of the three women waited more than two decades for something that looked like justice. Two of them received it. One did not, and may never.

What the Claremont case demonstrates is something investigators and forensic scientists speak about with increasing frequency — that cold cases are not necessarily dead ends. They are simply cases waiting for the science to catch up. DNA technology that did not exist in the nineteen nineties has reopened files that seemed closed forever. Material evidence preserved in storage, evidence that seemed insignificant or unprocessable at the time of collection, becomes actionable years later when the tools improve.

Some killers are patient. Some simply believe that time is on their side. In the case of Bradley Robert Edwards, time was working against him in a way he could not have known, because the evidence preserved from his crimes grew more powerful with every passing year, not less.
