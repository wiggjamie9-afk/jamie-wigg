# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 15
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. This is the one I've been saving to close out this run, and it's my favorite, so I'm glad you're here. Today we're talking about traditions. Rituals. The small, daft, weirdly sacred things a family does over and over, that quietly turn a bunch of people who live in the same house into something much bigger than that. A family.

Let me start with a tiny example, because traditions are almost never grand. In our house, on a Friday, we have what is officially and forever known as Fancy Toast. Now, Fancy Toast is, structurally, just toast. With a slightly more interesting topping than usual. That's the whole thing. But to my kids, Fancy Toast Friday is a pillar of Western civilization. It is non-negotiable. If I ever, in a moment of madness, suggested skipping it, I think there'd be a small riot. And it costs nothing, and it takes ten minutes, and it is one of the load-bearing walls of their entire childhood.

Here's the thing I worked out, and it genuinely changed how I see this whole parenting business. Kids do not remember days. They remember patterns. They won't remember the specific Friday. They'll remember that Fridays meant something. That there was a rhythm to the week, a shape to the year, things you could count on. And in a childhood, that rhythm is a kind of safety you cannot buy. A kid who knows what's coming, who knows that some things always happen, is a kid standing on solid ground.

And here's the part that gets me, and it's the reason I wanted to end the run right here. We spend so much of fatherhood, this whole run of episodes really, worrying about the big stuff. Discipline, screens, money, danger, getting it wrong, becoming our own dads. And all of that matters, it does. But the thing your kid will actually carry, the warm thing they'll hand to their own children one day, probably isn't a lecture you gave. It's Fancy Toast Friday. It's the silly voice you always do. It's the specific way your family does Christmas morning, or the walk you always take, or the song in the car. The tiny, repeated, ordinary stuff. That's the stuff that becomes them.

So let me get practical, even though traditions are the least practical thing in the world. A few thoughts.

One: it does not have to be big, and it really shouldn't cost much. The best traditions are small and free and slightly stupid. A pancake on a Sunday. A particular bedtime phrase you always say, the same words, every night. A film you watch every single New Year's Eve. The magic isn't in the scale. The magic is purely in the repetition. Do a small thing enough times and it stops being an activity and becomes a piece of who your family is.

Two: let the kids be the keepers of it. This is the bit I love. The most powerful traditions are often the ones the kids enforce. They'll be the ones reminding you. Dad, it's Friday, where's the Fancy Toast. They become the guardians of the ritual, and in doing that, they take ownership of the family itself. They're not just receiving a childhood. They're holding it, protecting it, passing it back to you. Let them. A tradition the kids defend is a tradition that'll outlive you.

Three: protect them fiercely, because the world will try to crowd them out. Life gets busy. There's always a reason to skip it, a thing running late, a tiredness, a more important grown-up problem. And it'll feel completely reasonable, each single time, to let the little ritual slide just this once. Don't. Or at least, don't let it slide twice. Those tiny moments are not the fluff around the important stuff. For a kid, they are the important stuff. Guard the daft little ritual like it matters, because to them, it does.

Here's what I want you to sit with, and it's the thing I want to leave you with at the end of all this. You are not just raising a person. You are building a world for them to have grown up in. A specific, particular, this-is-how-my-family-was world, full of in-jokes and silly voices and Friday rituals that make no sense to anyone outside the front door. And one day, a long time from now, your kid is going to be standing in their own kitchen, with their own children, and they're going to make a slightly fancier piece of toast on a Friday, and not even fully know why. And that, right there, is you. Still in the room. Long after you've left it.

That's the code. Not perfection. Not having all the answers. Just showing up, with your eyes, doing the small warm thing, again and again, until it becomes the air your kid grew up breathing.

That's the run, friends. Thank you for spending it with me. Go be ordinary and present and a little bit daft with the people you love.

This has been Dad Code. Now go make some Fancy Toast.
