Hello, this is Austin, and you're listening to Unexplained.

Tonight we step into a small mountain village in the Atlas Mountains of Morocco, in the spring of nineteen sixty-nine. It is just before dawn. The sky is clear. And then, without warning, the night tears open. A shrieking sound, a flash of light, and the heavens begin to rain stones. Thousands of them. Fragments of a black, alien rock falling out of the dark and burying themselves in the cold ground. By the time the sun rises, the village of Pultusk, and others like it across history, will remind us of something humbling. That the sky is not empty, and that on rare and frightening days, it falls. Tonight, we look up at the most dramatic of these visitors. This is the story of the great meteor explosion over Tunguska.

Let us go back further still, to the morning of the thirtieth of June, nineteen oh eight, to a vast and almost empty stretch of forest in Siberia, in the basin of the Tunguska River. It is a remote place even now. Then, it was at the very edge of the known world, home only to scattered reindeer herders and trappers. At around seven in the morning, the sky lit up. Witnesses, dozens of miles away, described a column of bluish light, almost as bright as the sun, moving across the sky. Then came a flash, and a sound like artillery, and then a shockwave that knocked people off their feet, shattered windows hundreds of miles away, and was felt around the world.

When the dust settled, something astonishing had happened. An area of forest covering roughly eight hundred square miles had been flattened. An estimated eighty million trees lay on the ground, their trunks all pointing outward, away from a single central point, like spokes blown flat from the hub of a wheel. The energy released was later estimated as hundreds, perhaps a thousand, times greater than the atomic bomb that would one day fall on Hiroshima. And here is the detail that makes Tunguska so strange. When scientists finally reached the site, years later, they found no crater. No great hole in the ground. Something had delivered the force of a thousand atomic bombs, and yet left no clear mark of impact at its centre. So let us do what we always do. Let us set the legend aside and rank the explanations by the weight of the evidence.

The strongest explanation, and the one that science overwhelmingly accepts today, is an air burst. The idea is this. A small asteroid or a fragment of a comet, perhaps fifty to two hundred feet across, entered the atmosphere at enormous speed. As it tore downward, the pressure and heat built to an unbearable degree, and at a height of several miles above the ground, the object violently exploded in mid-air before it could strike the surface. This single idea explains almost everything. It explains the blinding light and the column of fire, the rolling object burning through the sky. It explains the colossal shockwave that flattened the forest. And, crucially, it explains the absence of a crater, because the object detonated in the air and was largely consumed, never reaching the ground as a solid mass. The pattern of fallen trees, all pointing away from a central point with some left standing upright directly beneath, matches an overhead blast with remarkable precision. Tiny particles found in the soil and in tree resin from that year support a cosmic origin.

Then comes the next tier, the natural but more contested questions. Scientists still debate exactly what the object was. Was it a stony asteroid, which would explain certain dust particles, or was it a fragment of a comet, made largely of ice, which would help explain why so little solid material was ever found, since ice would vaporise almost completely? There is debate, too, about the exact size and altitude of the blast. And there is one more genuine puzzle. Some researchers have pointed to a small, deep lake near the site, called Lake Cheko, and asked whether it might be an impact crater after all, gouged out by a surviving chunk of the object. Other scientists strongly disagree, arguing the lake is far older. These are real scientific arguments, still unsettled, but they are arguments about the details, not about the basic truth. Something came from space and exploded in the sky.

Then there is the third tier, the further-out ideas, the ones the evidence supports far less but which the legend loves the most. Because no crater was found at first, and because the blast resembled, in some ways, a nuclear explosion decades before such a thing existed, imaginations ran wild. Some proposed that Tunguska was the crash of an alien spacecraft, its engine or reactor detonating above the forest. Others suggested it was a tiny black hole passing through the Earth, or a sliver of antimatter annihilating in the atmosphere. And a few even credited the inventor Nikola Tesla, imagining a secret energy weapon fired across the globe. These ideas are vivid, and they fed on the early mystery of the missing crater. But every one of them has been undercut by the same plain fact. A cosmic air burst already explains the evidence, without requiring spacecraft, or black holes, or secret weapons. The exotic theories survive on the thrill of the unknown, not on the strength of the proof.

So where does that leave us? Weigh it honestly, and the answer is no longer truly a mystery of cause, but it remains a mystery of fortune. An object from space exploded over Siberia with the force of a thousand bombs and flattened a forest the size of a great city. It happened in one of the emptiest places on the planet, and so, as far as we know, not a single life was directly lost in the blast itself. Now imagine that same object arriving a few hours later, as the Earth turned, over a crowded capital city instead of an empty forest. That is the thought that has quietly haunted scientists ever since.

And perhaps that is the real reason Tunguska will not let us go. It is not that we cannot explain it. We can, and we have. It is that the explanation is, in its own way, more frightening than any alien craft. The sky is not empty. It is full of moving rock and ice, most of it harmless, some of it not, and the same forces that built the planets still occasionally come knocking. Tunguska was not a message, or a weapon, or a visitor. It was a reminder. That we live on a small world, in a busy solar system, and that what fell on an empty Siberian forest in nineteen oh eight could, one day, fall somewhere else.

We are drawn to the mystery because it sits exactly where our control ends and our vulnerability begins. We can track the larger asteroids now. We can model the blast. We can map the fallen trees. But we cannot quite stand in that frozen forest at dawn, and feel the ground heave as the sky itself catches fire. That space, the one we cannot reach, is where the legend lives. And it is why, more than a century later, we are still walking among those long-fallen trees, still scanning the dark above us, still wondering when the sky will fall again.

This has been Unexplained. Sleep well, if you can.
