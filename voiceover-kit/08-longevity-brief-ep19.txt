Welcome to Longevity Brief. Today we are looking at the frontiers of longevity research — the cellular and molecular processes that scientists now believe drive aging itself, and the most promising therapeutic approaches currently being investigated.

For most of human history, aging was treated as inevitable — a passive wearing down of the body over time, like a machine accumulating rust. The scientific revolution in geroscience over the past thirty years has fundamentally changed that view. Aging is now understood as a biological process with specific drivers, measurable hallmarks, and — in principle — targetable mechanisms.

The concept of the hallmarks of aging was formalised in a two-thousand-thirteen paper in the journal Cell by researchers Carlos Lopez-Otin and colleagues. They identified nine hallmarks: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, and altered intercellular communication. A two-thousand-twenty-three update expanded this to twelve, adding chronic inflammation, dysbiosis of the microbiome, and compromised macroautophagy.

Understanding these hallmarks has given researchers specific targets to work with.

Cellular senescence has attracted particular attention. Senescent cells are cells that have stopped dividing but have not died. They accumulate with age — in joints, lungs, kidneys, fat tissue, and the brain — and secrete a cocktail of inflammatory signals collectively called the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. This chronic low-grade inflammation is now believed to drive or accelerate virtually every major age-related disease.

A class of drugs called senolytics has emerged to selectively clear senescent cells. The first senolytic combination to show results in humans was dasatinib and quercetin — dasatinib is a cancer drug, quercetin is a natural flavonoid found in onions and apples. Early human trials have shown the combination can clear senescent cells in fat tissue, reduce inflammatory markers, and improve physical function in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Multiple trials are now underway in conditions from Alzheimer's disease to diabetic kidney disease.

Epigenetic aging is another major research frontier. Our genome does not change appreciably with age, but the patterns of chemical marks that regulate which genes are expressed — collectively called the epigenome — drift substantially. These epigenetic changes are measurable. Researchers like Steve Horvath at UCLA developed what are known as epigenetic clocks: algorithms that use patterns of DNA methylation to estimate biological age, which can diverge significantly from chronological age. A forty-five-year-old might have a biological age of thirty-eight or fifty-five depending on their lifestyle, environment, and genetics.

Crucially, epigenetic aging appears to be partially reversible. Animal experiments have demonstrated that epigenetic reprogramming — using Yamanaka factors, the transcription factors that convert adult cells back into stem cells — can reverse epigenetic age in the retina, muscle, brain, and kidney, restoring function without triggering tumour formation. Human trials are still years away, but the science is advancing rapidly.

NAD-plus, a molecule critical to cellular energy metabolism and DNA repair, declines by roughly fifty percent between the ages of forty and sixty. This decline impairs the function of a class of enzymes called sirtuins, which regulate many aspects of cellular health, stress resistance, and longevity. Supplementation with NAD precursors — nicotinamide riboside and nicotinamide mononucleotide — has shown promise in animal models and is being studied in human trials. Evidence in humans remains preliminary but is generating genuine scientific interest.

The broader point is this: aging is increasingly being approached as a medical problem with biological solutions. The interventions available today are still largely behavioural — exercise, sleep, diet, stress management — and they remain the most evidence-backed tools we have. But the next decade of longevity research may look very different from the last. The field is moving from description to intervention, from observation to mechanism. Paying attention to it is worthwhile.
