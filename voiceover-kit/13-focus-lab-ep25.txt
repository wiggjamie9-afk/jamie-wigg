Welcome to Focus Lab.

Today we're talking about batch processing — a strategy borrowed from manufacturing that turns out to be one of the most powerful tools for protecting your focus throughout the day.

The core idea is this: instead of handling similar tasks whenever they arrive, you collect them and handle them all at once in a dedicated block of time. You're not switching between different types of thinking — you're staying in one cognitive mode for a sustained period and doing all the work that fits that mode at the same time.

Here's why this matters. Your brain has a cost for switching between different types of tasks. Writing an email, responding to a Slack message, reviewing a document, taking a phone call — each of these requires a different mental mode. Every time you switch modes, there's a startup cost. You need to reorient, recall context, adjust your thinking style. If you're switching tasks frequently throughout the day, you're paying that startup cost over and over. You end up spending more time transitioning between tasks than actually completing them.

Batch processing eliminates that cost by grouping tasks so that each mode switch happens as infrequently as possible.

The most common and highest-value application is email and messaging. Instead of checking and responding throughout the day — which turns your communication tools into a real-time interruption system — you designate two or three windows each day for communication. Outside those windows, you are not monitoring or responding to messages. This is a significant shift for most people, but the focus benefits are immediate.

The same logic applies to phone calls. Rather than taking calls whenever they come in, batch your outbound calls into a single block and set expectations with people about when you'll return calls.

Administrative tasks — filing, scheduling, updating records, processing receipts — should be batched too. Pick one time per week, put it in your calendar, and handle everything at once rather than letting these small tasks interrupt your work randomly.

Content creation and deep thinking tasks also benefit from batching, though in a slightly different way. Instead of trying to do a little bit of writing or strategy work every day, consider batching these into longer blocks on fewer days. An hour of truly uninterrupted writing beats four disconnected fifteen-minute sessions almost every time.

Here's a simple framework to get started. Look at the tasks you handled this week and group them into categories: communication, administrative, creative, research, meetings. Now ask yourself: which of these categories could I handle in dedicated blocks instead of throughout the day? Start by batching your communication — that alone will create more focused time than almost any other single change you can make.

One important note. Batch processing only works if you protect the blocks. If you batch your email to twice a day but still check it constantly, you haven't changed anything. The commitment to the schedule is the whole point.

Batch what can be batched. Guard what can't. Your focus is worth defending.
