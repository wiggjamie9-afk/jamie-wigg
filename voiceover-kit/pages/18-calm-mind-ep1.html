Today, we're going to gently understand something that can feel frightening when you don't have words for it: what's actually happening in your body when you feel anxious. Maybe you know the feeling. Your heart speeds up. Your chest tightens. Your thoughts start racing, or your stomach drops. And part of your mind whispers: something is wrong with me. I want to start with this: nothing is wrong with you. What you're feeling is your body doing exactly what it was built to do — just at a moment when you don't need it. Here's the simple version. Deep in your brain there's a small almond-shaped region often called the amygdala. Think of it as a smoke alarm. Its whole job is to keep you safe by spotting possible danger fast — faster than thinking. When it senses a threat, it triggers what's commonly called the fight-or-flight response. Your body releases stress hormones like adrenaline and cortisol. Your heart beats quicker to move blood to your muscles. Your breathing speeds up to take in more oxygen. Your senses sharpen. If a car suddenly swerved toward you, this response could save your life. The problem is, the smoke alarm can't always tell the difference between real danger and an unpaid bill, a difficult email, or a worry about the future. So it sounds the alarm anyway. That's anxiety: a survival system, firing when there's no actual emergency. It's uncomfortable, but it is not dangerous in itself, and it does pass. It can help to name the physical sensations as just that — sensations. A racing heart is your body preparing, not a sign of disaster. A tight chest is tense muscles, not a warning that you're about to fall apart. When we stop fearing the feelings themselves, they often start to loosen their grip. So here's one gentle thing you can try, drawn from approaches used in cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness. It's sometimes called "name it to tame it." When anxiety rises, try quietly labeling what's happening, without judgment. You might say to yourself: "This is my body's alarm going off. My heart is racing. My thoughts are speeding up. This is anxiety, and it will pass." Naming the experience gently engages the more reasoning part of your brain, and for many people, that simple act takes a little of the heat out of the moment. You're not fighting the feeling. You're not running from it. You're just noticing it, like watching weather move across the sky. So if you take one thing from today, let it be this: anxiety is your body trying to protect you. It's a false alarm, not a sign that you're broken. And you can learn, slowly and kindly, to turn the volume down. If anxiety is a regular, heavy part of your life, please consider talking with a doctor or a mental health professional — that's not a last resort, it's a wise and caring step, and there's no shame in it. The numbers are in the show notes. Thank you for spending these few minutes with me. Be gentle with yourself today. Next time, we'll explore calming techniques that actually work when anxiety shows up — simple ways to steady your body in the moment. Until then, take care of yourself. This has been Calm Mind.
