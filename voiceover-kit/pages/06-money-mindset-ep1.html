Welcome to Money Mindset — the show for people who were never actually taught how money works. I'm your host, and for the next twelve minutes, there's no judgment here, no jargon, and no math you have to be good at. Just one clear idea and one small step. Let's take a breath and begin. Today's question is the one almost everyone is quietly ashamed of: why do I keep spending money I told myself I wouldn't spend? Here's the first thing I want you to hear, and I mean it: you are not bad with money. Overspending is not a character flaw. It's not a willpower problem. It's a wiring problem — and once you understand the wiring, you stop fighting yourself and start working with yourself. Let's talk about what's actually happening in your brain when you spend. When you buy something — even something small, a coffee, a thing in a cart at 11pm — your brain releases a little hit of dopamine. Dopamine is the 'anticipation' chemical. Here's the catch that the apps and the stores know and you might not: most of the dopamine comes BEFORE you buy. The wanting feels better than the having. That's why the excitement fades about ten minutes after the package arrives. You weren't really buying the thing. You were buying the feeling of relief from wanting it. Now stack three modern traps on top of that. Trap one: spending stopped feeling like spending. When you tap a card or click 'buy now,' you don't feel the money leave. Studies have shown people will pay more — sometimes double — when they're not handling cash, because there's no little moment of 'ouch.' The ouch is useful. We accidentally engineered it out of our lives. Trap two: you're often not shopping — you're soothing. Tired, stressed, lonely, bored. Spending is one of the fastest, cheapest-feeling ways to change how you feel for a few minutes. The problem isn't that you're weak. The problem is that nobody gave you a different button to press when you feel that way. Trap three: everything is designed to remove the pause. One-click checkout. Saved cards. 'Buy now, pay later.' Free returns. Each one quietly deletes the few seconds where you might have thought, 'do I actually want this?' Let me make this real. Say you grab lunch out and a coffee on workdays — about eighteen dollars a day. Feels like nothing. But that's eighteen dollars times five days, that's ninety dollars a week. Over a month, roughly three hundred and ninety dollars. Over a year, around four thousand, six hundred and eighty dollars. Nearly five thousand dollars — and almost none of it was a decision you actually made. It was just the default. I'm not telling you to never buy a coffee. Please, buy the coffee if it brings you joy. The point is this: small, automatic, unconscious spending is where the money quietly goes — not the occasional big purchase you agonize over. So here's your single step for today. It's called the 24-hour pause, and it only applies to non-essentials over twenty dollars. When you want to buy something that isn't a need, you don't say no. Saying no triggers the part of your brain that wants to rebel. Instead you say: 'I can have this — in 24 hours.' You put it in the cart, you close the app, you set a reminder for tomorrow. That's it. Tomorrow, the dopamine has faded. You'll buy maybe a third of those things, and you'll genuinely enjoy them. The other two-thirds? You'll have completely forgotten you wanted them. People who try this routinely cut their impulse spending by thirty to forty percent without ever feeling deprived — because you never told yourself no. You just told yourself 'not yet.' One bonus move: if you spend to soothe a feeling, name the feeling first. 'I'm not shopping, I'm tired.' Naming it out loud breaks the spell surprisingly often. So let's recap, gently. Overspending isn't a flaw, it's wiring. The dopamine comes from the wanting, not the having. Tapping a card hides the 'ouch.' And a simple 24-hour pause on non-essentials over twenty dollars works WITH your brain instead of against it. Your one step today: the next time you want to buy something that isn't a need, give it 24 hours. That's all. If this helped even a little, follow the show so the next episode finds you — because next time we're building the thing that turns money fear into money calm: your first emergency fund, starting from wherever you are, even if that's zero. You've got this. I'll see you then. Money Mindset is general education, not personal financial advice. For big decisions, talk to a licensed professional.
