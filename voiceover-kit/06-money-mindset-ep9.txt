Welcome to Money Mindset. I'm Everet. Take a breath with me before we start. Twelve minutes, one idea, one step, zero judgment. Here we go.

Today we're going to talk about asking for more money at work — negotiating your pay. And I know, for a lot of people, those words alone bring a wave of dread. The sweaty palms, the fear of seeming greedy, the worry that they'll laugh or say no or think less of you. So before we touch any tactics, I want to spend a moment on the fear itself, because the fear is the real obstacle, far more than the words you'll eventually say.

Here's the idea. Negotiating your pay is not asking for a favor — it's a normal, expected business conversation, and the discomfort you feel is mostly an old story, not a real risk. Most of us were quietly raised to believe that talking about money is rude, that wanting more is greedy, that a good person just works hard and waits to be noticed. But here's the truth that changes everything. Employers expect this conversation. They budget for it. The person across the table has very likely negotiated their own pay more than once. You asking is not a faux pas. It's you behaving like a professional who understands their own value — and quietly, that earns respect more often than it loses it.

Let me give you the single most important reason to get comfortable with this, with a little arithmetic that tends to wake people up. Raises usually build on your current salary as a percentage. So a higher starting number doesn't just help you today — it lifts every future raise, because each one is calculated from a bigger base. A small bump now compounds for years. This is why negotiating even once, even a little, can be worth tens of thousands of dollars over a career. It is genuinely one of the highest-value ten-minute conversations you will ever have. The discomfort is brief. The payoff lasts decades.

So let's make it doable, because I don't want this to stay a scary idea. There are three quiet moves that take almost all the fear out of it.

The first move is to know your number before you walk in. Vague hope is terrifying; a specific, researched figure is calming. So you do your homework — you look up what people in your role, your industry, and your area are actually paid. There are free websites that show salary ranges for almost any job. When you know the real market range, you stop guessing and start standing on facts. And facts steady the voice.

The second move is to build your case on value, not need. This is so important. You never frame the ask around your rent going up or your personal bills — that's your situation, not their concern. You frame it around what you bring. You quietly gather your wins — the project you led, the money you saved them, the problem you solved, the extra responsibility you took on. You walk in able to say, here's the value I've added, and I'd like my pay to reflect it. That's not greedy. That's a fair, grown-up exchange.

And the third move is simply to practice the words out loud before the moment arrives. So much of the dread is just unfamiliarity — your mouth has never formed these sentences before. So you rehearse. Out loud, to a mirror, to a friend, to your own reflection in a dark window. Something plain and calm, like — based on my contributions over the past year and the market rate for this role, I'd like to discuss bringing my salary to this number. Say it ten times and it stops feeling like a cliff edge and starts feeling like a sentence. The confidence isn't something you're born with. It's something you rehearse into being.

One more gentle thing, because people forget it. Almost no one gets a no that ends their career. The realistic worst case is they say, not right now — and then you can ask the calm follow-up: what would I need to achieve to get there, and when can we revisit this? Now even a no has turned into a plan and a date. You truly cannot lose this conversation. You can only learn from it or win it.

So here is your one step for today, and notice it isn't go demand a raise tomorrow. That's too big, and timing matters. Your one step is just to find your number. Spend ten minutes looking up the real salary range for your role and your area on one of those free salary websites. Just learn the figure. Because the moment you know what your work is actually worth on the open market, the whole conversation stops being about your nerve and starts being about a fact — and facts are so much easier to say out loud.

Let's recap. Negotiating your pay isn't asking a favor — it's a normal conversation that employers expect and budget for. Because raises compound on your base, getting it right even once can be worth a fortune over a career. Know your number, build your case on the value you bring rather than your personal needs, and rehearse the words until they feel ordinary. And you can't really lose, because even a no becomes a plan for next time.

Your one step today: look up the real market salary range for your role and your area.
