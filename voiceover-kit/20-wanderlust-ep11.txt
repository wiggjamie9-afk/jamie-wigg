Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we are going to the country that taught the rest of the world how to live well. Italy. A long boot of land kicking out into the warm Mediterranean, and somehow, inside one set of borders, holding snow-capped Alps and lemon groves, Roman ruins and Renaissance galleries, the busiest cities and the sleepiest hill towns. Close your eyes and picture a piazza in a small Tuscan town in the soft gold light of early evening. The stone of the buildings is the colour of honey. The church bell sounds the hour, slow and unhurried. An old man crosses the square with a loaf of bread under his arm. Somewhere a coffee machine hisses, and the whole town seems to exhale and settle into the evening. This is Italy. And the secret of it is not the famous sights, though they are extraordinary. The secret is the rhythm.

Let me steer you gently away from the obvious mistake, which is trying to do Rome, Florence, Venice, and the coast all in one breathless week. Italy punishes hurry. So instead, let me give you a way of being there. Pick one great city and one quiet region, and let them breathe into each other. Spend your days in the city and your evenings, your real evenings, somewhere slower.

We start in Rome, because everyone should stand in Rome at least once. But here is how to do it without losing your soul to the crowds. Go to the great sights at the very edges of the day. The Colosseum and the Roman Forum just as they open, before the tour groups arrive, when the light is low and golden and you can almost hear the ghosts. Then, in the punishing heat of midday, do as the Romans do and disappear, into a long lunch, into a cool dark church, into a shaded corner of a park. The city comes alive again at dusk. Walk it then. Throw a coin in the Trevi Fountain, not because it is uncrowded, it never is, but because it is one of those silly rituals worth doing anyway. And eat your dinner late, at nine, ten o'clock, the way Italians do.

The food is where Italy reveals its deepest secret, which is regionalism. There is, in truth, no such thing as Italian food. There is Roman food, and Neapolitan food, and Bolognese food, and Sicilian food, and they are fiercely, gloriously different. In Rome, order cacio e pepe, the simplest of pastas, just cheese and black pepper and starchy water whipped into a silken sauce, and marvel that three ingredients can do that. In Naples, eat pizza, real pizza, soft and blistered and folded in your hands, in the city that invented it. In Bologna, eat the rich slow meat ragù that the rest of the world wrongly calls bolognese. Never order a cappuccino after eleven in the morning. It marks you instantly as a tourist, because to an Italian, all that milk after breakfast is faintly absurd. After lunch and after dinner, you take an espresso, standing up, at the bar, in one swift swallow, the way it is meant to be.

Now, the quiet region. My heart says Tuscany or Umbria, the rolling green hills, the cypress trees standing like dark candles along the ridgelines, the medieval towns crowning every hilltop. Rent a small car, just for a few days, and drive the back roads with no real plan. Stop in a town you have never heard of because the name on the sign pleased you. Find the one café on the square, order a glass of the local red, and watch the town go about its evening. This is where Italy lives. Not in the museums, lovely as they are, but in these unremarkable, perfect ordinary moments.

Here are your practical anchors. Number one, the riposo. In smaller towns, many shops and restaurants close in the early afternoon, roughly one until four, and reopen for the evening. Do not fight it. Eat your big meal at lunch, rest in the heat, and rejoin the day when the town wakes again. Number two, the coperto. Many restaurants add a small cover charge per person, a euro or two, for the bread and the table. It is normal, it is not a scam, and tipping beyond it is appreciated but never expected the way it is elsewhere. Number three, validate your train tickets. On the regional trains you must stamp your paper ticket in the little machine on the platform before you board, or you risk a fine. The fast intercity trains are different, but when in doubt, stamp it. Number four, dress a little. Italians take real pride in how they present themselves, the concept they call bella figura, the beautiful figure you cut in the world. You need not be elegant, but a churchgoing dress code, shoulders and knees covered, is required to enter the great cathedrals, and a little care in how you dress will be quietly returned to you everywhere.

And the thing to carry home. Italy is a daily argument against rushing. It insists that a meal is not fuel but an event, that a coffee is a pause and not a transaction, that an evening walk with no destination is one of the finest things a person can do. You will go for the art and the ruins. You will come home changed by the pace.

So your homework this week. On one evening of your trip, take a passeggiata. That is the Italian evening stroll, the whole town out walking, dressed nicely, greeting neighbours, going nowhere in particular, simply to see and be seen as the day cools. Join it. Walk slowly. Buy a gelato. Have absolutely no destination at all. That is the secret of Italy, learned in a single evening.
