Welcome to AI Briefing. Today we are looking at the money, specifically at what has been a growing undercurrent in the analyst and investor community: the question of whether the extraordinary valuations and spending rates in AI are sustainable. This is not a crash prediction and it is not a cheerleading session. It is an attempt to understand where the pressure points are and what signals to watch.

Start with the spending numbers, because they are genuinely staggering. The hyperscalers, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta, together committed to spending more than three hundred billion dollars on AI infrastructure in a single recent fiscal year. That includes data centers, chips, power infrastructure, and the talent to run it all. These are not startup experiments. These are capital allocation decisions at the scale of major national infrastructure projects.

The chip market tells the same story. Demand for Nvidia's flagship GPU hardware has consistently outpaced supply, and Nvidia's revenue growth over the past two years has been extraordinary by any historical comparison in the semiconductor sector. The build-out is real. The spend is real. Whether the returns that justify it are real is the question.

Here is the structural argument that concerns some analysts. The cost of producing AI answers, what the industry calls inference, has been falling fast but has not yet fallen to a level where most AI products are clearly profitable at scale. Companies are offering AI tools cheaply or free, building user bases and workflow integrations, on the bet that compute costs will fall far enough and fast enough to make those products sustainable. That bet requires that two things happen at the same time: costs keep falling, and customers develop enough genuine dependence that they will pay meaningful prices when pricing is eventually tightened.

The first part, falling costs, does seem to be happening. Models that cost tens of millions of dollars to train two years ago can be approximated at a fraction of that today. Inference costs per token have dropped dramatically. The efficiency curve is moving in the right direction.

The second part, paying customers with genuine workflow dependence, is more mixed. Enterprise contracts are being signed. Revenue from AI products is growing at every major company. But a significant portion of current AI usage is still exploratory, developers testing capabilities, businesses running proofs of concept, individuals trying things out. Converting that exploration into durable, price-inelastic demand is the commercial challenge that remains unproven at the scale required.

There is a parallel you will hear raised in these discussions: the dot-com boom. The internet did transform everything. But the transformation took longer than the first wave of investors expected, and many companies that were genuinely building real things did not survive the gap between early enthusiasm and sustainable revenue. The technology was right. The timing and the unit economics were not always right.

The AI moment is not identical to the dot-com moment. The applications are more immediate. The revenue is more real. The enterprise adoption is more deliberate. But the structural question, is the infrastructure being built ahead of the revenue that will eventually justify it, is a legitimate one, and the analysts asking it are not simply being contrarian.

What does this mean for how to think about the industry right now? A few things. Companies that are generating real revenue from AI, not just from selling the infrastructure to build AI but from applications that customers depend on and pay for, are in a fundamentally different position than companies burning capital on capabilities without a clear path to recovery. The distinction matters and it will matter more over the next two years.

The briefing on AI and the investment cycle: the spending is enormous, the efficiency curve is real and moving, but the gap between current spend and clearly sustainable revenue is also real. Watch who is generating genuine, durable enterprise revenue and who is still in the pre-revenue infrastructure phase. That gap is where the next set of industry headlines will come from.

That is the briefing. I am your host, and I will see you next time.
