This is True Crime Brief.

Tonight, a case that haunted a small Indiana town for more than six years, a double murder that produced almost nothing in the way of leads, and a resolution that came from one of the most unexpected places imaginable — a grainy photograph, an audio clip recorded on a victim's phone, and the slow, patient work of investigators who never let the case go cold in their minds even when it appeared cold on paper.

On the thirteenth of February, two thousand and seventeen, two teenage girls — Abigail Williams, thirteen, and Liberty German, fourteen — went hiking on the Monon High Bridge Trail in Delphi, Indiana. It was a Monday. Schools were closed. The trail followed an old trestle bridge over the Deer Creek, and the girls had been dropped off by a family member that afternoon.

Liberty German had her phone with her. At some point on the trail, she recorded video and audio on that phone, apparently because the girls had encountered a man and something about him had frightened her. The recording captured a partial image of a heavyset man in a blue jacket walking across the bridge toward them. It also captured his voice, a low, unhurried command: down the hill.

The next day, the girls did not come home. Their bodies were found in the woods near the creek. Both had been killed. The crime scene details were not fully disclosed by police, but investigators described what they found as a deliberate, controlled act. This was not random. Whoever had done this had stayed with the victims for some time.

Indiana State Police released the partial photograph and the audio recording publicly, asking for the community's help in identifying the man. The case became one of the most widely circulated unsolved murder investigations in the country. Millions of people saw the photograph. Hundreds of thousands of tips came in over the following years. Multiple suspects were named publicly. A man who had lived in Delphi at the time of the murders was arrested on unrelated charges in two thousand and nineteen, and his name circulated widely as a suspect, though he was ultimately ruled out.

The case consumed Delphi. The families of the two girls became advocates. The community organized. Local residents printed the image on flyers, on banners, on t-shirts. For six years, it went unsolved.

In October two thousand and twenty-three, investigators arrested a fifty-year-old man named Richard Allen, a CVS pharmacy employee who had lived in Delphi at the time of the murders and who had, in fact, spoken with investigators early in the investigation. New forensic evidence and a more thorough analysis of the physical evidence at the scene pointed to Allen. A gun he owned was linked by ballistics to evidence found near the girls. He was charged with two counts of murder.

The trial was contentious. Defense attorneys raised questions about the handling of evidence and the reliability of some of the forensic findings. The case faced legal complications including a change of venue and significant pretrial proceedings. Allen maintained his innocence.

In October two thousand and twenty-four, a jury found Richard Allen guilty on both counts of murder. He was sentenced to one hundred and thirty years in prison.

For the investigators who had worked the case through six years of near-silence, the resolution did not undo what had been lost. Two girls went hiking on a winter afternoon and did not come home. But what the Delphi case demonstrated is something that law enforcement has leaned on in the years since — that the evidence preserved in a brief, frightened recording on a teenager's phone can outlast any amount of time and eventually deliver an answer.
