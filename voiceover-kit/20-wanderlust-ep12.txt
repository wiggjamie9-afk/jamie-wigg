Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. Today we are going almost as far from anywhere as it is possible to go, to a pair of islands at the bottom of the world where nature is still very much in charge. New Zealand. Two long islands adrift in the vast Southern Ocean, more than a thousand miles from their nearest neighbour, so remote that human beings did not set foot here until around seven hundred years ago, making it one of the last large landmasses on earth to be settled. Close your eyes and picture a fjord on the south-western coast at first light. Sheer black cliffs rise straight out of dark water and vanish into low cloud. Waterfalls, dozens of them, thread down the rock face from the rain of the night before. The only sound is the cry of a single seabird and the slap of water against stone. This is New Zealand. A country with more sheep than people, more coastline than it knows what to do with, and scenery so absurdly beautiful that it almost feels staged.

Let me give you the lay of the land, because the two islands are quite different in temperament. The North Island is the warmer, greener, more populated one, home to most of the country and to the rich living culture of the Maori, the people who first voyaged here across the Pacific. The South Island is the dramatic one, the one of the snow-topped Southern Alps, the glaciers, the fjords, the wide empty valleys. If you have time for only one, and you are coming for the landscapes, choose the south. If you can manage both, fly into one and out of the other, and never double back.

The way to see New Zealand, the only honest way, is to drive it. Rent a campervan, or simply a small car, and treat the road itself as the destination. Distances look small on the map and take longer than you think, because the roads wind and climb and constantly tempt you to stop. And you must stop. Around every bend is another view that would be the highlight of an entire trip anywhere else, and here is merely a Tuesday.

In the south, give your time to a few great places. Queenstown, ringed by jagged mountains on the shore of a deep blue lake, is the adventure capital, the birthplace of the bungee jump, and if you have ever wanted to throw yourself off something tall and attached to a rubber cord, this is the spiritual home of that particular madness. But even if you never leave the ground, the setting alone is worth the journey. Drive on to Milford Sound, deep in Fiordland, ideally on a day after rain when a thousand temporary waterfalls pour down the cliffs. Take the boat out onto the water and feel how small a human being is against that scale. And find Lake Tekapo on a clear night, in the heart of one of the world's largest dark sky reserves, where the Milky Way arcs overhead so bright and so thick it casts a faint shadow, and you understand, perhaps for the first time, what the night sky looked like to every human who lived before electric light.

The culture matters as much as the scenery, and here is where many visitors rush past the best part. Make time, real time, for Maori culture. Visit a marae, a traditional meeting ground, if you are given the chance. Learn what the haka actually is, the ceremonial dance you may know only from the rugby, and understand that it is far older and deeper than sport. Learn that this country has two names, that the Maori name is Aotearoa, the land of the long white cloud, and that the relationship between the two peoples is woven into everything here.

Now, your practical compass. Number one, the seasons are upside down. New Zealand sits below the equator, so their summer is December to February and their winter is June to August. Plan accordingly, and never assume. Number two, the weather changes in minutes, especially in the mountains and the south. The locals have a saying, four seasons in one day, and they are not exaggerating. Always carry a warm layer and a rain jacket, even on a blue morning. Number three, respect the sun. There is a hole in the ozone layer over this part of the world, and the sunlight here is fierce, far stronger than the mild temperatures suggest. You can burn badly on a cool day. Wear sunscreen, always. Number four, the biosecurity is serious and you must take it seriously. This isolated ecosystem is incredibly fragile, so they check your boots, your tent, anything that has touched soil or nature elsewhere, for seeds and dirt. Clean your hiking gear before you fly, declare everything honestly, and understand that it is protecting something genuinely irreplaceable.

And the thing to carry home from the bottom of the world. New Zealand recalibrates your sense of scale. After a week among those mountains and fjords and that immense empty sky, the ordinary worries of your ordinary life look very small indeed, and that, I think, is the quiet gift of the wildest places. They put you back in proportion.

So your homework this week. One night, away from the towns, walk out into the dark and look up. New Zealand has some of the cleanest, blackest skies on the planet. Give your eyes twenty full minutes to adjust, no phone, no torch, and watch the southern stars come out in their thousands. Find the Southern Cross. It is a sky most of the northern world has never seen, and you will not forget it.
