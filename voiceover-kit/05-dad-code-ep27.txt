Being the Dad They Actually Need. Welcome to Episode twenty seven.

This one is the one I wish someone had handed me on day one.

When you become a dad for the first time, you arrive with a model. The model is usually your own father. You are either trying to replicate him or correct him, and often both at once, which is a complicated position to operate from. You want to keep the good stuff and fix the gaps, and the problem is you often do not even know where the gaps are until one of them causes a problem years later.

I spent the first few years of parenting doing a really solid impression of what I thought a good dad looked like. Strong. Steady. Reliable. Provider. Fixer. Slow to need anything, quick to help with everything. That was the template I had inherited and I followed it faithfully.

What I was slower to figure out was that my kids did not need that template. They needed me. The specific, actual me. With my specific kids who had specific temperaments and specific needs that had nothing to do with any blueprint I had inherited.

My son needed a dad who could sit in the mess of a problem with him without immediately jumping to solutions. I am a solutions person. This did not come naturally. But when I learned to just listen — to actually listen rather than queue up my answer — he started bringing me more. Because he felt heard rather than processed.

My daughter needed a dad who was emotionally available and not afraid to talk about feelings. I had been raised to see feelings as something to manage and move past, not something to sit with and explore. Learning to be honest about my own emotional state in front of her — not a performance, just an honest acknowledgement of what was actually happening inside me — changed our relationship. She trusted me more when I was real with her.

Being the dad they need means paying attention to who they actually are and being willing to grow in the direction that serves them. Not the direction that is easiest for you. The direction they need.

That is harder than it sounds. Because we all have habits and defaults and emotional reflexes built up over decades. Parenting asks you to override some of them in real time, in the middle of situations that are already stressful. That is genuinely difficult and I do not want to understate it.

But here is what I know: the dads who are most connected to their kids are not the ones who had the best model to start from. They are the ones who stayed curious about their kids. Who kept asking, what does this particular child need from me right now? Who noticed when their approach was not working and were willing to try something different without treating it as a failure.

Flexibility is not inconsistency. It is responsiveness. It means your kid knows you are actually paying attention to them and not just running a program.

There is also something worth saying about the dad your kids need you to be at different stages. The dad a four-year-old needs is not the dad a fourteen-year-old needs, which is not the dad a twenty-four-year-old needs. The role keeps evolving and you have to evolve with it. The dads who struggle most are often the ones who mastered one version of the job and then tried to keep doing that version long after the job description had changed.

The short version of all of this is simple to say and takes a lifetime to do. Stay curious about your kids. Let them change your mind about things. Be willing to not know. Be willing to ask them what they need rather than always assuming you already know.

You are not just raising children. You are being shaped by them. The good ones let that happen.

And if you take nothing else from twenty-seven episodes of this, take this: you do not have to get it right every time. You just have to keep showing up and caring enough to keep trying. That is what they will remember. Not your perfect moments. Your persistence.

Thank you for being here for this series. Now go be their dad.
