Welcome to AI Briefing. Today: enterprise AI adoption. Not the announcements, not the demos — the actual deployment numbers, what is working, and where organizations keep hitting the same wall.

McKinsey's annual AI survey released this quarter puts AI adoption in large enterprises at sixty-five percent, up from fifty-five percent twelve months ago. That ten-point jump sounds significant, and in one sense it is: it represents a genuine acceleration from the slow-burn experimentation phase that dominated two thousand twenty-two and most of two thousand twenty-three. But the more revealing number is buried further in the report. Of organizations that have deployed AI in at least one business function, only fourteen percent describe their deployments as operating at scale. The rest are running pilots, proofs of concept, or limited rollouts to a subset of users.

The adoption curve for enterprise AI looks less like a ramp and more like a funnel with a very narrow exit. Companies enter at the pilot stage easily and inexpensively. The bottleneck is not capability — frontier models are good enough for a wide range of enterprise tasks right now. The bottleneck is integration: connecting AI tools to internal data, internal systems, and internal workflows in a way that is secure, compliant, and reliable enough for production use.

Gartner's research on the same topic identifies three recurring failure modes for AI deployments that stall at the pilot stage. The first is data readiness. Enterprise AI tools are only as good as the data they can access, and most enterprise data is scattered across dozens of legacy systems, inconsistently formatted, and subject to access controls that were never designed with AI agents in mind. Cleaning and organizing that data for AI access is expensive and slow, and it is underestimated in almost every timeline.

The second failure mode is change management. AI tools do not replace tasks in isolation — they change workflows, which means they change jobs. The organizations that are scaling AI successfully are the ones that invested in training and communication alongside the technical deployment. The ones stalling are treating AI as a software problem and finding out it is also a people problem.

The third is measurement. Organizations that cannot clearly quantify what AI is delivering struggle to justify the ongoing investment, which means pilots that are actually working quietly get defunded because no one connected the output to a number the CFO recognizes. AI teams that survived budget reviews this year almost universally have a clear productivity metric attached to their work.

On the vendor side, the enterprise AI race is producing some notable consolidation. Salesforce has announced deeper native AI integration across its CRM suite, following Microsoft's continued rollout of Copilot features across Microsoft three sixty-five. The pattern is the same in both cases: embedding AI assistance directly into the software employees already use, rather than requiring them to switch to a separate tool. Research consistently shows that adoption rates are dramatically higher for embedded AI features than for standalone AI applications, even when the standalone applications are more powerful.

IBM is doubling down on its watsonx platform for regulated industries — banking, insurance, healthcare — where the procurement cycle is longer but the contract sizes are larger and the customer relationships are stickier. IBM's bet is that governance, explainability, and on-premises deployment options matter more in those sectors than raw model capability, and the early evidence from deals signed this quarter suggests they are right.

For anyone inside a large organization navigating this: the ceiling is not the AI itself. The ceiling is the organization's data infrastructure, its change management capacity, and its ability to measure what it is getting. Those are solvable problems. They just take longer than a proof of concept does, and the companies that plan for that gap are the ones making it through the funnel.

That is the briefing. Enterprise AI is real, the adoption curve is steep, and the gap between pilots and scale is where the real work happens.
