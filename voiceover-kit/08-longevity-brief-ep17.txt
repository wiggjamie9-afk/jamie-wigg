Welcome to Longevity Brief. Today we are covering protein — specifically why your requirements for it increase as you age, and what the latest research says about optimal intake for preserving muscle, protecting metabolic health, and extending healthy years.

For most of the twentieth century, dietary guidelines treated protein as a single-quantity nutrient. Eat enough to avoid deficiency, and you were covered. The recommended daily allowance — point eight grams per kilogram of body weight — was set based on studies measuring nitrogen balance in young adults. It was never designed to represent optimal intake for healthy aging. And yet it has guided clinical nutrition recommendations for decades.

The research picture now looks very different.

Starting in our forties, our bodies become less efficient at extracting the anabolic signal from dietary protein. This phenomenon is called anabolic resistance. When a twenty-five-year-old eats a meal containing twenty grams of protein, their muscles respond robustly — protein synthesis increases, repair happens, and lean mass is maintained. When a sixty-five-year-old eats the same meal, the anabolic response is blunted. Less protein is incorporated into muscle tissue. More is oxidised or lost.

The practical implication is that older adults need more protein per meal, and more protein overall, to achieve the same muscle-protective effect.

A consensus statement from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism, involving researchers from over twenty countries, recommends that healthy adults over sixty-five aim for one point two to one point six grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. For people who are physically active, who are ill, or who are recovering from injury, the target may be even higher — up to two grams per kilogram.

For a seventy kilogram adult, that means between eighty-four and one hundred twelve grams of protein per day at minimum.

Distribution also matters. Rather than consuming most of your protein in one or two large meals, spreading intake across three to four meals appears to optimise muscle protein synthesis across the day. Each meal should ideally contain somewhere between twenty-five and forty grams of high-quality protein — enough to adequately stimulate the anabolic response even in the context of age-related resistance.

Protein quality is not equal. Animal proteins — meat, fish, eggs, dairy — generally have higher leucine content and better amino acid profiles for stimulating muscle protein synthesis. Leucine is the key anabolic trigger. It activates the mTOR signalling pathway, which tells muscle cells to build. Plant proteins can absolutely contribute to total intake, but they typically need to be consumed in larger quantities to deliver equivalent anabolic stimulus, unless they are combined strategically.

For those who follow plant-based diets, soy protein and pea protein are among the better options. Combining rice and pea protein achieves an amino acid profile comparable to whey.

There is also an important connection between protein intake and bone health. Adequate protein supports collagen synthesis and increases calcium absorption. Contrary to older concerns about protein acidifying the blood and leaching calcium from bones, the current evidence shows that higher protein intake is associated with greater bone mineral density and lower fracture risk — especially when calcium and vitamin D are also adequate.

One final point. Protein is the most satiating macronutrient. Higher protein intake tends to reduce overall calorie consumption, preserve lean mass during weight loss, and maintain metabolic rate. For older adults managing weight or at risk of malnutrition, protein is a lever worth pulling deliberately.

The summary: the protein requirements in your sixties and beyond are meaningfully higher than in your twenties. Most people are not meeting them. And the consequences — in lost muscle, reduced strength, and accelerated biological aging — are real and cumulative.
