Welcome to Stoic Minute. I am Harry.

The Stoics held an unusual relationship with death. Not an obsession, and not an avoidance, but a kind of steady companionship. They called the practice memento mori, remember that you will die. Marcus Aurelius came back to it throughout his private journals, not out of despair, but because the awareness of an ending gave the present moment its full weight.

He wrote: Think of yourself as dead. You have lived your life. Now take what is left and live it properly.

There is something almost startling in that instruction. Take what is left. As if the life already lived belongs, in a way, to the past, and only what remains is truly in your hands. The Stoic point is not that life is short, though it is. The point is that most people live as though the end is so far away it is effectively unreal. And because it feels unreal, the urgency drains out of the present. Tomorrow there will be time for the conversation that matters. Next year there will be time for the work you actually care about. Someday you will stop drifting and begin.

The awareness of death does not make the Stoic morbid. It makes them present. When you genuinely hold the fact that this day will not return, the choices inside it sharpen. Not in a panicked way, not in a way that makes you anxious about wasting a single minute, but in a quiet, clarifying way. The afternoon opens differently when you have remembered, just once, that afternoons are not infinite.

Epictetus, who had been enslaved and knew what it was to have time taken from you by force, put it simply: it is not death that a man should fear, but he should fear never beginning to live.

Here is today's practice. Before this day ends, sit for two minutes with a single question: If this were my last ordinary day, how would I have spent it differently? You do not need to answer dramatically. Most of what shifts will be small: a conversation given your full attention, a resentment you chose not to carry, a moment of quiet appreciation for something you usually walk past. Death is not asking you to be extraordinary. It is asking you to be present.

Remembering that you will die is, in the Stoic view, one of the most life-affirming things you can do.
