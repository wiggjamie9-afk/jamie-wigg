# PODCAST #05: DAD CODE — Episode 6
**Voice to use:** Brian or Bill (Warm, casual male)
**Episode 1 voice used:** Michael

---

Hey everyone, Michael Scott here, and welcome back to Dad Code. Today we're talking about one of the hardest things I think a modern dad has to learn how to do, and it goes against every single instinct in your body. Today we're talking about resilience. About raising a kid who can fall down and get back up. Which means, and here's the hard part, learning how to not catch them.

Let me describe the moment, because you know this moment. Your kid is wobbling along the top of a low wall. Not high. Not really dangerous. But your whole nervous system lights up like a fire alarm and your hands shoot out and you hover, ready to grab. And ninety percent of the time, they're fine. They find their balance. They jump down grinning. And you exhale. Now multiply that hovering hand across a whole childhood, across every challenge, every disappointment, every small struggle. That's the trap I'm talking about. The hovering hand.

Here's the thing I had to really swallow. My job is not to remove every obstacle from my kid's path. My job is to raise a person who can handle obstacles when I'm not there. And those are almost opposite jobs. Every time I rush in and fix it, smooth it, carry it, solve it, I'm robbing my kid of a tiny rep at the gym of getting back up. I think I'm being a good dad. I'm being a loving one. But I'm quietly teaching them that struggle is an emergency, and that they can't handle anything without me swooping in.

There's a phrase that flipped this for me. I am not raising a child. I am raising a future adult. Say that one again. I am not raising a child, I am raising a future adult. Because the goal was never a kid who's comfortable today. The goal is an adult who's capable in twenty years. And capability is not built in comfort. It is built in the small, survivable struggles we let them have now.

So what does that actually look like? It's not throwing them in the deep end and walking off. That's not resilience, that's neglect, and there's a world of difference. The skill is in the dose. Let them struggle just past the point where it's easy, but stay close. Be the safe harbor they can come back to, not the boat that won't let them sail. Let them tie their own shoe even though you're late and it's agony to watch. Let them have the hard conversation with the friend. Let them feel the disappointment of the thing that didn't go their way, and sit with them in it rather than instantly buying a replacement to make the feeling go away.

And that's the part most of us get wrong, me included. We treat our kid's bad feelings as a problem to solve immediately. He's sad, quick, fix it, distract him, buy him something, make it stop. But a kid who is never allowed to feel a hard feeling never learns that hard feelings pass. And that, that right there, is the actual root of resilience. Not toughness. Not gritted teeth. It's the deep, bone-level knowledge that I can feel something awful, and survive it, and come out the other side. You can only learn that by being allowed to go through it, with someone calm beside you.

So here's the practical bit. When your kid hits a wall, before you jump in, try one question. What do you think you could try? That's it. You're not abandoning them. You're handing the problem back, gently, with a vote of confidence attached. You're saying, I think you've got this, and I'm right here. Most of the time they surprise you. And the look on their face when they solve it themselves, when they realize they did it, that look is worth a hundred times more than the thing you would've fixed for them.

Here's what I want to leave you with. Love is not the same as rescue. Sometimes the most loving thing you can do is sit on your hands, keep your mouth shut, and let your kid find out how strong they actually are. That's so much harder than catching them. But the falling is the lesson. The getting up is the muscle. And one day, when you're not in the room, that muscle is all they're going to have. Build it now, while it's only a low wall.

Next week we're going somewhere I've been putting off. Online safety. The internet, and how on earth we keep these kids safe in a world none of our own dads ever had to think about.

This has been Dad Code. Now go and let your kid try the hard thing. And keep your hands in your pockets.
