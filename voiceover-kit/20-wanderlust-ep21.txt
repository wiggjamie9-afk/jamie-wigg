Welcome to Wanderlust. I'm William. This is our final episode in this run, and I want to do something a little different. Instead of a destination, I want to talk about travel itself. About what it does to you over time. About what twenty years of moving through the world has actually taught me, and what I wish someone had told me at the beginning.

The first thing is the most fundamental. Travel is not the reward for working hard. It is not a holiday from real life. It is real life. The experiences you have on the road, the people you meet, the moments of genuine connection and genuine discomfort and genuine wonder, these are not a break from your actual existence. They are among the most formative parts of it. Treat travel with that seriousness and it gives back accordingly.

I remember the first time I really understood this. I was twenty-three, sitting on a slow train through rural India, somewhere between Varanasi and Kolkata, watching the flat agricultural plain of West Bengal move past the window in the orange light of late afternoon. I had been sick for three days. I was behind on sleep. I had very little money left. And I was experiencing something I can only describe as a profound, wordless happiness. Not despite the difficulty. Because of the totality of it. Because I was completely present in a life that had become briefly, entirely unfamiliar. That feeling, I spent the next two decades chasing it, and also learning that you can create it without a plane ticket, but that a plane ticket is a very efficient way to get there.

The second thing I'd tell my younger self. Go somewhere that makes you uncomfortable. Not dangerous. Just uncomfortable. A country where you don't speak the language and the script is illegible and the food is unlike anything you've eaten and the social rules are different enough that you feel constantly slightly off-balance. This is where the real education happens. Not in the beautiful, easy, familiar places. In the places that require you to be humble and patient and willing to look foolish while you figure it out.

Japan was that country for me at twenty-five. The characters were completely illegible. The etiquette was intricate and important and I didn't know any of it. I accidentally sat in the reserved carriage on a bullet train, made every possible mistake with chopstick etiquette, and stood helpless in front of ticket machines I couldn't read. I was also experiencing one of the most extraordinary, precise, detail-oriented cultures on earth, and the discomfort was inseparable from the wonder.

The third thing. The people are more important than the places. Every destination I remember most vividly, I remember because of a person. The tuk-tuk driver in Phnom Penh who took me to his village and introduced me to his family and taught me three words of Khmer that I still remember. The woman in a small town in southern Chile who ran a guesthouse alone and cooked dinner for her six guests and told stories about the Pinochet years in a quiet, precise voice that made the room completely still. The old man in a mountain village in Crete who pressed a glass of his home-distilled raki into my hand at nine in the morning and asked, through a young cousin translating on a phone, what I thought of Greece. These people are the whole point. The cathedral, the museum, the famous view — these are the backdrop. The people are the story.

The fourth thing. Document less and experience more. I am not against photographs. I take them. But I have noticed over many years that the camera is also a way of managing the intensity of an experience, of putting glass between yourself and the thing you are looking at. Some moments should be looked at directly, fully, without the intervention of a lens. The screen of your phone is a very effective way of not being somewhere. Use it thoughtfully.

The fifth thing. Generosity is the best currency. When in doubt, tip more than you think is necessary. When someone invites you into their home, say yes. When offered food you are uncertain about, eat it anyway. When a local wants to practise their English with you, give them your full attention. These small acts of generosity do more for the experience of travel, and for the honest exchange between cultures, than any amount of money spent on entrance fees and tourist experiences.

And the last thing. The hardest to hear, perhaps. Travel is a privilege. Not everyone can do it. Not everyone is welcome the same way in the same places. The freedom of movement I have described across this podcast is unevenly distributed in the world, and if you hold a passport that lets you move freely and arrive warmly welcomed almost everywhere, that is a form of luck worth naming. The best travelers I have met are also the most grateful ones. Grateful not in a performative way, but in the deep, structural sense of understanding that none of this is earned or owed. It is offered.

Go. Go while you can. Go further than feels safe. Stay longer than seems sensible. Eat the thing you can't pronounce. Walk the street you weren't planning to. Say yes before your rational mind says no.

The world is very large and also somehow very small, and it is full of people who are more like you than different from you, waiting in cities and villages and islands and mountain towns to be the best part of your story.

Thank you for listening to Wanderlust. Until next time, keep moving.
