This is History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, the story of a map that rewrote the world — drawn by a man who had never left his house.

In the twelfth century, in the city of Palermo, on the island of Sicily, there lived a geographer named Muhammad al-Idrisi. He was born in Morocco, educated in the great Islamic intellectual tradition that had preserved and expanded the knowledge of the ancient world, and he had travelled widely in his youth, through North Africa and into Europe as far as England. But by the time he did his greatest work, he was at the court of a Norman king, a Christian ruler of a Muslim-majority island at the centre of the Mediterranean Sea, and he had been there long enough to know it as home.

The king was Roger the Second of Sicily. He was a remarkable man by any measure. He ruled a kingdom in which Arabic, Greek, Latin, and Norman French were all spoken at court. His officials included Muslims and Christians and Jews. His royal robe, which still exists and can be seen in Vienna today, has an inscription in Arabic praising Allah. He was, in the strict theological terms of his age, a Christian king. But he was also something rarer: a man genuinely curious about the world.

Roger had heard that the Islamic geographical tradition contained detailed knowledge of the shape of the earth that the European world had lost or never possessed. He invited al-Idrisi to his court, and for fifteen years, al-Idrisi sat in Palermo and worked. He collected accounts from travellers and merchants. He interrogated sailors about coastlines and distances. He cross-referenced the Greek geographers with the Islamic ones and with his own direct knowledge, and he synthesised everything into the most accurate and comprehensive map of the known world that had yet been produced anywhere on earth.

In eleven fifty-four, the year of Roger's death, al-Idrisi completed the work. He produced a book of seventy detailed regional maps, and a single great circular world map engraved on a disc of pure silver, roughly one and a half metres across and weighing more than a hundred kilograms. He titled the book something that translates roughly as A Diversion for the Man Longing to Travel to Far-Off Places. It is known today simply as the Tabula Rogeriana — the Book of Roger.

There is one thing about the map that always surprises people when they first encounter it. It is upside down. South is at the top. North is at the bottom. This was not an error. It was a convention of the Islamic geographical tradition, and al-Idrisi followed it naturally. The continents are in the right relationship to one another. The Mediterranean, the Black Sea, the coastlines of Africa, the outline of Europe — all recognisable, all more accurate than almost any European map of the same period. But turned one hundred and eighty degrees from the orientation we consider normal today.

What we call normal is, of course, simply what became standard later. There is no natural reason why north should be at the top of a map. It is a convention, not a truth. Al-Idrisi drew the world as his tradition taught him to see it, and his world was as real and as carefully observed as anyone else's.

The Tabula Rogeriana described the world from Ireland in the northwest to Korea in the east, from Scandinavia in the north to sub-Saharan Africa in the south. It included trade routes, mountain ranges, rivers, cities, and distances. It remained the most sophisticated map of the world in existence for three hundred years. And it was made in a palace where a Christian king and a Muslim scholar worked together in a city where the call to prayer could be heard from the same streets as the church bells.

The silver disc itself was destroyed or lost centuries ago. We know it only from descriptions. The book survived in manuscripts copied by later scholars, and from those copies the maps were eventually reconstructed. Al-Idrisi died around the year eleven sixty-five. Roger the Second had died eleven years before him, and al-Idrisi did not stay long in Sicily after his patron was gone. He returned to Morocco and died there, far from the island where his greatest work was made.

The collaboration between them produced something neither could have produced alone. A Christian king's ambition and resources. A Muslim scholar's learning and method. And in between, something that belonged to neither tradition exclusively, but to both, and to the world.
