This is History Uncovered. I'm John. Tonight, the night the music almost died — and the one decision that saved it.

In the year seventeen forty-one, a man named George Frideric Handel sat alone in a rented house in London and wrote a letter saying he was finished. He was fifty-six years old. He was in debt. The opera company he had built and rebuilt over decades had collapsed a second time, emptied by fashion and competition and the simple cruelty of public taste. His health had failed him twice — a stroke in seventeen thirty-seven had briefly paralysed his right side, and he had recovered through sheer stubbornness, only to find himself back on the edge of bankruptcy. The London audiences who had once adored him had moved on. The critics had buried him. And he was tired.

And then a letter arrived from Ireland.

It was from a charitable committee in Dublin, inviting him to come and give a series of concerts to raise money for prisoners' debt relief, a hospital, and a charity for musicians' families. Dublin, unlike London, was a city that still loved Handel. The invitation was sincere, and the terms were generous. And a friend — a librettist named Charles Jennens — had recently sent Handel a text he had assembled from the Bible, a meditation on the life, death, and resurrection of Christ. Jennens had been pestering him about it for over a year. Handel had set it aside.

Sometime in August of seventeen forty-one, in the house in London where he had written his goodbye letter, Handel opened Jennens's libretto and began to compose.

What followed is one of the most extraordinary creative episodes in the history of music. In twenty-four days, Handel composed the entirety of what we now call the Messiah. Over two hundred and fifty pages of music, encompassing three acts and fifty-three movements, for soloists, chorus, and orchestra. He barely stopped. Those who brought him food reported that he sometimes did not eat, sometimes did not sleep, sometimes was found at his desk weeping, or staring at nothing. He worked with a speed and an intensity that suggested a man not so much composing as transcribing something he could already hear entire, somewhere beyond himself.

On the twenty-fourth day he was done. He reportedly said, when he finished the great choral climax — the Hallelujah chorus — that he had seen all of heaven open before him, and the great God himself. Whether he said it or not, the story feels true to what the music sounds like.

He took the manuscript to Dublin. The premiere was held on the thirteenth of April, seventeen forty-two, in a concert hall on Fishamble Street. The audience had been asked in advance to leave their swords at home — the gentlemen wore swords in those days — and the ladies had been asked not to wear their widest hoop skirts. All of this was to allow as many people as possible to squeeze in. They sold over seven hundred tickets for a hall built for six hundred. The concert raised four hundred pounds for the three charities, enough to release one hundred and forty-two people from debtors' prison.

The reviews said it was the finest piece of music ever heard in Ireland.

Handel lived another eighteen years after that night in Dublin. He became wealthy. He became beloved again in London, performing the Messiah there to growing acclaim. He lost his sight in his final years but kept performing from memory, at the organ, until almost the end. When he died in seventeen fifty-nine, they buried him in Westminster Abbey with a statue showing him holding the score of the Messiah open to the soprano solo: I know that my redeemer liveth.

The piece has been performed continuously somewhere in the world every year since seventeen forty-two. It has never stopped. In that sense, what Handel wrote in those twenty-four days has never actually ended.

The audience still rises for the Hallelujah chorus, as they reportedly did on the night of the premiere. No one is entirely sure why the tradition started. Some say King George the Second stood, and everyone else followed. Some say it was simply that the music demanded it, and the body answered before the mind had time to decide.

Perhaps that is explanation enough.
