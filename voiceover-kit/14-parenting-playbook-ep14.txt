Welcome back to Parenting Playbook. Let me start with a small, everyday scene. You ask your child to put their plate in the sink, or pop their dirty washing in the basket, and you'd think you'd asked them to climb a mountain. There's the sigh. The why do I have to. The but it's not fair. And it would honestly be quicker, in that moment, to just do it yourself. So often, you do. Today, we're talking about chores and responsibility, and how to raise a child who genuinely contributes to family life, rather than just living in it like a guest.

Let me begin with something I really believe matters. Giving your child chores is not about getting free labour, and it's not about being strict. It's one of the kindest, most useful gifts you can give them. A child who learns to contribute grows up feeling capable, needed, and part of something. The research here is quite striking. Children who help around the home tend to grow into more responsible, more confident, more resilient adults. The work itself is small. What it builds inside them is not.

So let's understand what's really going on. Young children, believe it or not, often want to help. Watch a toddler who is desperate to push the hoover or wipe the table. The instinct to join in and be useful is there from the very start. What sometimes happens is that we accidentally train it out of them, because their help is slow and messy, so we shoo them away and do it ourselves. By the time we actually want their help, years later, we've taught them that chores are our job, not theirs. The trick is to welcome the helping early, even when it's not very helpful at all.

Here are some principles that hold up.

One. Start small and start young. A two or three year old can put toys in a box or carry their cup to the kitchen. A four or five year old can lay out cutlery or feed a pet with a little help. Match the job to the age, and let it grow as they do. The point in the early years is the habit, not the result.

Two. Make it a normal part of life, not a punishment. Chores should never be the thing you threaten them with. They're simply what everyone in a family does, because we all live here and we all pitch in. Frame it as belonging, not as a chore in the gloomy sense. In this family, we all help.

Three. Resist redoing it in front of them. If your child makes their bed and it's lumpy, and you smooth it out while they watch, you've just told them their effort wasn't good enough. Let it be imperfect. The wobbly job they did themselves teaches far more than the perfect one you did for them.

Four. Be careful with rewards. A little pocket money for certain jobs can be fine, but try not to pay for everything. If every contribution comes with a reward, helping becomes a transaction. Some jobs are simply part of being in the family, done for no reason other than that we all share the load.

Five. Use routines and visible reminders rather than nagging. Like so much in parenting, predictability helps. A simple chart, or chores tied to a fixed moment in the day, means the routine does the reminding, not your repeated voice. After dinner, plates go in the sink. It's just what happens.

Let me give you some scenarios.

Scenario one. The young child whose help is more hindrance than help. Let them help anyway. Yes, it takes longer and it's messier. You are not getting a job done efficiently. You are growing a helper for the years ahead. Praise the effort warmly and ignore the imperfection.

Scenario two. The older child who resists and argues every single time. Reduce the negotiation by making the expectation clear and consistent, not up for daily debate. Tie the chore to a routine, keep your own tone calm and matter of fact, and let the natural rhythm carry it. You're not begging. You're stating how the household works.

Scenario three. The child who does a job badly, perhaps on purpose, hoping you'll give up and do it yourself. Stay kind but hold the line. Have them try again, with a little guidance, rather than rescuing them. If you take over, you teach them that doing it poorly is the way to get out of it.

Now, the bigger picture. Resistance to chores is completely normal, and it will come and go. There will be cooperative spells and stubborn ones, and a difficult patch doesn't mean you've raised someone idle or entitled. You're playing a long game here, and the lessons land over years, not days.

And there's room for many reasonable approaches. Families differ enormously on how they handle chores, pocket money, and responsibilities, shaped by their values and circumstances. There's no single correct system. What matters is that your child grows up understanding that they're a contributing member of the home, not a guest being waited on.

One gentle note. If your child seems unable to manage tasks you'd expect for their age, struggles far more than their peers with everyday responsibilities, or finds simple jobs genuinely overwhelming rather than just unappealing, that can be worth a quiet conversation with their teacher, or your doctor or health visitor. Sometimes what looks like reluctance is actually difficulty that deserves a closer look.

So here's where I'd begin this week. Pick one age-appropriate job and make it your child's. Tie it to a fixed moment in the day, resist redoing it behind them, and praise the effort rather than the polish. Keep it consistent, and let contributing quietly become part of who they are.

You're not trying to get the chores done for free. You're raising a person who knows how to look after themselves, and how to be part of something bigger than themselves. That's worth a few lumpy beds along the way.
